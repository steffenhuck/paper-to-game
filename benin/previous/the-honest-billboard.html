The Honest Billboard

Benin City, Nigeria. In 2017 almost 1 in 4 households saw someone leave for Europe by the road across the desert and the sea. Europe pays for posters here, telling people not to go.

About four minutes. Two campaigns of three billboards. Every number comes from a 2018 survey of 555 households in Benin City.

    How to play

    The setting. Benin City in Nigeria is where more people leave for Europe by the dangerous road across the Sahara and the Mediterranean than almost anywhere else. European governments pay for poster campaigns there, mostly saying that Europe is not as good as people think.

    What you do. You choose 3 billboards out of 6. Every one states a true fact. Then each one turns over to show how people in Benin City answered the same question in a real 2018 survey.

    The yardstick. Of 100 people, a billboard warns those who believed something rosier than the truth, is old news to those who knew it, and backfires on those who believed something gloomier, because for them the truth makes Europe or the trip look better. A billboard's score is warned minus backfired. A campaign's score adds up its three billboards, and you see the best score possible next to yours.

    Two campaigns. After the first campaign you get a second. The billboards you have seen keep their scores, and the rest you must judge from what you learned. If your first campaign is already the best possible, the game ends there.

    What the game is trying to show. Whether the people these campaigns target are really as misinformed as the campaigns assume, and about what.

    A billboard's score is about beliefs: whether the fact is better or worse than what someone thought. The survey cannot say whether anyone would then stay or go, and neither does the game.

    About the paper

    Bernd Beber and Alexandra Scacco (2022). "The Myth of the Misinformed Migrant? Survey Insights from Nigeria's Irregular Migration Epicenter." Ruhr Economic Papers No. 957, RWI Essen. doi:10.4419/96973121.

    Fidelity: statistical, from reported distributions only

    Nothing in this game is simulated or random. Every share comes from a histogram or stacked bar in the paper, read off the figure, or from a number in its text. The 100 dots show shares; they are not individual people.

    It demonstrates where Benin City's survey answers sat relative to the true values, item by item. It assumes three things of ours: where "about right" ends (within a factor of 1.5 for money, 7.5 points for the asylum rate; the conclusions hold from 1.25 to 2 and from 5 to 10 points); that guesses are spread evenly within each histogram bar; and that a fact worse than your guess makes the option look worse to you. That is a statement about beliefs, not behaviour. The paper establishes the distribution of beliefs in a representative 2018 sample of one city. It does not test any campaign, and nothing here says anyone would stay or go.

    What the paper hands over

    A survey of 555 households in Benin City (October–November 2018), with incentivised best guesses about Europe (Fig. 8), the journey (Figs. 11, 14, 15), asylum (Fig. 17) and the respondent's own chances (Fig. 16), plus interviews with ten returnees. The true values are the paper's: GNI per capita in the EU in 2017; the maximum Italian (NASpI) and British (Jobseeker's Allowance) benefits; 28,130 of 122,040 Nigerian asylum claims accepted in 2011–2017.

    The discovery

    Campaigns assume would-be migrants overrate Europe. In the survey they did not: their guesses about Europe were about right or too gloomy (the typical guess of European income was €660 a month, against about €2,500). What they got wrong was the journey and their own odds: four in five thought they personally would make it to Europe, and most overestimated the asylum rate. So an honest message about Europe backfires; an honest message about the road lands.

    The "making it" billboard

    The fact ("most who set out never get past Libya") is the paper's footnote 34; the paper does not know the exact share. The comparison is between a person's view of their own chances and a fact about everyone who tries. Any one person might be right to be confident; on average, the paper says, respondents vastly overestimate their odds.

    What is cut

    Life expectancy (not a campaign message); the risk histograms for injury, death, abuse and slavery (no true value exists to compare with); the splits by interest in migrating, by family migration and by gender (the paper reports the key patterns hold in each); community norms; self-reported uncertainty.

    Divergences

    Benefits are shown monthly, as the paper plots them (it asked weekly). The paper's € axis comes from naira; we place its ticks at about ₦415 per euro, which reproduces every label and the €660 median. "Don't know" answers count as "knew it / no change", never as warned.
