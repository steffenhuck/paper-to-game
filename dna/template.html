Watson & CrickCambridge, 1953

Watson's Cardboard

The puzzle

Cambridge, early 1953. James Watson and Francis Crick are racing to find the shape of DNA, the molecule that carries genes. X-ray pictures say it is a helix, the same width all the way along. Inside it sit four kinds of base, A, G, T and C, in any order.

A jigsaw, then, with four kinds of piece and no picture on the box. How do the pieces fit across the middle without the width ever changing? And how could such a thing copy itself?

The cardboard

Watson cut the four bases out of stiff card, true to scale, and pushed them around his desk. Like with like bulged. Then, on the morning of Saturday 28 February, he saw it: A with T has exactly the shape of G with C. Two pairs, one width. Five weeks later he and Crick sent their letter to Nature.

Now it's your desk

This game lets you be Watson, with nothing but some cardboard. The pieces are his cut-outs. One chain is given; find the other, and watch whether the rails stay straight. Then pull your ladder apart and see what he and Crick saw next: it can copy itself.

Story: Watson, The Double Helix (1968), ch. 26. The letter itself does not mention cardboard.

How to play

Build the other chain so every rung holds when you zip it.

The ladder. Two blue rails (the backbones of DNA) with rungs between them. Each rung is two pieces, one pinned to each rail, meeting in the middle.

The pieces. Four kinds: A and G are long, T and C are short. Each has an end with two contact points, top and bottom; each point is a bump or a dent.

A rung holds when the two pieces exactly span the gap between the rails and every bump meets a dent. Too long and the rails bow out. Too short and they pinch in. A bump against a bump pushes the piece away.

The ladder

One rail already has its pieces. Tap a piece in the tray to pin it in the next empty spot (marked in red); tap a pinned piece to take it back. Nothing is tested until you press Zip it. Then every rung is tested, top to bottom. There is no clock: take as long as you like.

Your score

Ten points for every rung that holds, out of 100. Next to it: what someone pinning at random gets (about 25), someone who reads only the lengths or only the ends (about 50), and someone who reads both (100). Then what your ladder shows, your letters counted, and your ladder copied and twisted into the real shape. Play again for a new chain.
