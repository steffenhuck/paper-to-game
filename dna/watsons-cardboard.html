Watson & CrickCambridge, 1953

Watson's Cardboard

The puzzle

Cambridge, early 1953. James Watson and Francis Crick are racing to find the shape of DNA, the molecule that carries genes. X-ray pictures say it is a helix, the same width all the way along. Inside it sit four kinds of base, A, G, T and C, in any order.

So how do the bases fit across the middle without the width ever changing? And how could such a molecule copy itself?

The cardboard

Watson cut the four bases out of stiff card, true to scale, and tried them against each other on his desk. Like with like bulged. Then, on the morning of Saturday 28 February, he saw it: A with T has exactly the shape of G with C. Two pairs, one width. Five weeks later he and Crick sent their letter to Nature.

What you do here

The pieces in this game are his cut-outs. One chain is given; you build the other, and watch whether the rails stay straight. That is his morning. Then the letter's last line: one chain fixes the other, so a ladder pulled apart can copy itself.

Story: Watson, The Double Helix (1968), ch. 26. The letter itself does not mention cardboard.

How to play

Build the other chain so every rung holds when you zip it.

The ladder. Two blue rails (the backbones of DNA) with rungs between them. Each rung is two pieces, one pinned to each rail, meeting in the middle.

The pieces. Four kinds: A and G are long, T and C are short. Each has an end with two contact points, top and bottom; each point is a bump or a dent.

A rung holds when the two pieces exactly span the gap between the rails and every bump meets a dent. Too long and the rails bow out. Too short and they pinch in. A bump against a bump pushes the piece away.

Ladders

One rail already has its pieces. Tap a piece in the tray to pin it in the next empty spot (marked in red); tap a pinned piece to take it back. Nothing is tested until you press Zip it. Then every rung is tested, top to bottom. Two ladders.

The copy

Your second ladder is pulled apart into two halves. A red fork runs down them; on each rung it lights one spot on each half. Tap the piece that belongs there before the fork moves on. It moves on as soon as both spots are filled, and gets faster.

The end

The two copies twist up into the real shape, and you see what you found. One game, no do-overs; a new game number gives new chains.

What's real here

The game in one line

You build the second chain of a DNA ladder from cut-out pieces, zip it to see which rungs hold, then pull it apart and copy it. The rule that decides which rungs hold is Watson and Crick's; the pieces, their lengths, the ladders and the fork are this game's.

The cardboard

The pieces are cardboard because that is how the pairs were found: in February 1953 Watson cut the four bases out of stiff card, to scale, and pushed them around his desk until an adenine–thymine pair and a guanine–cytosine pair turned out to be the same shape (Watson, The Double Helix, 1968, ch. 26). The cardboard is history, not the letter, which says only that the structure rests on "published experimental data and stereochemical arguments". The aim, to build the other chain, is the letter's own consequence: "if the sequence of bases on one chain is given, then the sequence on the other chain is automatically determined."

What the paper found

In a one-page letter to Nature in April 1953, James Watson and Francis Crick proposed the structure of DNA. Two chains wind round the same axis, right-handed, running in opposite directions. The sugar-phosphate backbones are on the outside (Linus Pauling's model of a month earlier had three chains with the phosphates in the middle). The bases are inside, flat, and joined across in pairs by hydrogen bonds. One base of each pair must be a purine (adenine or guanine, two rings) and the other a pyrimidine (thymine or cytosine, one ring). Assuming the bases take their most plausible (keto) forms, only adenine with thymine and guanine with cytosine can bond. So the order of bases along one chain is unrestricted, but it fixes the order on the other. That explains why, as Erwin Chargaff had measured, DNA holds as much adenine as thymine and as much guanine as cytosine. And, in the letter's most famous sentence, "It has not escaped our notice that the specific pairing we have postulated immediately suggests a possible copying mechanism for the genetic material." The helix has a base pair every 3.4 Å along the axis, each turned 36° from the last, so it repeats every 10 pairs (34 Å); the phosphates sit 10 Å from the axis. The authors say the model is "roughly compatible" with the X-ray data then published and "must be regarded as unproved" until checked.

Mechanism: a toy

This game demonstrates that the letter's two conditions, one purine with one pyrimidine (so every rung has the same width) and hydrogen bonds that line up (in the keto forms), leave exactly two pairs, and that this forces one chain to spell out the other, so a ladder pulled apart can be rebuilt twice over. Each condition alone would leave twice as many pairs; together they leave A–T and G–C.

It assumes pieces instead of molecules. Length stands for ring size: a long piece (A, G) and a short piece (T, C) exactly span the rails; two long pieces are too wide, two short ones too narrow. The lengths are our units; any lengths with purines longer than pyrimidines give the same answer. The bumps and dents stand for hydrogen donors and acceptors at the letter's positions 6 and 1. Which base has which is standard chemistry for the keto forms, not printed in the letter. The game uses the letter's two contacts per pair; guanine and cytosine were later found to share a third. The ladders, their sequences (drawn from the game number), the fork and its speed are ours.

The paper establishes a proposed structure and the pairing argument behind it, from published data and stereochemistry; it says the structure is unproved. It does not describe how copying happens: the letter only says the pairing "suggests" a mechanism. The copy in this game (each half rebuilding its partner) is the version Watson and Crick set out a month later, in Nature 171:964, and that later experiments bore out. Neither the zip nor the copy in this game is evidence for the structure: the game shows what follows if it is right.

What is cut

Pauling's and Fraser's rival models; the argument that the material is a salt and that the phosphates outside give cations easy access; the water content; the reason ribose would not fit; the helix's geometry, except at the end, where the copies twist a tenth of a turn per rung, the letter's 36°, one full turn per ten rungs.
