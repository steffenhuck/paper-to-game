A week of evenings · Granovetter, 1973

Granovetter's Dashed Line

You are looking for a job in a town of seven circles. Each week you give seven evenings to the people you know. The town reacts. Then the week's news travels along the lines, and you watch which of it reaches you, and from whom.

Spend your evenings so that you hear of as many job openings as you can.

Built from Mark S. Granovetter, "The Strength of Weak Ties", American Journal of Sociology 78(6), 1973, pp. 1360–1380.

What Granovetter found. He asked people in a Boston suburb who had found their job through a contact how often they saw that contact. One in six said often. More than half said now and then. More than a quarter said rarely, once a year or less: an old college friend, a former workmate, someone they had nearly forgotten. The people closest to you move in your circle and hear what you hear. The person you hardly see spends their evenings somewhere else, and is the only line between that somewhere and you.

What would have followed. If the news that mattered came from close friends, a town of close circles would be a town of well-informed people. It is a town of islands.

The West End of Boston, around 1960: a neighbourhood of close family circles, where almost nobody worked or belonged to anything inside it, could not form one organisation against the plan that demolished it. Charlestown, with its clubs and local jobs, fought the same city and won. In this game's terms: a town with no dashed lines, where a leaflet fills one circle and stops.

How to play

Spend your evenings so that you hear of as many job openings as you can.

The town

Seven circles of people, drawn as rings of dots. Inside a circle everyone sees everyone often. A few grey dashed lines join one circle to another. You are the big dot in the middle, in Home, with Ann, Ben and Cal. Your lines are black: the people with names are the people you know. At the start you see your three friends often (thick lines) and hardly see the eight others (dashed lines): someone from the firm you used to work at, from college, from your street, the choir, the club, your cousins.

A week

You have seven evenings. Tap a name, on the map or in the row of names, to give that person one evening; tap again for two; tap again for none. One evening makes the line thin (you see them now and then). Two make it thick (often). None leaves it dashed (rarely). The map shows the lines as they will be before you commit. Every week starts from none. Then tap Spend the week.

What the town does

Two people you see often come to know each other: a new grey dashed line between them. A person you see often introduces you to someone from their circle: a new name on your map, with a dashed line to you. A person outside Home whom you see often has given you two of their evenings, so their lines in their own circle go thin that week. And once a week you run into someone by chance: another new name. A line, once there, never vanishes.

The news

Seven openings a week, each first heard by one person, who turns blue. Whoever hears passes it on to each person they are tied to: on a thick line always, on a thin line usually (three times in four), on a dashed line sometimes (one in three), and a little less at every pair of hands it has passed through. The blue mark travels along the lines; where it stops short, that person did not mention it. You keep what you hear. The week's line tells you who told you, who told you the same thing again, and what never reached you.

The verdict

After five weeks: how many of the town's openings you heard of; who told you first, by how often you saw them, against the paper's own table; how many pairs of hands the news passed through; what two other players would have heard from the same news; and what the paper says about towns with no dashed lines.
