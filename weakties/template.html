Granovetter's Dashed Line

You are looking for a job in a town of seven circles. Three weeks; each week you give seven evenings to the people you know. The town reacts. Then the week's news travels along the lines, and you watch which of it reaches you, and from whom.

Spend your evenings so that you hear of as many job openings as you can.

Built from Mark S. Granovetter, "The Strength of Weak Ties", American Journal of Sociology 78(6), 1973, pp. 1360–1380.

How to play

Spend your evenings so that you hear of as many job openings as you can.

The town

Seven circles of people, drawn as rings of dots. Inside a circle everyone sees everyone often. A few grey dashed lines join one circle to another. You are the big dot in the middle, in Home, with Ann, Ben and Cal. Your lines are black: the people with names are the people you know. At the start you see your three friends often (thick lines) and hardly see the eight others (dashed lines): someone from the firm you used to work at, from college, from your street, the choir, the club, your cousins. A thick line takes two evenings a week to stay thick.

A week

You have seven evenings. Tap a name, on the map or in the row of names, to give that person one evening; tap again for two; tap again for none. One evening makes the line thin (you see them now and then). Two make it thick (often). A line you give no evening thins by one step about one week in two, on a coin: thick to thin, thin to dashed; a dashed line stays dashed. The map shows a tapped line as it will be; the others as they are now. Then tap Spend the week, or Stay in all week if you gave no one an evening.

What the town does

Two people you see often come to know each other: a new grey dashed line between them. A person you see often introduces you to someone from their circle: a new name on your map, with a dashed line to you. A person outside Home whom you see often has given you two of their evenings, so their lines in their own circle go thin that week. And once a week you run into someone by chance: usually someone new, a dashed line; about one time in three someone you already know, and the old line comes back thick for that week. A line, once there, never vanishes.

The news

Seven openings a week, each first heard by one person, who turns blue. Whoever hears passes it on to each person they are tied to: on a thick line always, on a thin line usually (three times in four), on a dashed line sometimes (one in three), and a little less at every pair of hands it has passed through. The blue mark travels along the lines; where it stops short, that person did not mention it. You keep what you hear. The week's line tells you who told you, who told you the same thing again, and what never reached you.

The verdict

Your score: the openings you heard of in three weeks as a share of what could have been heard on this town's news (the best three weeks we could find knowing the news in advance, or your own run if it did better), in three bands of blue. Then the same news five ways: you, the best plan without foreknowledge, your circle, nobody, and the news known in advance; the best plan drawn on this town's map, with the lines its news came along; and who told you first, against the paper's own table.
