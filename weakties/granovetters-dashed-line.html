A week of evenings · Granovetter, 1973

Granovetter's Dashed Line

You are looking for a job in a town of seven circles. Three weeks; each week you give seven evenings to the people you know. The town reacts. Then the week's news travels along the lines, and you watch which of it reaches you, and from whom.

Spend your evenings so that you hear of as many job openings as you can.

Built from Mark S. Granovetter, "The Strength of Weak Ties", American Journal of Sociology 78(6), 1973, pp. 1360–1380.

The paper. The people closest to you move in your circle and hear what you hear. The person you hardly see spends their evenings somewhere else and is your only line into it. So the news comes down the dashed lines, and a town whose circles have no dashed lines between them is a town of islands.

Boston, around 1960. The West End, close family circles and nothing between them, could not form one organisation against the plan that demolished it; Charlestown, with its clubs and local jobs, fought the same city and won.

How to play

Spend your evenings so that you hear of as many job openings as you can.

The town

Seven circles of people, drawn as rings of dots. Inside a circle everyone sees everyone often. A few grey dashed lines join one circle to another. You are the big dot in the middle, in Home, with Ann, Ben and Cal. Your lines are black: the people with names are the people you know. At the start you see your three friends often (thick lines) and hardly see the eight others (dashed lines): someone from the firm you used to work at, from college, from your street, the choir, the club, your cousins. A thick line takes two evenings a week to stay thick.

A week

You have seven evenings. Tap a name, on the map or in the row of names, to give that person one evening; tap again for two; tap again for none. One evening makes the line thin (you see them now and then). Two make it thick (often). A line you give no evening thins by one step that week: thick to thin, thin to dashed; a dashed line stays dashed. The map shows a tapped line as it will be; the others as they are now. Then tap Spend the week, or Stay in all week if you gave no one an evening.

What the town does

Two people you see often come to know each other: a new grey dashed line between them. A person you see often introduces you to someone from their circle: a new name on your map, with a dashed line to you. A person outside Home whom you see often has given you two of their evenings, so their lines in their own circle go thin that week. And once a week you run into someone by chance: another new name. A line, once there, never vanishes.

The news

Seven openings a week, each first heard by one person, who turns blue. Whoever hears passes it on to each person they are tied to: on a thick line always, on a thin line usually (three times in four), on a dashed line sometimes (one in three), and a little less at every pair of hands it has passed through. The blue mark travels along the lines; where it stops short, that person did not mention it. You keep what you hear. The week's line tells you who told you, who told you the same thing again, and what never reached you.

The verdict

Your score: the openings you heard of in three weeks as a share of what could have been heard on this town's news (the best three weeks we could find knowing the news in advance, or your own run if it did better), in three bands of blue. Then the same news five ways: you, the best plan without foreknowledge, your circle, nobody, and the news known in advance; who told you first, against the paper's own table; and the paper's two towns, the West End and Charlestown.

Game built from this paper; the paper is the authority where the two disagree. Page numbers below are the journal's.

What the paper says

The strength of a tie between two people is a combination of the time they spend together, the emotional intensity, the intimacy and the services they do each other (p. 1361). The stronger the tie between you and a friend, the more of your other friends you share: strong ties take time, bring your friends into each other's company, and run between similar people, and once two of your close friends know of each other, anything short of friendship between them is a strain (p. 1362). Pushed to the limit, for exposition, this says that a triangle with two strong sides and no third side never occurs (Fig. 1, p. 1363). It follows that no strong tie can be the only path between two groups, so whatever flows between groups flows along weak ties; and that news passed through weak ties reaches more people over greater social distance, provided the urge to retell it fades a little at every retelling, as it must in any real network (pp. 1364–1366). At the level of one person: the people you see most are inside your circle and know what you know; the person you hardly see is in another circle and is your line into it. Of 54 people in a Boston suburb who had found their job through a contact, 17 per cent saw the contact often, 56 per cent now and then, 28 per cent rarely, and in 84 per cent of 64 cases the news had come straight from the employer or through one intermediary (pp. 1371–1372). At the level of a community: a neighbourhood of close circles with no weak ties between them cannot organise, however warm the circles; the West End of Boston could not resist the renewal that demolished it (pp. 1373–1375).

What the game does with it

You are the job changer of the paper's survey, and your one control is the paper's one measured variable: how often you see each person. Seven evenings a week on the people you know; two evenings make a line "often", one "now and then", the survey's own categories; a line you give no evening thins by one step that week and never vanishes, so a close friend costs two evenings a week to keep, which is the paper's premise that strength is time. The town reacts as the paper says it does: two people you see often come to know each other; a person you see often introduces you to their circle (the paper quotes Gans, who was drawn into his first contact's circle in the West End); and a chance meeting reactivates a tie once a week, as the paper's respondents reported. Then the week's news travels along the lines. The verdict scores your three weeks as a share of what could have been heard on this town's news (the best three weeks found with the news known in advance, or your own run if it did better), shows the same news five ways (you, the best plan without foreknowledge, your circle, nobody, the news known in advance), and reads who told you first against the paper's Boston table. The paper's second table, how many pairs of hands the news passed through (39 per cent straight from the employer, 45 through one intermediary, 13 through two, 3 through more), is in the calibration and not on the verdict: the toy reproduces its shape (five in six within one pair of hands) but not its middle.

Mechanism kind and claim boundaries

Toy. The game runs a model built to illustrate the paper's argument, calibrated so that a mixed week reproduces the paper's two tables. The town (seven circles, one of them yours, every circle a clique, nine dashed lines among the outer circles and none into Home but yours), the probabilities with which a person passes news on (always on a thick line, three times in four on a thin one, one in three on a dashed one, and a fifth less at every pair of hands), the rate of news, the rule that a person who gives you two evenings sees their own circle less, and the rates of introductions and chance meetings are ours. The paper says only that strong ties are more motivated and that retelling dampens.

What the game demonstrates: that under this game's assumptions, evenings spread across circles bring more distinct news than evenings kept in the circle, that the circle's news reaches you more than once, and that the pattern of who told you first, for a week like the paper's respondents', looks like the paper's table. What the game assumes: everything in the paragraph above, and that news is heard or not, with nothing about its quality. What the paper establishes: the argument from tie strength to bridges and diffusion, which it states as a hypothesis with the evidence it had; and the two Boston tables, from a small sample the paper itself calls exploratory. A run of this game is no evidence for the paper's claim about the world.

The discovery survives plausible changes to our own choices: with the pass probabilities, the damping, the rate of news and the finite-time rule varied, spreading across circles still beats the circle and the circle still tells you its news more than once. What the game cannot show is anything the paper leaves out: the content of ties, what a lead is worth, influence exerted on your behalf, negative or one-sided ties.

What is left out

Emotional intensity, intimacy and services as parts of strength (the survey used time alone, and so does the game). Similarity as a reason for closure. The small-world studies, including the finding that chains from white senders to a Black target were completed twice as often when the first link was an acquaintance rather than a friend (pp. 1368–1369). Rapoport and Horvath's tracing of a school's friendship choices, where more children were reached through seventh and eighth choices than through first and second (p. 1369). The paper's remarks on transitivity models of small groups. The quality of a lead by the path it came along ("earmarked for one person", p. 1373): the verdict reports the hands, but every opening counts one. The community section is read on the end screen, not played.

Design memo, in brief

Three concepts were written out: the week (you are the job changer; a town that reacts), the weave (you plan a neighbourhood's workplaces and clubs, and see whether a leaflet can cross it), and the leaflet (you are the news, tapping your way along lines on a screen with no words). The week was built because its control is the paper's own variable and its reaction is the paper's own mechanism; the leaflet's journey is kept as the week's animation. Look: a Swiss grid in winter, white, ink, one cold blue for news and nothing else, lines in the paper's own code, solid for strong and dashed for weak. Revised after the author's plays: chosen people ringed on the map; five weeks cut to three; a score against what could have been heard; lines made states that thin when untended, instead of every week starting from none; a closing bet tried and removed as too much of a quiz.
