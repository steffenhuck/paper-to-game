How to play

Part one: build a country where boys and girls are friends. Tap one card in each of three rows: how big the houses are, whether jobs go mostly to dads or to both the same, and what parents say a child should learn (do as you’re told, work hard, think for yourself). The picture shows what you set. Ring the bell: ten kids go out to play, boys on the left, girls on the right, and lines are drawn between friends. A red line is a friend from the other side. The ruler under the playground counts how many of the ten kids have one. You get three countries; the cards go back to the middle each time. Then the map: your countries on the ruler next to Finland and Portugal.

Part two: get the big-house kids mixing like the small-house kids. One street in Britain, five small houses and five big. The small-house kids’ mark is on the ruler as a dotted tick. Tap one card in each of three rows for the big-house families: what the mums think about mums working, what a child should learn, and whether the week after school is all clubs, a bit of both, or out on the street. Draw it, and the big-house kids draw their friends again. Three tries. Then the game says what moved the mark, what didn’t, and why the paper says the gap stays.

The rule of the world. Boys on the left, girls on the right, ten kids, twelve lines: that never changes. Only the number of red lines does.

The numbers under the drawing come from the paper; the game rounds them to kids of ten and says “a touch” or “about half a kid” rather than decimals. Which kids hold the red lines is a coin toss with a visible seed in the address bar.

About this game

The paper. Manuel Bagues and Natalia Zinovyeva, Gender Segregation in Childhood Friendships and the Gender-Equality Paradox, working paper, University of Warwick, 2 July 2025. Game built from this paper; the paper is the authority where the two disagree.

What it found. Across 37 European and North American countries, half a million children aged 11 to 15 were asked how many close friends they have who are boys and who are girls. At eleven, about a quarter of close friends are of the other sex: in Finland one in six, in Portugal four in ten. The richer, more gender-equal countries, where parents say a child should think for themselves, are the ones where children mix least. The same countries are the ones whose universities are most sorted by subject. That is the gender-equality paradox, and the paper finds it on the playground years before anyone picks a subject. Inside one country (ten thousand British children followed from birth), richer families’ children have fewer friends of the other sex, but children of mothers with more equal views have slightly more. So the paradox is prosperity, not views. Richer parents fill the week with clubs and lifts, and raise calmer, more popular children who are happy with the friends they have; those explain part of the gap, and the rest is unexplained. Children who had friends of both kinds at seven pick slightly less gendered subjects at sixteen.

What kind of mechanism this is. Reconstructed estimates, played as a toy. Every number the game runs on is one the paper reports (its correlations across countries, its marginal effects within Britain, its shares), and a headless check reproduces the paper’s own landmarks from them: Finland and Portugal, the age-eleven average, the gap between the poorest and richest fifth, the sign and order of every lever. But the paper reports associations and says, plainly, that none of them is causal; the game lets you set a dial and watch the share move.

  • The game demonstrates that the paper’s patterns, put together as if they were levers, take a player who builds the rich, equal, think-for-yourself country to the sorted end of the ruler; that inside a country the mothers’ lever points the other way; and that the family levers cannot close the gap between big houses and small.
  • The game assumes what the construction invented: that a country’s share is a weighted sum of three dials, each weighted by its own reported correlation; that a share becomes ten children and twelve lines; what a big house typically does; that a lever can be pulled one and a half standard deviations with the paper’s per-standard-deviation effect holding all the way; that effects add.
  • The paper establishes the correlations and marginal effects as reported, and nothing finer: not what would happen to Finland’s playground if Finland were poorer, nor to a rich child’s friends if the parents stopped the dance class.

Where the dials come apart. In the paper’s 37 countries, rich, equal and think-for-yourself go together. When you set the jobs dial without the parents’ dial, or build a country from a corner few real countries live in, the game says so: the paper cannot tell from outside which does the work. That is what part two is for.

What was cut. The three equality measures are one dial; the ages 13 and 15; how easy it is to talk to friends; siblings, fathers at home, single-sex schools; puberty; romance at seventeen; the policy paragraph.
