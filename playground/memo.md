# Me and My Friends — design memo

## 0. Given

A gift, not one of Steffen's: the beta line goes in the footer, no signature, no counter. ## 1. Paper

Manuel Bagues and Natalia Zinovyeva, *Gender Segregation in Childhood Friendships and the Gender-Equality Paradox*, working paper dated 2 July 2025 (University of Warwick), 83 pages with appendix. JEL J16, I2. No DOI in the PDF; no code or data attached. Read from `inbox/` as a typeset PDF: every number below is from typeset text or tables except where marked *read off a figure*. The paper can be read on the authors' pages at Warwick; the memo cites by section, table and figure number.

## 2. What the paper hands over

**Primary: an estimate.** Two datasets and a long table of associations. Country level (HBSC, 37 countries, ages 11–15, 2002–2010): the share of close friends who are of the opposite sex (mean 27 per cent at 11; Finland 16, Portugal 39; Table A1, p. 17) correlates negatively with every measure of "vertical" gender equality (GII ρ = 0.43, GGGI ρ = −0.48, "men have more right to a job" ρ = 0.36), with GDP per capita (ρ = −0.39) and, most strongly, with the share of parents who say a child should learn independence rather than obedience or hard work (ρ = −0.67; Figure 3, Table 1). Individual level (Millennium Cohort Study, about 10,000 British children born 2000–01): children from richer families have fewer opposite-sex friends (lowest income fifth 65.6 per cent with a mixed friend group at 11, highest fifth 48.2; Figure 4; −3.9 points per standard deviation of income, Table 3), in 34 of the 37 HBSC countries too; but, inside the country, children whose mothers hold more gender-egalitarian views have *more* opposite-sex friends (+0.8 points per SD overall, +1.8 at age 11; Tables 3, A14). Clubs outside school reduce opposite-sex friendship (−2.1 points per SD), unsupervised time raises it (+3.3), a good relationship with the parents lowers it (−1.4), "think for yourself" parents' children have 3 points fewer than "obey" parents' (Tables 4, 5). Richer children are calmer, more cooperative, score higher, enter puberty later, and every one of those traits goes with same-sex friendship; together they account for about a quarter of the income gradient, the week (clubs, roaming, parents) for about an eighth (Table 6, pp. 33–34). Richer children are also more popular and more satisfied with their friends (Table A18): preference, not constraint. Downstream: children with opposite-sex friends at 7 pick subjects at 16 with a slightly higher opposite-sex share (0.067 SD at GCSE, 0.072 at A-level), still have opposite-sex friends at 14 (+6 points), hold more egalitarian views at 14 (0.09 SD) and want jobs with about 3 points more opposite-sex workers (Table 7). At the country level, where children mix, women's propensity to graduate in STEM is higher (ρ = 0.43) and the Duncan segregation index lower (ρ = −0.47; Figure 5).

**Secondary: an argument.** Lareau's two childhoods: concerted cultivation (clubs, lifts, supervised afternoons, self-expression) against natural growth (the street, whoever is there); and Charles and Bradley's "gendered selves", that prosperity lets children express gender-typical preferences in carefully arranged, gender-sorted settings. The paper does not formalise it, and says plainly that none of its estimates is causal.

## 3. The discovery, and its kind

**A counterintuitive result, twice.** The public belief: in rich countries where women and men are treated alike, boys and girls will be friends more. The paper: less, and already at eleven, and the countries where the playground is most sorted are the ones whose universities are most sorted by subject. Then the second turn, which is the paper's own contribution: look inside a country and the equality part of the paradox reverses (egalitarian mothers' children mix *more*), while the money part stays (richer families' children mix less, everywhere). So the paradox is prosperity, not norms, and what prosperity buys: a week of dance class and football club and a lift home, and a calm, popular child who is happy with the friends they have. The game stages both turns and ends on the chain the paper then draws to subject choice at sixteen, read, not played.

## 4. The core narrative

In rich countries, where mums and dads say girls and boys can do anything, you would think boys and girls would be friends more. They are friends *less*. The reason is not what the parents believe. It is money, and what money buys: rich kids go to dance class and football club and get driven everywhere, and they are happy with the friends they make there, who are mostly all girls or all boys. Kids with no clubs play out on the street with whoever is there. And the kids who had friends of both kinds at seven pick slightly different subjects at sixteen, and the countries where the playground mixes are the ones where the university mixes.

## 5. Who lives in the paper

- **The country** (37 of them): rich or poor, equal or not, parents who prize obedience, hard work or independence. Controls the conditions; could be asked to anticipate its playground. A non-obvious thing to play, and the paradox lives at its level.
- **The parent**: fills in the survey at nine months (four statements about mothers and work), at three (which qualities matter: obey, work hard, think for yourself), at seven (how many clubs a week), at eleven (allowed out without a grown-up?). Controls every lever the paper measures that a family chooses; income is dealt. Could be asked to anticipate what the child draws.
- **The child**: names up to three boy and three girl close friends (HBSC); says whether their friends are mostly their own sex or a mixture (MCS); at sixteen picks subjects. Controls the outcome itself, which makes the child the demand side of the paper's story and a poor player of it: the paper's finding is partly that rich children *want* same-sex groups.
- **The club** (the dance class, 80 per cent girls; the football club): the institution where the sorting happens. Controls who is in the room. A candidate agent; rejected because the paper observes clubs only through siblings and never the room itself.
- **The researchers' instruments**: the two survey questions, the country-level correlation, the within-country probit. The identification is theirs: country level against family level.

**What this game does differently.** *Position*: the parents of a street, answering the paper's own survey (an agent, plural), after a prologue in which the player is a country. Not an experimenter, not a witness, and not a nation drawing a curve: the controls are the four survey questions the paper measures. *Format*: a challenge the player cannot meet the way they expect. A target is given in the aim's units (kids in ten with a friend from the other side), the obvious levers go the wrong way, and the game names the shortfall as the result. Neither a scored allocation over time nor a measurement game. *Device*: one of our own, **the crossing**: a child's drawing of the playground with boys on one side and girls on the other, friendship lines drawn one by one, red only when a line crosses the middle, and a crayon ruler from 0 to 10 under it where each try drops a mark. No fold, no sheet, no weather. *Chassis*: crayon on buff sugar paper (Drawn, by the Given); a light page after a dark one; five crayons; a child's print hand; wobbly rings round the chosen answer. Shares nothing with Julian's ink-and-one-rose but the family.

## 7. The aim and the verdict

**Aim, part one:** *Build a country where boys and girls are friends.*
**Aim, part two:** *Get the big-house kids mixing like the small-house kids.*
Yardstick, both parts: *Of ten kids, how many have a friend from the other side*, a crayon ruler with ten marks, read in the kids the aim counts.

**Verdict leads with:**
- *What you did.* The country you built where most kids had a friend from the other side was the poorest, least equal, do-as-you're-told one; the rich, equal, think-for-yourself one came out at the Finland end. Inside, you got the big-house kids to N of ten; the small-house kids were at about seven.
- *What the paper says.* Richer, more equal countries have more boys-on-one-side playgrounds at eleven, and more boys-on-one-side universities too. Inside a country it is the money, not the views: children of equal-minded mothers mix a little *more*; children of richer families mix less, in 34 countries of 37. Part of why is the week money buys, part is the calm, popular child money raises, who likes the friends they have; most of it the paper cannot say.
- *What that means.* Children with a friend from the other side at seven pick slightly less boyish or girlish subjects at sixteen, still mix at fourteen, hold more equal views, and want slightly more mixed jobs. Where the playground mixes, so does the university. The paper shows patterns, not levers; this game let you pull them.

## 8. Three concepts

**A. Build a Country, then Look Inside** (controller for ninety seconds, then agent: the parents of a street; a challenge that cannot be met the expected way). *Controls*: three three-way dials for the country (how rich the houses, whether jobs go to dads or to both, what parents say a child should learn), then three for the big-house families on one street (what the mums think about mums working; what a child should learn; whether the week is clubs, a mix, or the street). *Deliberation*: which dial will mix the playground? Then: which lever closes the gap between the big houses and the small ones? *Sees after*: the playground drawn line by line, red lines across the middle, the ruler mark dropping; after the tries, Finland and Portugal placed on the same ruler. *Moment*: the "good" country comes out at the Finland end; then, inside, the mums' dial goes the other way, and the gap will not close. *Fun*: anticipation (ring the bell), agency (the dials move the mark, the wrong way), discovery (twice), mastery (the second country is built to test one dial). *Second playthrough*: a different hypothesis per dial, the corners few real countries live in, the street with the levers in a different order.

**B. One Childhood** (agent: a single parent; a picture book of five ages; no score, fates in words). The player answers the MCS's own questions as they come (nine months, three, seven, eleven) for one child in a dealt house; at seven, eleven, fourteen the child draws "me and my friends"; at sixteen a subject list. *Deliberation*: what kind of parent to be. *Moment*: the cultivated, cheerful child draws all girls or all boys. *Fun*: anticipation, discovery. *Second playthrough*: the other childhood. Fails the walkthrough on size: the paper's per-child effects are a few points on a coin that lands mixed six times in ten, so one child's drawing is noise and the player learns nothing from it; ten children would have to be drawn to show a pattern, and then the player is a street, which is A.

**C. The Kid's Week** (subject: an eleven-year-old; wordless; the reckless one). A crayon week, seven afternoons; the parents' money has filled some with clubs and lifts; the player taps where to go when there is a choice and, in each place, taps who to play with from who is there (the dance class: nine girls and a boy; the street: whoever). Lines form over the week. *Deliberation*: who do I pick, given who is here. *Moment*: a week of clubs leaves no one to pick from. *Fun*: agency, discovery, a screen with no words on it. *Why it loses*: the player becomes the demand side, and half the paper's explanation is that rich children *prefer* their same-sex groups and are happy in them; a player who is the preference cannot find that out. And the room's composition is invented: the paper never sees the club. Its walkthrough is in section 9.

## 10. The pick, and why

**A.** The paradox is the paper's title and its first act is a ninety-second trap a stranger walks into with their own hands: the country they would want to live in sorts its playground. The second act is the paper's own move, the within-country reversal, and it lands as a second surprise the stranger has to think about, because they have just learnt the opposite. The target in part two is honest: it cannot be reached with the paper's levers, and the game says so, with the paper's shares. The sentence afterwards has no "guess": *I built the country I'd want, and its playground was the most sorted; I looked inside and it wasn't the equality, it was the money and the week it buys.* Originality: no game in the table has had the player build a country from three survey answers and then prise it open; none has drawn its data as a child's drawing.

**Author test.** Bagues and Zinovyeva know the paradox and will enjoy watching someone build Finland on purpose. What they will enjoy more is that the game's second act is their own argument: the dials that cannot be told apart from outside (rich, equal, permissive travel together, ρ 0.59–0.72) come apart inside the country, and the game says plainly where the paper's own numbers run out (a quarter, an eighth, the rest unexplained). The corners of the country dials where few real countries live are flagged on screen, which is the caveat they wrote themselves (p. 6).

**Runner-up: B, One Childhood.** Switch if the author wants a single child's story over a street's shares and accepts that the drawings are then drawn from the paper's probabilities with a declared magnification.

**What the reckless concept would have given.** The room deciding: a week of clubs with no one of the other kind to pick. Kept: the week is one of the three family dials, with clubs at one end and the street at the other, and the result line says what the week did. Given up: being the child, and a screen with no words.

## 11. The shape

Two parts and a verdict, about five to seven minutes. **Part one**: up to three countries built from three dials, each reset to the middle before every try; after each, the playground and the ruler; after the third, or after the second if the player stops, the reveal with Finland and Portugal on the ruler. **Part two**: the street (no choice: the two playgrounds and the gap), then up to three tries at the three family dials for the big houses, each reset to the middle; after the third, the shortfall named, the paper's shares, the children's bubbles. **Verdict**: three lines, the chain to sixteen, ANOTHER STREET, How to play, About, the beta line. Presets are the paper's: the dial positions are the paper's minimum, mean and maximum (Table A1), the quintiles are Figure 4's, the levers' ends are one and a half standard deviations. No score; the ruler is the yardstick and the verdict is in words. Seed visible in the URL; the seed sets only where the kids stand and which of them hold the lines.

## 12. Controls, mechanism, feedback, moment

**Controls.** Part one: three rows of three crayon cards (house: small, middling, big; jobs: mostly dads, dads first, both the same; parents say: do as you're told, work hard, think for yourself); tapping a card redraws that piece of the country picture at once (the preview), and RING THE BELL commits. Part two: three rows of three (mums: stay home, either way, same as dads; a child should learn: do as you're told, work hard, think for yourself; the week: all clubs, a bit of both, out on the street); tapping redraws the family picture; DRAW IT commits. Every row starts in the middle each try.

**Mechanism.** Reconstructed estimates, played as a toy (section 16). Part one: the country's share of opposite-sex friends is the age-11 mean plus a weighted sum of the three dials, each weighted by its own reported correlation and scaled so that all-up lands on Finland and all-down on Portugal; the share of children with no opposite-sex friend follows from the share by the line through Finland and Portugal (Figure A9); the ruler reads ten times one minus that. Part two: the big-house share is the top quintile's (Figure 4) plus the paper's age-11 marginal effects for each lever times the distance of the lever from what a big house typically does. Robustness in section 17.

**Feedback.** The crossing: the playground redrawn, lines appearing one by one, red across the middle; the ruler mark dropping; earlier tries' marks staying as small pencil ticks; the result stated by the game in its own voice (what you set, what came of it, what the mark did, what that means).

**Moment.** Part one, first ring of the bell: six of ten, "the Finland end". Part two, first draw: the mums' dial moved it the other way, and the mark fell short of seven.

## 14. What is cut

The three vertical-equality measures (collapsed to one dial, "who gets the jobs"); the three ages 11, 13, 15 (the game is eleven); the quality-of-friendship questions; siblings, father at home, single-sex schools (controls, not the story); the authoritative/authoritarian distinction is kept as a card but its effect is a touch; the ordered-probit against the probit (the game shows shares); the adult Facebook comparison (Figure A11); the puberty index; the romance at seventeen (Table A20); the policy paragraph of the conclusion (one sentence on the verdict screen).

## 15. Divergences

- **Levers for correlations.** The paper reports associations and says none is causal; the game lets the player set a dial and see the share move. Declared on screen at each discovery and on About (section 16).
- **The country toy.** The paper never regresses the share on all three country measures at once; the game weights each dial by its own bivariate correlation and scales to Finland and Portugal. A multivariate fit from the reported correlations would give almost all the weight to parenting and a small positive weight to equality; the two constructions agree wherever the dials move together and disagree in the corners, which the game flags as "few real countries look like this" (section 17).
- **Ten kids, twelve lines.** A country's share becomes a drawing of ten children with twelve friendship lines; which children hold the red lines is a seeded draw.
- **What a big house typically does.** The levers in part two move the big-house share relative to the top quintile's typical mums' views, parenting values, clubs and roaming; those typical values are ours, built from the paper's correlations (Tables A4, A6), and the calibration re-runs the mechanism with them set to zero.
- **Lever ends at ±1.5 SD**, and clubs floored at the lowest a family can go; the paper's effects are per SD around the mean.
- **The street is five small and five big houses**: the paper's bottom and top income fifths (Figure 4), nothing in between.
- **Finland and Portugal's dial pictures** are placed by the paper's description (Nordic high on equality, income and permissive parenting; Southern Europe low), not by their exact values, which the PDF does not list.

## 16. Mechanism kind and claim boundaries

**Kind: reconstructed estimates, played as a toy.** Every number the mechanism runs on is one the paper reports (correlations in Figure 3 and p. 14; marginal effects in Tables 4, 5 and A14; shares in Figure 4 and the text), and the calibration check reproduces the paper's own landmarks from them. But the paper reports associations and says, in three places, that none is causal; the game lets the player set a dial and watch the share move. So:

- **The game demonstrates** that the paper's reported patterns, put together as if they were levers, carry a player who builds the rich, equal, think-for-yourself country to the sorted end of the ruler and a player who looks inside to a gap that the family levers cannot close; and that the equality lever points one way across countries and the other way inside one.
- **The game assumes** everything the construction invented: that a country's share is the weighted sum of three dials, each weighted by its bivariate correlation; that a share becomes ten children and twelve lines; what a big house typically does; that a lever can be pulled to one and a half standard deviations with the paper's per-SD effect holding all the way; that effects add.
- **The paper establishes** the correlations and marginal effects as reported, with their standard errors, and nothing finer: not what would happen to Finland's playground if Finland were poorer, nor to a rich child's friends if the parents stopped the dance class. The game says so at both discoveries and on About.

**Correlation is not causation.** The dials are levers only in the game. On every result the game speaks of "the paper's 37 countries" and "ten thousand British children", and the verdict's last line says the paper shows patterns, not levers. **An average effect is not an individual response.** The game never draws one child's fate from a marginal effect: it draws a playground of ten whose red lines are the share, and says "of ten kids".

**Where the discovery comes from.** Part one's discovery (all dials up lands at the Finland end) follows from the three reported negative correlations and from nothing the designer chose; it survives the multivariate alternative construction on the diagonal (section 17). Off the diagonal, the two constructions disagree whenever the equality dial moves without the parents' dial, and there the game adds the paper's own hedge (p. 6: "these correlations may simply reflect underlying socioeconomic development"): from outside it cannot tell which does the work. Part two's discoveries (the mums' lever goes the other way; the gap cannot be closed) follow from the signs of Tables A14, 4 and 5 and from the size of the Figure 4 gap against them; the "cannot be closed" result is checked with the typical-big-house construction removed. The 1.5 SD lever ends are the designer's and only change how much of the gap closes (about half, against the paper's observed eighth plus quarter), which the shortfall text states as the difference between what families could do and what they do.

## 17. Calibration plan and result

Checked headlessly against the paper (`calibration.js`, 42 checks, `calibration-result.txt`): all dials up reproduces Finland's 16 per cent share and 41 per cent with no opposite-sex friend; all down reproduces Portugal's 39 and 6; the middle reproduces the age-11 mean (27 per cent) and about a fifth with none (p. 17: 21 per cent); each dial lowers mixing when raised, with swings ordered as the reported correlations (0.67 > 0.43 > 0.39); the alternative multivariate construction from the same correlations orders the diagonal identically, and every setting where the two constructions disagree in sign carries either the corner line or the hedge line on screen. Part two: the small houses sit at 65.6 per cent (about seven of ten); the big houses with levers in the middle within 0.03 of the top fifth's 48.2; each lever's sign matches its table (mums +, think-for-yourself −, work-hard between and near obey, street +), the week moves most and the mums' views least; no setting of the three levers reaches the small-house share, the best setting is the one the paper's signs imply, and it closes about half the gap; both survive with the typical-big-house adjustment set to zero; the Table 5 income effect per SD times the 2.8 SD between top and bottom fifths gives the Figure 4 gap. The drawing: over 300 seeds and all 27 settings, exactly round(kids) children hold a red line, twelve lines every time, and the red-line count within one of share times twelve. Words: no decimals anywhere in result text.

## 18. Playing it in numbers (build.md §3)
