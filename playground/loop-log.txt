SHORTFALL
  You can’t get there from here. Pulling every lever as far as it goes (mums work the same as dads, “do as you’re told”, out on the street) gets the big-house kids to five and a half of ten, and closes about half of the gap.
  The paper, looking at what families actually do rather than what they could, puts the week at about an eighth of the gap and the children themselves at a quarter: big-house kids are calmer, get on with everyone, score higher, grow up a little later, and every one of those goes with same-side friends. They are also more popular, and happier with the friends they have.
  The rest of the gap, more than half, nobody can explain yet.

VERDICT
  Of the countries you built, the one where most kids had a friend from the other side (nine and a half of ten) was small houses, jobs mostly for dads, “do as you’re told”. The fewest (about six) was big houses, jobs for both the same, “think for yourself”. Inside, you got the big-house kids to five and a half of ten; the small-house kids were at six and a half.
  Richer, more equal countries have more boys-on-one-side playgrounds at eleven, and more boys-on-one-side universities too. Inside a country it is the money, not the views: children of equal-minded mums mix a little more; children of richer families mix less, in 34 countries of 37. Part of why is the week money buys, part is the calm, popular child money raises, who likes the friends they have; most of it the paper cannot say.
  Children with a friend from the other side at seven pick slightly less boyish or girlish subjects at sixteen, still mix at fourteen, hold more equal views, and want slightly more mixed jobs. Where the playground mixes, so does the university. The paper shows patterns, not levers; this game let you pull them.
