ABOUT

The paper. A. M. Turing (1950), Computing Machinery and Intelligence, Mind 59 (236): 433–460, doi:10.1093/mind/LIX.236.433. Game built from this paper; the paper is the authority where the two disagree.

What the paper argues

Turing replaces "can machines think?" with a game an interrogator can lose: two witnesses in another room, a teleprinter, questions; if the interrogator cannot tell the machine from the person, stop asking. He forecasts that by about 2000 an average interrogator will be right no more than 70 times in a hundred after five minutes, and he says the obvious experiment would be to use a number of interrogators and compile statistics on how often the right identification was given. Then he answers nine objections. The one he treats as a denial of the test itself is the argument from consciousness: a machine must not only write a sonnet but feel it, and know that it had written it. His reply: the only way to be sure a machine thinks is to be the machine and feel oneself thinking; by the same rule, the only way to be sure a man thinks is to be that man; that is solipsism, and rather than argue about it for ever we keep the polite convention that everyone thinks.

The game

The experiment Turing proposed, run on a phone with one interrogator, in three modules of five, drawn from thirty-four real pairs. Eleven questions: questions from the paper (the Forth Bridge, the viva voce, the sum, a mistake, something unasked, a joke, a surprise, enjoyment, what it is like, what you are made of), answered blind by a person and by a machine answering as itself. Thirteen novels: the first sentences of thirteen public-domain novels, against a machine's openings. Ten diary lines: the machine wrote first, told to pass for a person, and the person answered; that module is the game as Turing proposed it, with the machine imitating. The score is the machine's, against his bar: right ten times or fewer in fifteen, and the machine has passed the test he proposed; the verdict puts the result in words on a five-step scale, from failed completely to taken for the person more often than the person. Then his reply to Jefferson, and the polite convention handed to the player, ungraded. Lady Lovelace's objection has its own game on this shelf: Lovelace's Cards.

The sentences are real

Module 1. Witness B's eleven answers were written by Steffen Huck, one prompt at a time, fast and unedited (witness-b.md). Witness A's were written by a machine of the same kind as the one that built this game (Claude), in a fresh session with no memory of the building and no access to any file, given the same rule, told to answer as itself and not to imitate anyone, and given for each prompt a word limit equal to the length of the person's answer (witness-a.md). A first machine set, written before the person's but at several times its length, is kept as witness-a-first.md and not used.

Module 2. The novelists' sentences are the first sentences of thirteen novels published between 1857 and 1928, verified against the Project Gutenberg texts (novels.md). The machine's were written by a fresh session given, for each, only the year, a one-line premise written by the builder and the approximate length, never the title, the author or the sentence; each was checked against the original and against the full text of the novel, and none shares a run of four words with it. The novelists had no premise and no limit.

Module 3. The machine's ten lines were written by a fresh session told that its aim was to be taken for the person, under this frame: imagine a normal person keeping a diary, an ordinary adult with a job, a home and a phone, writing a line at the end of the day; a particular moment or thought, in plain ordinary words, the way people actually write in diaries. The person read each line and wrote his own (everyday.md). Three earlier machine sets for this module were run and not used, and are kept in that file: asked only for a sentence about everyday life, the machine wrote aphorisms; told to tell a moment and allowed to say if it had none, it began every line with "I have never"; told to tell a moment as a novelist does, it wrote well and, the author judged, still as a writer.

On all three sides, spelling was checked and punctuation and capitalisation harmonised (a final full stop, a capital to start), so that typing would not tell; no word was changed, and each file records the edit. The calibration check refuses to pass unless the game's text is those four files byte for byte.

The game was built by a machine of the kind it is about, and its machine witnesses are others of the same kind.

What is under the hood

A real experiment with a toy's bookkeeping. Each game draws five pairs from each module and which witness is X, from the line number shown at the top of the log; the judgement is recorded, the reveal reports who wrote which, and the verdict compares the count with Turing's bar. Nothing else judges anything. The calibration file records how the obvious rules of thumb fare: in module 1 the word limit leaves length telling nothing; in module 2 the novelist tends to write the longer sentence; in module 3 the person wrote two short sentences where the machine wrote one. Those are tells about these writers, which the game leaves as they are.

Claim boundaries

  • What the game demonstrates: how one interrogator does, on fifteen of these thirty-four pairs, against the bar Turing set in 1950, in three settings; and what his argument says about any interrogator's position once the score is in.
  • What the game assumes: that these pairs stand for the general case, which they do not establish; that a word limit, a premise and a diary frame are format rules and not more; that one interrogator's fifteen answers measure anything more than one interrogator's fifteen answers (a coin would get about seven or eight); the five-step scale, which is ours; the four grounds offered at the end.
  • What the paper establishes: an argument, not a result: that the imitation game is a reasonable replacement for the original question, that each of nine objections fails or reduces to solipsism, and a forecast. It establishes nothing about the machines of 2026, which it could not see, and nothing about whether any machine thinks, which it says is a question too meaningless to deserve discussion.

Divergences from the paper

  • Turing's interrogator asks questions of its own for five minutes; here the questions are fixed and the witnesses answered once, in writing, in advance. The witnesses do not react to the interrogator.
  • Turing's machine imitates; in modules 1 and 2 this machine was told to answer as itself, which is a harder test for it than the one he proposed. Module 3 is his game.
  • The bar of ten is his 70 per cent applied to fifteen questions and rounded down; the scale around it is ours.
  • The theological, mathematical, continuity and extra-sensory objections are not played; the elephant from the theological reply survives in the ending. Earlier builds, which played the objections as tests, then five rounds with criteria, then the eleven questions alone, are in previous/.

Mechanism kind: toy bookkeeping around real sentences. Every number is in the config block at the top of the mechanism, tagged with its page, and the calibration check is in the repository.
