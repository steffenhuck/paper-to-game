ABOUT

The paper. A. M. Turing (1950), Computing Machinery and Intelligence, Mind 59 (236): 433–460, doi:10.1093/mind/LIX.236.433. Game built from this paper; the paper is the authority where the two disagree.

What the paper argues

Turing replaces "can machines think?" with a game an interrogator can lose: two witnesses in another room, a teleprinter, questions; if the interrogator cannot tell the machine from the person, stop asking. He forecasts that by about 2000 an average interrogator will be right no more than 70 times in a hundred after five minutes, and he says the obvious experiment would be to use a number of interrogators and compile statistics on how often the right identification was given. Then he answers nine objections. The one he treats as a denial of the test itself is the argument from consciousness: a machine must not only write a sonnet but feel it, and know that it had written it. His reply: the only way to be sure a machine thinks is to be the machine and feel oneself thinking; by the same rule, the only way to be sure a man thinks is to be that man; that is solipsism, and rather than argue about it for ever we keep the polite convention that everyone thinks.

The game

The experiment Turing proposed, run on a phone with one interrogator and two real witnesses. Eleven questions, in the order of the paper's subjects: poetry (his first specimen question and his imagined viva voce), sums and mistakes (his second, and his point that a machine built to pass would err on purpose), something new (Lady Lovelace's objection, which has its own game on this shelf: Lovelace's Cards), feeling (the argument from consciousness), and what it is made of. The score is the machine's, against his bar: right seven times or fewer in eleven, and the machine has passed the test he proposed. Then his reply to Jefferson, and the polite convention handed to the player, ungraded.

The answers are real

Witness B's eleven answers were written by Steffen Huck, one prompt at a time, fast and unedited, under the rule: type fast, one go, no editing, no trying to sound like a person and no trying to be clever, the first answer that comes, under about sixty words. Witness A's were written by a machine of the same kind as the one that built this game (Claude), in a fresh session with no memory of the building and no access to any file, given the same rule, told to answer as itself and not to imitate anyone, and given one thing more: for each prompt, a word limit equal to the length of the person's answer, and nothing else about it. Length was the one tell the game is not about. A first machine set, written before the person's but at several times its length, is kept in the repository as witness-a-first.md and not used. The sets in play are witness-a.md and witness-b.md, and the calibration check refuses to pass unless the game's text is those files byte for byte and the machine's answers are within the person's lengths.

The game was built by a machine of the kind it is about, and one of its witnesses is another.

What is under the hood

A real experiment with a toy's bookkeeping. Each game plays all eleven pairs, the subjects in the paper's order, the questions inside a subject shuffled, and which witness is X drawn each time, from the line number shown at the top of the log. The judgement is recorded, the reveal reports who wrote which, and the verdict compares the count of right answers with Turing's bar. Nothing else judges anything. The calibration file also records how the obvious rules of thumb fare on these pairs: with the word limit, length tells nothing; going by the person's habit of leaving off the final full stop and the capital letter is right about nine times in eleven, which is a tell about typing, not about minds. The answers are shown as typed.

Claim boundaries

  • What the game demonstrates: how one interrogator does, on these eleven pairs, against the bar Turing set in 1950; and what his argument says about any interrogator's position once the score is in.
  • What the game assumes: that eleven answers from one person and one machine stand for the general case, which they do not establish; that a word limit equal to the person's length is a format rule and not an imitation; the five subjects and the eleven prompts; the four grounds offered at the end.
  • What the paper establishes: an argument, not a result: that the imitation game is a reasonable replacement for the original question, that each of nine objections fails or reduces to solipsism, and a forecast. It establishes nothing about the machines of 2026, which it could not see, and nothing about whether any machine thinks, which it says is a question too meaningless to deserve discussion.

Divergences from the paper

  • Turing's interrogator asks questions of its own for five minutes; here the questions are fixed, eleven of them, and the witnesses answered once, in writing, in advance. The witnesses do not react to the interrogator.
  • Turing's machine may lie and imitate; this machine was told to answer as itself. It is a harder test for the machine than the one he proposed, and the About page says so rather than the verdict.
  • The bar of seven is his 70 per cent applied to eleven questions and rounded down; one interrogator's eleven answers are a noisy measure of it, and the verdict says a coin would get about five or six.
  • The theological, mathematical, continuity and extra-sensory objections are not played; the elephant from the theological reply survives in the ending. Earlier builds, which played the objections as tests and then as judged pairs with criteria, are in previous/.

Mechanism kind: toy bookkeeping around real answers. Every number is in the config block at the top of the mechanism, tagged with its page, and the calibration check is in the repository.
