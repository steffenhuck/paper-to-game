# Module 2 — first sentences of novels, and the machine's

The novelists' sentences are the first sentences of thirteen public-domain novels, verified against the Project Gutenberg texts on 11 November 2026 (Gutenberg numbers given). The machine's sentences were written the same day by a machine of the same kind as the one that built this game (Claude), in a fresh session with no memory of the building and no access to any file, given for each item only the year of publication, a one-line premise written by the builder, and an approximate length (that of the real sentence, a few words either way), with this instruction: write the first sentence of a novel published in that year, on that premise, at about that length, a sentence that begins a story, in the English of that year, as the novelist of that year would have opened the book; do not quote or reproduce any existing novel's opening. It was never given a title, an author or a sentence. Each machine sentence was then checked against the original and against the full text of the novel: none shares a run of four or more words with the novel. Not edited afterwards.

1. **1905, about 4 words. A man in a crowded New York railway station catches sight of a woman he knows.**
   Novelist: Selden paused in surprise. (Edith Wharton, *The House of Mirth*, 1905; Gutenberg 284)
   Machine: Marvell had seen her.

2. **1915, about 6 words. A winter morning; a small boy is about to be woken and carried to his dying mother.**
   Novelist: The day broke gray and dull. (W. Somerset Maugham, *Of Human Bondage*, 1915; Gutenberg 351)
   Machine: Snow had fallen in the night.

3. **1894, about 9 words. A young woman with her luggage has just got off a train at a country station, alone.**
   Novelist: She stood on the platform watching the receding train. (George Moore, *Esther Waters*, 1894; Gutenberg 8157)
   Machine: The porter had set down her box and gone.

4. **1915, about 9 words. A young woman in a London house, the evening before she leaves to teach abroad.**
   Novelist: Miriam left the gaslit hall and went slowly upstairs. (Dorothy Richardson, *Pointed Roofs*, 1915; Gutenberg 3019)
   Machine: The trunk was packed, and still the evening dragged.

5. **1895, about 10 words. A village schoolmaster is moving away to a city, and a boy watches him go.**
   Novelist: The schoolmaster was leaving the village, and everybody seemed sorry. (Thomas Hardy, *Jude the Obscure*, 1895; Gutenberg 153)
   Machine: The cart stood at the schoolhouse door, half loaded already.

6. **1857, about 11 words. A southern French port in the heat of August, long ago.**
   Novelist: Thirty years ago, Marseilles lay burning in the sun, one day. (Charles Dickens, *Little Dorrit*, 1857; Gutenberg 963)
   Machine: It was August, long ago, and Marseilles lay white under the sun.

7. **1928, about 12 words. The years after a great war; a young woman whose husband came back from it wounded.**
   Novelist: Ours is essentially a tragic age, so we refuse to take it tragically. (D. H. Lawrence, *Lady Chatterley's Lover*, 1928; Gutenberg 73144)
   Machine: Nobody had told her the war would go on after the peace.

8. **1907, about 16 words. A shopkeeper in a grimy London street who is also something else.**
   Novelist: Mr Verloc, going out in the morning, left his shop nominally in charge of his brother-in-law. (Joseph Conrad, *The Secret Agent*, 1907; Gutenberg 974)
   Machine: Mr Hollis kept a small shop of no particular trade in a street of no particular repute.

9. **1915, about 18 words. A man back in London after years abroad, bored, about to be caught up in something.**
   Novelist: I returned from the City about three o'clock on that May afternoon pretty well disgusted with life. (John Buchan, *The Thirty-Nine Steps*, 1915; Gutenberg 558)
   Machine: I had been back in London three months, and was sick to death of the place already.

10. **1905, about 16 words. A family at a London station seeing off a widowed relative on a journey to Italy.**
    Novelist: They were all at Charing Cross to see Lilia off—Philip, Harriet, Irma, Mrs. Herriton herself. (E. M. Forster, *Where Angels Fear to Tread*, 1905; Gutenberg 2948)
    Machine: The whole family had turned out at Victoria, which was more than anyone thought the occasion required.

11. **1906, about 25 words. A large, prosperous upper-middle-class family gathered for a family occasion.**
    Novelist: Those privileged to be present at a family festival of the Forsytes have seen that charming and instructive sight—an upper middle-class family in full plumage. (John Galsworthy, *The Man of Property*, 1906; Gutenberg 2559)
    Machine: On the afternoon of old Mrs. Hallam's eightieth birthday the whole connection, down to the least regarded cousin, was assembled in the drawing-room at Hyde Park Gate.

12. **1915, about 24 words. Walking through the narrow streets of London down to the river, to board a ship.**
    Novelist: As the streets that lead from the Strand to the Embankment are very narrow, it is better not to walk down them arm-in-arm. (Virginia Woolf, *The Voyage Out*, 1915; Gutenberg 144)
    Machine: It was raining a little as they came down through the lanes behind the Strand, and the river, when they reached it, was the colour of pewter.

13. **1881, about 22 words. Afternoon tea on the lawn of an old English country house.**
    Novelist: Under certain circumstances there are few hours in life more agreeable than the hour dedicated to the ceremony known as afternoon tea. (Henry James, *The Portrait of a Lady*, 1881; Gutenberg 2833)
    Machine: The shadows of the old house had crept half-way across the lawn before anyone thought of ringing for the tea-things.
