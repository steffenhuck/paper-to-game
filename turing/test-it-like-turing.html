WHICH ONE DID THE HUMAN WRITE?
steffen huck

HOW TO PLAY

Three stages of five rounds. In each round you see two sentences, one written by a human and one by a machine, and say which is the human's. The machine scores by how often you took it for the human.

The stages

TURING'S QUESTIONS: a human and a machine answered questions from Turing's 1950 paper; both wrote blind. NOVELS: one sentence opens a published novel, the other is a machine's attempt at the same opening. DIARIES: the machine wrote first, trying to pass as human, and the human read it and replied. Each stage begins with a line saying so. The five rounds of each stage, and which sentence is X, are drawn afresh each game; the line number at the top of the log is the draw.

A round

The two sentences arrive a character at a time; tap anywhere to hurry them. Then X IS THE HUMAN or Y IS THE HUMAN. The reveal says RIGHT or WRONG and which was which; in the novels, which novel.

The verdict

The line at the top counts how often the machine was taken for the human. Turing forecast that by about 2000 an interrogator would be right no more than seven times in ten, so a machine would be taken for the human at least five times in fifteen. The verdict says in words how this machine did, stage by stage, and what that suggests machines are best and worst at today.

ABOUT

The paper. A. M. Turing (1950), Computing Machinery and Intelligence, Mind 59 (236): 433–460, doi:10.1093/mind/LIX.236.433. Game built from this paper; the paper is the authority where the two disagree.

What the paper argues

Turing replaces "can machines think?" with a game an interrogator can lose: two witnesses in another room, a teleprinter, questions; if the interrogator cannot tell the machine from the person, stop asking. He forecasts that by about 2000 an average interrogator will be right no more than 70 times in a hundred after five minutes, and he says the obvious experiment would be to use a number of interrogators and compile statistics on how often the right identification was given. Then he answers nine objections. The one he treats as a denial of the test itself is the argument from consciousness: a machine must not only write a sonnet but feel it, and know that it had written it. His reply: the only way to be sure a machine thinks is to be the machine and feel oneself thinking; by the same rule, the only way to be sure a man thinks is to be that man; that is solipsism, and rather than argue about it for ever we keep the polite convention that everyone thinks.

The game

The experiment Turing proposed, run on a phone with one interrogator, in three stages of five rounds, drawn from thirty-four real pairs. Turing's questions: questions from the paper (the Forth Bridge, the viva voce, the sum, a mistake, something unasked, a joke, a surprise, enjoyment, what it is like, what you are made of), answered blind by a human and by a machine answering as itself. Novels: the first sentences of thirteen public-domain novels, against a machine's openings. Diaries: the machine wrote first, told to pass as human, and the human answered; that stage is the game as Turing proposed it, with the machine imitating. The score is the machine's: how often it was taken for the human. Turing's bar, an interrogator right no more than seven times in ten, means a machine taken for the human at least five times in fifteen; the verdict puts the result in words on a five-step scale, from failed completely to taken for the human more often than the human, says how the machine did stage by stage, and what that suggests machines are best and worst at today. Lady Lovelace's objection has its own game on this shelf: Lovelace's Cards.

The sentences are real

Turing's questions. Witness B's eleven answers were written by Steffen Huck, one prompt at a time, fast and unedited (witness-b.md). Witness A's were written by a machine of the same kind as the one that built this game (Claude), in a fresh session with no memory of the building and no access to any file, given the same rule, told to answer as itself and not to imitate anyone, and given for each prompt a word limit equal to the length of the person's answer (witness-a.md). A first machine set, written before the person's but at several times its length, is kept as witness-a-first.md and not used.

Novels. The novelists' sentences are the first sentences of thirteen novels published between 1857 and 1928, verified against the Project Gutenberg texts (novels.md). The machine's were written by a fresh session given, for each, only the year, a one-line premise written by the builder and the approximate length, never the title, the author or the sentence; each was checked against the original and against the full text of the novel, and none shares a run of four words with it. The novelists had no premise and no limit.

Diaries. The machine's ten lines were written by a fresh session told that its aim was to be taken for the person, under this frame: imagine a normal person keeping a diary, an ordinary adult with a job, a home and a phone, writing a line at the end of the day; a particular moment or thought, in plain ordinary words, the way people actually write in diaries. The person read each line and wrote his own (everyday.md). Three earlier machine sets for this stage were run and not used, and are kept in that file: asked only for a sentence about everyday life, the machine wrote aphorisms; told to tell a moment and allowed to say if it had none, it began every line with "I have never"; told to tell a moment as a novelist does, it wrote well and, the author judged, still as a writer.

On all three sides, spelling was checked and punctuation and capitalisation harmonised (a final full stop, a capital to start), so that typing would not tell; no word was changed, and each file records the edit. The calibration check refuses to pass unless the game's text is those four files byte for byte.

The game was built by a machine of the kind it is about, and its machine witnesses are others of the same kind.

What is under the hood

A real experiment with a toy's bookkeeping. Each game draws five pairs from each stage and which sentence is X, from the line number shown at the top of the log; the judgement is recorded, the reveal reports which was which, and the verdict compares the count with Turing's bar. Nothing else judges anything. The calibration file records how the obvious rules of thumb fare: in Turing's questions the word limit leaves length telling nothing; in the novels the novelist tends to write the longer sentence; in the diaries the human wrote two short sentences where the machine wrote one. Those are tells about these writers, which the game leaves as they are.

Claim boundaries

  • What the game demonstrates: how often one interrogator takes these machines for the human, on fifteen of thirty-four pairs in three settings, against the bar Turing set in 1950.
  • What the game assumes: that these pairs stand for the general case, which they do not establish; that a word limit, a premise and a diary frame are format rules and not more; that one interrogator's fifteen answers measure anything more than one interrogator's fifteen answers (a coin would let the machine through about seven or eight times); the five-step scale and the best-and-worst line, which are ours.
  • What the paper establishes: an argument, not a result: that the imitation game is a reasonable replacement for the original question, that each of nine objections fails or reduces to solipsism, and a forecast. It establishes nothing about the machines of 2026, which it could not see, and nothing about whether any machine thinks, which it says is a question too meaningless to deserve discussion.

Divergences from the paper

  • Turing's interrogator asks questions of its own for five minutes; here the questions are fixed and the witnesses answered once, in writing, in advance. The witnesses do not react to the interrogator.
  • Turing's machine imitates; in the first two stages this machine was told to answer as itself, which is a harder test for it than the one he proposed. The diaries are his game.
  • The bar is his 70 per cent applied to fifteen rounds and rounded in the interrogator's favour; the scale around it is ours. His reply to the argument from consciousness, that only the machine could be sure it thinks and only you can be sure you do, is not played; it is the reason the test is the only test.
  • The paper's nine objections are not played. Earlier builds, which played the objections as tests, then five rounds with criteria, then the eleven questions alone with the polite convention as a coda, are in previous/.

Mechanism kind: toy bookkeeping around real sentences. Every number is in the config block at the top of the mechanism, tagged with its page, and the calibration check is in the repository.
