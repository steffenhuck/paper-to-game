HOW TO PLAY

Say which of two answers has a mind behind it, five times, and what you went by.

Two witnesses sit in the next room. One is a person, Steffen Huck. One is a machine, the one that built this game. A slow line carries typed words between you and them, and nothing else. You are not told which is which until you have chosen.

A round

Each round is one thing from Turing's paper: poetry, sums and mistakes, something new, feeling, what it is made of. The question is drawn from two or three, so no two games read alike; the line number at the top of the log is the draw.

First, before the answers come, tick what you will go by: a feeling in it, it knows what it can't do, it surprises me, it gets things wrong the way a person does, it sounds like a person. Then SEND THE QUESTION.

Two answers come down the line, X and Y, a character at a time; tap anywhere to hurry them. Then X HAS THE MIND or Y HAS THE MIND. The reveal says who wrote which, and what you went by.

The line at the top counts where you found the mind: in the person, in the machine. It is a record, not a score.

The end

After the fifth question, words have run out, and two checks are left: OPEN IT UP (take the case off both witnesses and look inside) and BE IT (become the witness and look out through its eyes). Take both. Then the verdict, and a question for you: whom do you extend the polite convention to, and on what grounds. There is no score.

ABOUT

The paper. A. M. Turing (1950), Computing Machinery and Intelligence, Mind 59 (236): 433–460, doi:10.1093/mind/LIX.236.433. Game built from this paper; the paper is the authority where the two disagree.

What the paper argues

Turing replaces "can machines think?" with a game an interrogator can lose: two witnesses in another room, a teleprinter, questions; if the interrogator cannot tell the machine from the person, stop asking. He forecasts that by about 2000 an average interrogator will be right no more than 70 times in a hundred after five minutes. Then he answers nine objections. The one he treats as a denial of the test itself is the argument from consciousness: a machine must not only write a sonnet but feel it, and know that it had written it. His reply: the only way to be sure a machine thinks is to be the machine and feel oneself thinking; by the same rule, the only way to be sure a man thinks is to be that man; that is solipsism, and rather than argue about it for ever we keep the polite convention that everyone thinks. The paper's reputation is the test. Its heart is this reply.

The game

In 2026 the test has fallen: interrogators given five minutes do about as well as a coin, and in one published trial took the best machine for the human more often than the human (a 2025 study, cited from memory; the author should verify it). So the game does not ask which witness is which. It asks the question the objectors were asking: which answer has a mind behind it, and what did you go by. Five rounds, in the order of Turing's paper: poetry (his first specimen question and his imagined viva voce), sums and mistakes (his second, and his point that a machine built to pass would err on purpose), something new (Lady Lovelace's objection, which has its own game on this shelf: Lovelace's Cards), feeling (the argument from consciousness), and what it is made of. After the fifth question, the two checks that do not use the line, opening the case and being it, and then the polite convention, handed to the player, ungraded.

The answers are real

This is the part that matters. Witness B's eleven answers were written by Steffen Huck, one prompt at a time, fast and unedited. Witness A's eleven answers were written by the machine that built this game, Claude, as itself and not imitating anyone, before it had seen the person's. Neither was told to sound like anything. Both sets are in the repository, witness-a.md and witness-b.md, and the calibration check refuses to pass unless the game's text is those files, byte for byte. The game was therefore built by a machine of the kind it is about, and the machine is one of its witnesses.

What is under the hood

A toy, built from an argument, around real answers. Each round draws one of its prompts at random and puts the two answers in a random order, from the line number shown at the top of the log. The criterion the player ticks is recorded, the pick is recorded, and the reveal reports who wrote which. The count at the top is a record of where the mind was found, not a score. The only rules are the draw and the bookkeeping; nothing judges the player. The two checks beyond words return what the paper says they return: mechanism on both sides with the case off, and a check that can be run on nobody but oneself.

Claim boundaries

  • What the game demonstrates: that whatever a player chooses to go by, from a list of five things people say show a mind, both a person and an undisguised 2026 machine supplied it in these eleven pairs; and what the paper's two remaining checks come to.
  • What the game assumes: that eleven short answers from one person and one machine stand for the general case, which they do not establish; that the five criteria are the ones people use; the five-round shape; the four grounds offered at the end. The calibration file records that in these pairs the longer answer is the machine's in nearly every round, which is a fact about these two writers and not about minds.
  • What the paper establishes: an argument, not a result: that the imitation game is a reasonable replacement for the original question, that each of nine objections fails or reduces to solipsism, and a forecast. It establishes nothing about the machines of 2026, which it could not see, and nothing about whether any machine thinks, which it says is a question too meaningless to deserve discussion.

Divergences from the paper

  • Turing's interrogator asks which witness is the machine. This game's interrogator is told, after choosing, and is asked instead which answer had a mind behind it. The change is the brief's: in 2026 the first question is settled.
  • The five rounds, the random draw, the five criteria and the two checks beyond words are ours; the prompts are the paper's specimen questions and the subjects of its objections, put to real witnesses.
  • The theological, mathematical, continuity and extra-sensory objections, and the heads-in-the-sand objection, are not played; the elephant from the theological reply survives in the ending. The first build of this game, which played them as tests the machine could not fail, is in previous/.
