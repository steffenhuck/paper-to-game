ABOUT

The paper. A. M. Turing (1950), Computing Machinery and Intelligence, Mind 59 (236): 433–460, doi:10.1093/mind/LIX.236.433. Game built from this paper; the paper is the authority where the two disagree.

What the paper argues

Turing replaces "can machines think?" with a game an interrogator can lose: two witnesses in another room, a teleprinter, questions; if the interrogator cannot tell the machine from the person, stop asking. He forecasts that by about 2000 an average interrogator will be right no more than 70 times in a hundred after five minutes. Then he answers nine objections. The one he treats as a denial of the test itself is the argument from consciousness: a machine must not only write a sonnet but know that it had written it, and feel. His reply: the only way to be sure a machine thinks is to be the machine and feel oneself thinking; by the same rule, the only way to be sure a man thinks is to be that man; that is solipsism, and rather than argue about it for ever we keep the polite convention that everyone thinks. The paper's reputation is the test. Its heart is this reply.

The game

You are the interrogator, after the old test has fallen. Fifteen demands, all from the paper (its specimen questions, its imagined viva, its list of things a machine was said never to do, and one demand each from the theological, mathematical, Lovelace, continuity and extra-sensory objections). Three checks, which are the three positions the paper takes in prose: the chair, the case off, and being it. Six tests, then the convention is yours.

Lady Lovelace's objection is the demand "do something really new, and take you by surprise". Her Engine has its own game on this shelf: Lovelace's Cards.

What is under the hood

A toy, built from an argument. A test is a demand and a check. Every demand checked from the chair is a demand about behaviour, and witness A, a universal machine built in 2026, produces it, with the three exceptions that are the paper's own: a question written against one machine's own tables (which the next machine answers); a dish of strawberries (which finds a mouth, not a mind); and a demand to be made of nerves (which from the chair cannot be seen, and with the case off finds a material). A demand about feeling, knowing or having, checked from the chair, returns two claims and no check. With the case off, every demand returns mechanism on both sides and the thing asked for on neither. Being it cannot be run on anyone but you. The counter of separations that hold never moves; the calibration file checks all forty-five cells and three hundred random games. The only random element is the card-reading run, drawn at chance from the line number shown at the top of the log.

Claim boundaries

  • What the game demonstrates: that under this game's assumptions the paper's reply sorts every proposed test into one of two bins, runnable (and passed, or passed next) or not runnable (on a machine or on a person), with no third bin.
  • What the game assumes: the premise, taken from the builder's brief, that in 2026 machines pass the five-minute imitation game easily (the published evidence we know of is a 2025 study in which interrogators took the best machine for the human more often than the human; this is cited from memory and the author should verify it); that the transcripts stand for what a 2026 machine does, which is our writing; the six-test budget; the two "holds" rules, which combine two of the paper's own conditions into one counter; the four grounds offered at the end.
  • What the paper establishes: an argument, not a result: that the imitation game is a reasonable replacement for the original question, that each of nine objections fails or reduces to solipsism, and a forecast. It establishes nothing about the machines of 2026, which it could not see, and nothing about whether any machine thinks, which it says is a question too meaningless to deserve discussion.

Divergences from the paper

  • The three checks are our names for positions the paper takes in prose; it never lines them up as a menu.
  • Witness B's answers are ours except where the paper supplies them (the sonnet declined, the sum, the chess, the viva voce). Witness A's answers are ours, written to the 2026 premise; the chess and the first sum are the paper's own specimen answers, including the specimen answer to the sum, which is wrong by a hundred, as the paper's section on mistakes allows.
  • The card-reading run is forty cards, not four hundred, and nobody in it is telepathic.
  • The heads-in-the-sand objection and the argument from informality of behaviour are not played. Section 3 to 5 (universality) is the premise that A is a universal machine. Section 7 (learning machines) appears only in the demand to learn and in what the case-off check finds for pleasure.

Mechanism kind: toy. Every number is in the config block at the top of the mechanism, tagged with its page, and the calibration check is in the repository.
