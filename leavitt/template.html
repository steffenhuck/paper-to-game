Leavitt’s Cloud

The Small Magellanic Cloud, on glass

Night after night, the observatory photographed a far-off cloud of stars on glass plates. Some of its stars blink. You will look at the plates, turn a dial until one star’s blinks line up, and put it on a chart. Then the next star.

Leavitt’s Cloud

How to play

The plates

Each glass plate is a photograph of a far-off cloud of stars on one night. On the glass a star is a black dot: the brighter the star that night, the bigger the dot. The star you are timing has a thin black ring round it.

Every plate becomes a dot in the box below the glass: its night across, how bright the star was up. By date, the dots jump about, because the nights are uneven.

The dial

The beat is how long the star takes to blink once: a quick climb to bright, then a long slow fade. Turn the dial to try a beat. The dots are then laid out by where each night falls in that beat, two beats side by side. At the right beat each beat holds one blink and the dots make the same shape twice. At a wrong beat they scatter. The top dial goes from 1 day to 128 days; the fine dial below nudges a little shorter or a little longer.

At twice the right beat the dots line up too, but each beat holds two blinks. Look for one blink per beat.

Write it down

When you are happy, press Write it down. Only then does Miss Leavitt’s beat for that star appear, in violet, with her blink drawn over your dots.

The chart

Each star goes on the chart as a stroke from its faintest to its brightest, at the beat you wrote. Across: the beat, each step a doubling. Up: brighter. Look at what the strokes do.

The last star

For the seventh star you first set a beat from how bright it is, before its plates come in. A dashed stroke shows where your beat would put it on the chart. Then its plates arrive and you time it as before, starting from your setting.

Violet

Everything in violet is Miss Leavitt’s. It appears only after you have written yours.
