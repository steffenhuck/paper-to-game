Harvard, 1912

Blink with Leavitt

A far-away cloud of stars, on glass

In 1912, Henrietta Leavitt at Harvard looked at photos of a far-away cloud of stars, taken on glass night after night. Some of the stars blink: they get bright, fade, and get bright again, over and over.

You will look at her photos, turn a dial to find how long each star takes to blink, and put the star on a chart. Then you look at the chart. Is there a pattern?

How to play

The photos

Each glass photo shows a far-away cloud of stars on one night. Every dot is a star. Small and pale pink: a faint star. Big and dark: a bright star. The key in the corner shows the scale. The star in the big ring is the one you are timing. Stars you have already timed keep a small ring, with the blink time you found, so you can compare their dots with the new one.

Each photo becomes a dot in the box below it, the same size as the star was that night. Further right means a later night. Higher (and bigger) means the star was brighter that night. At first the dots look messy, because the photos were taken on random nights.

The dial

The blink time is how long the star takes to blink once: a quick jump up to bright, then a slow slide down. Move the dial to guess it. The dots jump to new places: now they show where each night fell inside one blink, and the box shows two blinks side by side.

A thin line joins the dots in order. Wrong guess: the line is a messy scribble. Right guess: it turns into a neat zigzag, a quick jump up and a slow slide down, twice. The top dial goes from 1 day to 128 days. The lower dial is for fine tuning.

Careful: if the zigzag jumps up twice in each half of the box, your guess is twice too long.

A good way to do it

Slide the top dial slowly from left to right. Nearly everywhere the line is a scribble; at a few spots it snaps into a tidy shape. Stop there and check: you want one hump in each half of the box, a quick rise and a slow slide. Two humps in each half means your time is twice too long, so halve it. Then use the fine dial until the dots hug the line as tightly as they can, with no stray dots. The tightest picture is the answer.

Once the chart shows a pattern, use it: a big dark star means start far to the right, a small pale one far to the left.

Write it down

When the dots line up, press Write it down. Then you see Leavitt’s own blink time for that star, in violet, and her blink drawn over your dots.

The chart

Each star goes on the chart as a line from its faintest to its brightest, in the same colours as on the glass. Left to right is the blink time you wrote: slow blinkers on the right. Each step to the right doubles the time. Up is brighter. Watch what the lines do.

The last star

For the seventh star you guess its blink time from its brightness, before its photos arrive. The glass shows all seven stars at their brightest in a row, faintest to brightest, with the blink times you found. The new one sits between two of them: guess a time between theirs. A dashed line shows where your guess would put it on the chart. Then its photos come in and you time it as before, starting from your guess.

Violet

Everything in violet is Leavitt’s. You only see it after you have written down yours.
