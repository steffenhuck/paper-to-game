# Leavitt's Cloud — design memo

## 0. Given

Mine: Steffen's signature and counter, no beta line. His ideas, binding:

- **It is Leavitt, not Pickering.** The circular is signed by Edward C. Pickering, the director, but its first sentence says the statement "has been prepared by Miss Leavitt". The game credits her throughout, and, although these games normally carry no biography, the debrief gets a short mention of who signed and who did the work.
- **Simple and sparse.** One instrument, one chart, few screens, few words.
- **Straight, but subtly feminine** in the look. Read here as: no ornament, no pastiche, no pink; a quiet palette of lavender glass, aubergine and violet ink, a fine serif, rounded buttons.

Precedent is not overridden by any of this.

## 1. Paper

Leavitt, H. S. (1912), "Periods of 25 variable stars in the Small Magellanic Cloud", *Harvard College Observatory Circular* 173: 1–3 (signed Edward C. Pickering, 3 March 1912). Bibcode 1912HarCi.173....1L. Read online via the NASA ADS: https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1912HarCi.173....1L. Available: the ADS scan only (3 pages). No transcription was found, so Table I was read from the scan by eye and every number in the config block is tagged as read off the scan; the calibration check re-derives the paper's own residual columns from it, which catches a misread digit.

## 2. What the paper hands over

**Primary: an estimate.** Table I: for 25 variable stars, the brightness at maximum and minimum (photographic magnitudes, provisional scale), an epoch and a period in days, and the residuals from two straight lines. Figure 2: magnitude against the logarithm of the period, two straight lines, "the logarithm of the period increases by about 0.48 for each increase of one magnitude in brightness". **Secondary: a short argument.** The stars are "probably at nearly the same distance from the Earth", so their periods go with their real output of light. Plus a verbal description of the light curve: a slow fade, a long time near minimum, a very rapid rise to a brief maximum, a range of about 1.2 magnitudes for bright and faint alike.

## 3. The discovery, and its kind

**A pattern found in hard-won measurements** (emergence in the data, with a dash of identification): the brighter a variable in the Cloud, the slower it beats, and on the right scale the relation is a straight line. Why it matters is the identification step in one sentence: because all the stars sit in one cloud, about equally far away, how bright they look is how bright they are. The famous consequence (a star's beat gives its true brightness, so its faintness gives its distance) is *not* claimed in the paper; it is hoped for ("the parallaxes of some variables of this type may be measured"). The game plays the pattern, states the reason, and reads the consequence on the verdict as what came after.

## 4. The core narrative

Far away there is a cloud of stars. Some of them blink: they get bright, then slowly dim, then flash bright again, over and over, each at its own pace. A woman at Harvard looked at hundreds of photographs of that cloud, night after night, and worked out how long each blinking star took to come round again. Then she noticed something nobody expected: the bright ones blinked slowly and the faint ones blinked fast, every time. And since all of them are in the same cloud, the same distance away, that meant the slow ones really do give out more light. A star's blink tells you how bright it truly is.

## 5. Who lives in the paper

- **The plate reader** (Leavitt): estimates each star's brightness on each glass plate against comparison stars, then finds the period from irregular dates. Controls: the period she tries. Anticipates: where the next maximum falls.
- **The star**: pulses; its brightness and period are fixed by its nature ("mass, density and surface brightness"). Controls nothing the paper names.
- **The chart maker**: chooses the axes; Figure 1 (period in days) gives two curves, Figure 2 (log period) two straight lines.
- **The observatory** (Pickering's circular): decides what to publish and what to hope for (spectra, parallaxes, the remaining two thousand).

## 7. The aim and the verdict

**Aim (the sentence the player reads):** *Find how long each star in the Cloud takes to blink, and see what the blinks have in common.*

**Verdict, the lines the end screen leads with:**
1. What you did: *You timed six stars of the Cloud by their blink, and a seventh from its light alone.* (Then, in words, how near your beats were to hers.)
2. What the paper says: *Miss Leavitt's twenty-five fall on a straight line. The brighter the star, the slower its blink: a blink about three times as long goes with about two and a half times the light.*
3. What it means: *They are all in one cloud, about equally far away, so this is how bright they really are. Time a star like this anywhere in the sky and you know its true brightness; how faint it looks then says how far away it is. The circular does not claim that. It hopes for it.*

## 8. Three concepts

**A. The Plate Reader** (experimenter; discovery with a prediction close). The player is Leavitt at the plates. Each round is one star: its plates arrive (black dots on glass, bigger is brighter, each on its date), and the player tunes a beat dial until the scattered dots line up into one tooth per beat. They write the beat down; her number and her curve appear in violet under theirs; the star drops onto a chart as a stroke from its faintest to its brightest. Controls: a beat dial (coarse and fine). Before pressing they are thinking: are these dots lining up, is this one tooth or two, and later, *this star is bright, so where should I start looking?* After: the fold snaps, the ghost agrees or not, the chart grows. Moment: by the third star the chart has a slope; the player starts using it to find beats faster; the seventh star is timed from its light alone. Fun: discovery, mastery (the dial gets easier once the law is known), anticipation (the snap). Replay: new plates, a different order of discovery, the prediction tried with the law in mind.

**B. The Cloud Keeper** (controller). The player sets a star's size and watches its blink and light; aim: populate the Cloud to match the plates. Fails: the paper has no model of the star, so the controller would be turning a dial that *is* the law; there is nothing to discover, only to confirm.

**C. The Heartbeat** — the reckless one (subject; real time; no words on the play screen). The player is the photographic plate: stars blink in a time-lapse and the player taps along with each, a rhythm game; the chart builds from their taps. Moment: the big ones are slow, the small ones flutter. Fails on the walkthrough: the paper's periods span a factor of a hundred (1.25 to 127 days), so at any single time-lapse speed the fastest flicker at eight times a second while the slowest take half a minute; changing speed per star hides the very comparison the game is about.

## 10. The pick, and why

**A, The Plate Reader.** The player does what the paper did, with the paper's own difficulty (irregular plates, an unknown beat), and finds the paper's pattern with their own hands; then the pattern turns into a tool, which is the strongest sign that it was discovered and not told. The sentence afterwards has no "guess": *I timed the blinking stars, the bright ones were the slow ones, and I timed the last one from its light.* Originality: no game in the table has had the player tune a hidden period by folding data, a tactile, radio-dial sort of act.

**Author test.** An astronomer knows the answer and still has to find the beat; the snap of a fold is satisfying however often one has seen it. The alias (a beat twice hers gives two teeth) is the real trap of the trade and stays in.

**Runner-up: C, The Heartbeat.** Switch if the author wants a thirty-second wordless piece for a talk and accepts a time-lapse whose speed changes between stars, with a dial that says so.

**What the reckless concept would have given.** Rhythm in the body: feeling the big stars heave and the small ones flutter. Kept: the ringed star on the plate pulses at its own pace, all the time, in a fixed time-lapse, so the bright ones visibly breathe slower. Given up: tapping, and a screen without words.

## 11. The shape

Seven rounds, one long session, no score.

1. **Harvard 1425** (4.547 d, mid-bright) — first reading watched: the plates arrive one at a time with a caption.
2. **Harvard 1505** (1.25336 d, the faintest at maximum) — fast; teeth visible already in the date view.
3. **Harvard 823** (31.94 d, bright) — far right; with three strokes the slope is visible.
4. **Harvard 1374** (8.397 d) — lands between.
5. **Harvard 821** (127.0 d, the brightest) — the far end.
6. **Harvard 1436** (1.6637 d, faint) — found fast by a player who has the law.
7. **Harvard 824** (65.8 d, second brightest) — the prediction close: set the beat from its light, then the plates fold at the guess, then tune and write.

Then: her other eighteen in violet ink, her two lines, the Figure 1 / Figure 2 switch, the verdict. Order reason: the first three span the range so the discovery arrives by about the third star; four to six reward using it. Presets are the paper's own stars; nothing is invented except the plates.

## 12. Controls, mechanism, feedback, moment

- **Controls.** The beat dial: a coarse slider from 1 to 128 days, doubling ticks evenly spaced (1, 2, 4 ... 128), and a fine slider of ten per cent either way, reset to the centre when the coarse one moves. Every round starts with the dial at 8 days. WRITE IT DOWN commits. A small *by date / by beat* switch on the record panel.
- **Mechanism (reconstructed).** For each played star, 22 plates on irregular dates spanning four to six of its cycles, brightness from a light curve shaped as the paper describes (a rise over the last sixth of the cycle, a slow fade that lingers near minimum) between Table I's maximum and minimum, timed from Table I's epoch, plus eye-estimate noise of about a tenth of a magnitude. Folding is phase = ((t − epoch)/P) mod 1, the record shown over two beats.
- **Feedback.** The fold (dots line up or smear as the dial moves); the ghost (her curve in violet over the player's dots after commitment); the drop (a stroke on the chart, faintest to brightest, at the player's beat; her mark in violet if it differs).
- **Moment.** Round 3: the bright star lands high and right and the three strokes form a slope. Round 5: the player drags straight to the far end because the star is huge. Round 7: the player places the star on the line before any plate is seen.
- **The game's voice after each write:** what you wrote, what she wrote, and in words whether yours is hers, a multiple of hers (two or three teeth per beat), or neither.

## 13. The look

**Register: the plate on the light box** (Austere family: a scientific plate, a museum label). From the paper's setting: the Harvard plate stacks, where the Cloud exists only as glass negatives, stars as black dots on clear glass, read on an illuminated stand in a dim room, numbers written in ink. The game's whole world is that glass, lit, in a dark room.

- **Palette:** *velvet* `#1F1822` (the dark room, the page ground); *glass* `#E9E6EE` (every lit plate and chart, lavender-white); *silver* `#17141B` (the emulsion: star dots, the player's dots and strokes, type on glass); *violet ink* `#6A3FA0` (Miss Leavitt's hand: her numbers, her curve, her marks, the ring round the star; on velvet the lighter `#B49BE0`); *lilac* `#A99CBD` (hairlines, grids, secondary text).

- **The one visual rule:** *the sky is black on glass, her hand is violet, and nothing appears in violet until you have written yours.*
- **Play screen at 380 px.** The aim, one line in italic, with ? at the right. A small capitals line: STAR 2 OF 7 · HARVARD 1505. **The plate** (348 × 120, glass): the Cloud's crowd of fine specks, the star ringed in violet and pulsing at its own pace in a fixed time-lapse; answers "which star, and how bright is it?". **The record** (348 × 210, glass): dots on a ruled field, brighter up, by date or by beat, two beats separated by a hairline; answers "is it lining up?". **The dial**: the beat written large in Spectral (*4 days 13 hours*), the coarse slider with doubling ticks, the fine slider; answers "what beat am I trying?". ## 14. What is cut

The comparison-star sequences and the magnitude scale; the provisional-scale caveat (on About); the epochs as numbers; the eighteen stars not played (they arrive in her ink); the minima line as something the player fits (drawn by her); UY Cygni, spectra and the "two fundamental questions" (one line on About); the 1777-star catalogue; all magnitudes as numbers (brightness is a dot size and a height).

## 15. Divergences

- **The plates are ours.** The paper prints no plate-by-plate estimates; the game draws 22 per star from Table I's maximum, minimum, epoch and period with a light-curve shape from the paper's words. Her plates spanned years and many hundreds of cycles; ours span four to six cycles so a period can be found by eye with a thumb.
- **No magnitudes on screen.** Brightness is shown as dot size and height; direction kept (brighter up), the 0.48 slope said in words (three times the blink, two and a half times the light).
- **Brightness stroke at the player's beat.** If the player writes a wrong beat, the stroke goes where they put it, with her mark beside it, so the chart is honest about their reading.
- **Time-lapse on the plate.** The pulsing ring uses one fixed rate for all stars (about two seconds a day, capped so the slowest still visibly moves), so the comparison of paces is true within the screen.

## Mechanism kind and claim boundaries

**Reconstructed.** The game runs a constructed plate-generating process reproducing Table I's periods, maxima, minima and epochs and the light-curve shape the paper describes.

- **Demonstrates:** that from irregular plates a period can be found by folding, and that the 25 stars of Table I, set by period and brightness, fall on the paper's straight lines.
- **Assumes:** the individual plates, their dates and errors, and the exact light-curve shape; the time-lapse.
- **The paper establishes:** the 25 periods and brightness ranges, and the straight-line relation between brightness and the logarithm of the period, about 0.48 per magnitude, on a provisional magnitude scale. It argues, but does not show, that the stars are at nearly one distance. It does not measure any distance.

**The discovery comes from the paper, not from the rules.** The chart is built from Table I's brightness and the beats the player writes; the beats are recoverable because the plates are drawn at her periods. No scoring, objective or constraint of ours produces the slope. The prediction round's yardstick (how near the guess was) is ours and is said in words only.

## Calibration plan

1. Table I as transcribed: the least-squares slope of maximum and of minimum magnitude on log period lies within ±0.25 of 1/0.48 ≈ 2.08 magnitudes per decade; the circular's "about 0.48" is recovered as 0.40–0.60.
2. Her residual columns: with lines of the fitted slopes, residuals recomputed from the transcription match the printed Res. M and Res. m to within 0.25 for every star (a check on misread digits); report the worst.
3. Average range (min − max) is 1.2 ± 0.15 magnitudes.
4. Plates: over 300 seeds per played star, folding at her period gives the lowest dispersion of a grid from 1 to 128 days, apart from integer multiples; the fold at half her period is visibly worse; the fold at her period ±3% is still recognisable for the declared span (dispersion below a threshold).
5. Robustness of the discovery to our choices: with a symmetric (sine) light curve and with noise doubled, the true period is still the best non-multiple fold for every played star.
6. The words: the day-and-hour formatter gives no decimals; the comparison words (hers, twice hers, three times, half, near, off) classify a battery of beats correctly.

- **The verdict is four headed parts**, computed from the player's own seven: *What she measured and found* (fixed); *What you measured and found* (how many were hers, how many too long/too short, what the player's own chart shows, the last-star guess); *Where you and she part*; *If your numbers had stood*. The what-if is labelled as the game's, not the paper's. The final chart draws a dashed line through the player's seven whenever they are not hers.
