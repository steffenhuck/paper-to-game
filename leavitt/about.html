Leavitt’s Cloud

About

Built from Henrietta Swan Leavitt, “Periods of 25 variable stars in the Small Magellanic Cloud”, Harvard College Observatory Circular 173 (1912), 1–3, signed by Edward C. Pickering, 3 March 1912. Bibcode 1912HarCi.173....1L. Read it at the NASA Astrophysics Data System. Game built from this paper; the paper is the authority where the two disagree.

What the paper found

Leavitt had a catalogue of 1,777 variable stars in the two Magellanic Clouds, all faint, on a limited stock of long-exposure plates. For 25 in the Small Cloud she found the period. Set side by side, the brighter ones had the longer periods; plotted against the logarithm of the period, the stars at their brightest fell on one straight line and at their faintest on another, the logarithm rising by about 0.48 for each magnitude. Since the stars are probably all at nearly the same distance, she wrote, their periods go with how much light they really give out. She hoped for spectra and for the parallaxes of some such stars, and for a study of all the variables in the Clouds.

What the game does

You time seven of her twenty-five from glass plates, by folding the plates on a trial beat until they line up; each goes on a chart as a stroke from its faintest to its brightest at the beat you wrote. The seventh you first set from its light alone. Then her other eighteen and her two lines appear. The chart can be spaced by doublings of the beat, as her Figure 2 (two straight lines), or by days, as her Figure 1 (two curves).

The mechanism, and what it claims

Kind: reconstructed. The game runs a constructed set of plates that reproduces Table I’s periods, brightest and faintest magnitudes and epochs, with a light curve shaped as the circular describes it (a slow fade, a long time near the faintest, a very quick rise to a brief maximum).

The game demonstrates that a period can be found from uneven plates by folding them, and that her twenty-five stars, set by period and brightness, fall on her straight lines.

The game assumes the plates themselves: their dates, how many (26 a star, covering four to six blinks; hers covered years and many hundreds), the estimating error (about a tenth of a magnitude), and the exact shape of the light curve. It also assumes the fixed time-lapse at which the ringed star blinks on the glass.

The paper establishes the 25 periods and brightness ranges, and the straight-line relation between brightness and the logarithm of the period, on a provisional scale of magnitudes. It argues, but does not show, that the stars are at nearly one distance, and it measures no distance. Using the relation to measure distances came later, once the brightness of a few such stars nearby could be fixed.

The numbers

Table I was read by eye off the scan; no transcription was found. A check recomputes the printed residual columns from it and agrees for every star but one: for Harvard 1374 the faintest-magnitude residual is printed as −0.3 where its own numbers give +0.3, a slip in the 1912 table. The game uses the magnitudes as printed. No magnitude appears on screen: brightness is a dot’s size and a stroke’s height, and the 0.48 is said in words (a blink about three times as long, about two and a half times the light).

What is left out

The comparison stars and the magnitude scale; the epochs; the light curves of brighter stars such as UY Cygni, and the questions about spectra and the limits of mass the circular raises; the other two thousand variables.
