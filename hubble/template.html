Something is in the way. The Sun itself moves through space at about 300 km/s, toward the north. Nebulae ahead of us look as if they come toward us; those behind look faster than they are. Hubble solved for the Sun's motion in the same step as the rate.

Hubble's distances were about seven times too short, we now know, and today's rate is about 70 km/s for every million parsecs. The relation itself held. He wrote that it "may represent the de Sitter effect", and left the obvious consequences for later.

How to play

Find out whether the far nebulae drift at random or whether their speed is tied to their distance. Twelve nights. Then say how fast.

The sky. The smudges are nebulae, the faint far-off ones beyond our own stars. Tap one, and the card below tells you its name and what it costs: bright ones one night, faint ones two or three. You have twelve nights, the lamps along the top. Then tap Point the telescope.

The eyepiece. Through the eyepiece the nebula breaks into stars; its brightest is ringed in amber. The brightest star of any such nebula is about as bright as any other's, in truth: it looks faint only because it is far. On the right half of the field sits a comparison star just like it, which the dial brings nearer (it brightens and grows) or pushes farther away (it fades and shrinks). Put the comparison star where it looks as bright as the nebula's brightest star, by your own eye; the readout says how far away you have put it, and that is your reading of the nebula's distance. Tap Read the distance.

The spectrograph. A sandwich of three strips. Above and below, the light of a calcium lamp in the laboratory, with its two amber lines: the lamp does not move, so that is where calcium's lines sit at rest. Between them, the nebula's light, with the same two calcium lines as dark bars. If the nebula is moving away, its bars sit to the right of the amber lines (redder); if it is coming toward us, to the left. Turn the measuring screw: the two crosshairs start on the amber lines and travel across; the readout is how far they have travelled, as a speed. Put the crosshairs on the dark bars, by your own eye, and tap Read the speed.

The plate. Each nebula becomes a dot: distance across, speed up. Red is going away, blue is coming toward us. After your dot lands, a faint hollow dot shows where Hubble's own table puts that nebula. Watch the shape the dots make, and choose your next nebula accordingly.

The Sun. When the nights are over, one tap takes out the Sun's own motion through space, which Hubble worked out at the same time. The dots move.

The rate. Swing a line through your dots and commit. Then say how fast a nebula far beyond your plate is going, and watch its spectrum come in.
