    How to play

    Bring the boy to a square twice this size, by asking, not telling, before Meno has to go.

    The floor. A grey-green square is laid in it, two tiles each way: four tiles. A square twice its size holds eight tiles. Glowing pegs mark where a line from the square's corner can end: along the floor, or from corner to corner across the tiles. Tap a peg to lay a line to it; the square on that line shows faintly. Tap it again to lift it.

    Your answer. Before the lesson you tap the line you would choose yourself. Nobody acts on it.

    The lesson. You have Meno's five questions. Each button uses one.

    • Ask him: which line? (no line laid): he lays the line he believes in, in white, and counts the square on it.
    • Ask him: how big is the square on this line? (a line laid): if he still thinks he knows, he ignores your line and tests his own; once he has said "I do not know", he counts the square on yours.
    • Tell him: it's this line. He says yes. He does not count it.

    The count. He lays a white tile on every tile of the square, then the halves, two halves to a tile. The column at the right of the floor stacks what he counted against two marks: four (the first square) and eight (twice). Tiles past eight are red.

    Colours. White is his own: what he lays, counts and says. Ochre is yours. Grey-green was given.

    His statue. Beside him stands a small statue of what he believes. A count that proves him wrong knocks it over. A line he counts himself raises a white statue, tied down. A line you tell him raises an ochre one, not tied.

    The end of the lesson. When he has a line for eight, by his count or by your word, or when Meno has to go.

    Next morning. Meno gives him a square of three tiles each way, nine, and asks for its double. You only watch. Then the end screen says where he stands, on four steps: sure and wrong; stuck; right, but told; right, and found.

    About this game

    The paper. Plato, Meno, written around 385 BCE, in Benjamin Jowett's translation. Read it at classics.mit.edu/Plato/meno.html; the scene with the boy is 82b–86c in the usual numbering. An ancient text has no DOI. Game built from this paper; the paper is the authority where the two disagree.

    What happens in it. Meno, a rich young man from Thessaly, asks Socrates whether goodness can be taught. Socrates says he does not even know what goodness is. Meno's answers fail one after another, and Meno objects that nobody can look for something they do not know: how would they recognise it? Socrates answers with a demonstration. He calls over a slave boy of Meno's household who has never been taught geometry, draws a square of two feet each way, four square feet, and asks for the side of a square twice the size. The boy says double the side; it gives sixteen. He says three; nine. He says "I do not know", and Socrates says he is better off for it, because now he will look. Then, by drawing and asking, Socrates brings him to the square on the line from corner to corner: eight. "Were not all these answers given out of his own head?" Meno agrees. At the end of the dialogue Socrates says why knowledge is worth more than a true opinion: true opinions are as good a guide while they stay, but they run away, like the walking statues of Daedalus, unless they are tied down by working out the reason. That tying, he says, is recollection.

    The game. You are Socrates at the floor, with the boy and five questions before Meno has to leave. You can ask him which line makes the double square, lay a line from the corner and ask how big the square on it is, or tell him the line. He follows four rules: he counts truly, whole tiles and halves; while he thinks he knows, he tests only his own line; once stuck, he will count the square on any line you lay; and he agrees with whatever you tell him. The next morning Meno gives him a square of three and asks for its double.

    Mechanism: a toy. The game runs a small model of the boy built to illustrate the dialogue's argument.

    • It demonstrates that, under this game's rules, a boy who is told an answer agrees and has not learned it, a boy who still thinks he knows does not look at a better line, and a boy who counts the answer himself can find it again in a new square. The geometry is exact: the doubled side gives four times the square, three gives nine, the line from corner to corner gives eight, made of four whole tiles and eight halves.
    • It assumes the four rules, the five questions, the next morning and what each kind of boy does on it. These are ours. Plato says the boy would not have looked while he thought he knew (the torpedo passage), that true opinions run away until tied by the reason, and that the boy would know if asked again in different forms; he does not run the next morning, and he does not say what a told boy would do.
    • Plato establishes an argument, not a measurement: one demonstration on one boy, offered as evidence that learning is recollection. Readers since have argued over how much Socrates led him.

    What the game changes. In the dialogue Socrates draws every line, the boy's wrong squares included; here the boy lays his own guesses, so that white can mean his. Socrates never tells the boy the answer; the game lets you, to show what telling does. The dialogue counts the tilted square as four halves of four-foot squares; the floor counts it in one-foot tiles and halves, which is the same reason (a line from corner to corner cuts a square in two). Meno's hurry is the dialogue's ("compelled to go away before the mysteries"); the five questions are ours: the dialogue's Socrates asked the boy about fifty. The ladder of four places, and putting "stuck" below "right, but told", is ours; Plato ranks knowing your ignorance above false confidence and knowledge above true opinion.

    The record. By our count the boy replies 52 times in the scene. Fifteen replies are his own: the numbers he counts, his two guesses, "I do not know", "I do not understand", and "from this". The other 37 are yes, certainly, true.

    The rest of the dialogue, which the end screen reads rather than plays: if goodness were knowledge it could be taught; there seem to be no teachers of it, since Themistocles, Aristides, Pericles and Thucydides had their sons taught riding, music and wrestling and could not make them good; so the goodness of good statesmen is right opinion, a gift of the gods, not knowledge; and nobody will know for sure until someone finds out what goodness is.
