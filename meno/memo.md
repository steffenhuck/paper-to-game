## 1. Paper

Plato, *Meno* (c. 385 BCE), in Benjamin Jowett's translation, as printed from the Internet Classics Archive (classics.mit.edu/Plato/meno.html, 33 pages). Readable online there and in every edition of Plato; the slave-boy passage is 82b–86c in Stephanus numbering, which the PDF does not carry, so page numbers below are the PDF's. Only the text was available: no figures (the dialogue's figures were drawn in the dust and are reconstructed from the words), no code, no data.

## 2. What the paper hands over

**An argument**, primary, with a **demonstration** inside it that behaves like an experiment with one subject. Meno asks whether goodness can be taught; Socrates says he does not know what it is; Meno's definitions fail; Meno objects that you cannot look for what you do not know (p. 12); Socrates answers with a demonstration on a slave boy who has never been taught geometry (pp. 13–20), then argues on hypothesis that if goodness were knowledge it would be taught, finds no teachers (the sons of Themistocles, Aristides, Pericles and Thucydides, pp. 26–28), and closes on the difference between knowledge and true opinion, which is "as good a guide" while it stays but runs off like Daedalus' statues unless tied by "the tie of the cause" (pp. 30–31). The demonstration is the one part with numbers in it: a square of two feet holds four; the boy says the double square has a double side (it holds sixteen); then three (nine); then "I do not know"; then, from the figure Socrates draws, eight on the line from corner to corner. Secondary: the states of a learner the dialogue names on the way, which the game formalises into a toy model of the boy (section 16).

## 3. The discovery, and its kind

**A mechanism made intelligible**, with a counterintuitive result at its heart. How does anyone find out what they do not know? By being asked, not told: a confident wrong answer has to be broken by checking it (the boy counts his own square and it is four times, not twice); the breaking leaves him stuck, which Socrates says is the best thing that has happened to him; only then does he look; and what he finds by counting is his, and can be found again in another form, while what he is merely told is a true opinion that runs away. The counterintuitive part is double: doubling the side quadruples the square, and the shortest way to teach (telling) does not make anyone know.

## 4. The core narrative

A rich young man asks a poor old man whether you can teach someone to be good. The old man says he doesn't even know what good is, and the young man gets cross: how can you look for something when you don't know what it is? So the old man calls over a boy who works in the house and has never had a lesson, draws a square on the floor, and asks him how to make one twice as big. The boy is sure: make the sides twice as long. He counts it: four times as big. He tries a bit less: still too big. Now he's stuck, and says so, and the old man says that's the best thing that has happened to him today, because now he'll look. A few questions later the boy finds the answer himself, on the line from corner to corner. Nobody told him. And the old man says something else: if I'd just told him, he'd have said "yes" and lost it by morning.

## 5. Who lives in the paper

- **Socrates**, the questioner: controls what he draws in the dust and what he asks; never (he says) tells. Could anticipate the boy.
- **The boy**, a slave of Meno's household, untaught: controls which line he names; counts; says "I do not know". The subject of the demonstration.
- **Meno**, the observer and judge ("observe whether he learns of me or only remembers", p. 13); in a hurry ("compelled to go away before the mysteries", p. 8); wants answers handed to him ("I would rather that you should answer, Socrates", p. 6).
- **Anytus**, sure that any Athenian gentleman can teach goodness, angry at being asked (pp. 24–28).
- **The fathers and sons**: four great Athenians who had their sons taught riding, music and wrestling and could not make them good (pp. 26–28).
- **The torpedo fish**, Meno's figure for Socrates, which numbs whoever touches it (p. 12).
- **Daedalus' statues**, which walk off unless tied down (p. 31).
- **The guide to Larisa**, who leads as well with a true opinion as with knowledge (p. 30).

## 7. The aim and the verdict

**The aim**, on its own line on the start screen, at the top of the play screen and first in How to play: *Bring the boy to a square twice this size — by asking, not telling — before Meno has to go.*

**The verdict** says, in words:

- *Where the boy stands*, as a headline in his own voice: "I found it, and I found it again." / "I had it yesterday." / "Double the line, Meno!" / "I do not know, Meno." Under it the plinth: the statue tied with a cord, the plinth empty with footprints leading off, the old statue of the double line still standing, or the empty plinth with nothing yet on it.
- *What Plato says that is*, one rung of four, all four drawn as a ladder with the boy on his: sure and wrong; stuck, and better off ("is he not better off in knowing his ignorance?"); right, but told, a true opinion that walks off like Daedalus' statues; right, and found by counting, the opinion tied by working out the cause, which Plato calls recollection and says becomes knowledge when he is asked again in other forms.
- *What you did*: what you would have said at the start, whether you let him be wrong, which lines you laid, what he counted, whether you told him.
- *The dialogue's own boy*, in a line: double, sixteen; three, nine; "I do not know"; Socrates drew three more squares and the four lines from corner to corner, and the boy counted the halves: eight, "from this". Plato's own verdict on him: true notions "stirred up as in a dream".
- *What follows*, read not played: Meno's question was whether goodness can be taught. Plato's answer: if it were knowledge, it could be drawn out like this; the great men of Athens could not pass theirs to their sons, so theirs was right opinion, a gift of the gods, not knowledge; and nobody will know for sure until someone finds out what goodness is.

## 8. Three concepts

**A. You hold the stick** (experimenter; strategic). The player is Socrates with the boy at the floor, Meno watching, a purse of five questions before Meno must go. The original square is laid in the floor. Controls: tap a peg to lay a line from the square's corner (along the ground, or corner to corner across one or two tiles), then one of two buttons: *Ask him* (with no line laid: "Which line, boy?", and he lays his own and counts it; with a line laid: "How big is the square on this line?", and he counts it) or *Tell him: it's this line*. The boy follows three rules the screen states: he counts truly, whole tiles and halves; while he thinks he knows, he tests only his own line; he agrees with whatever you tell him. Before the first question the player privately commits to their own answer. Deliberation: tell or ask; whether to correct him or let him count his mistake; which line to spend a question on once he is stuck. After: his count, his statue toppling or rising, his words. Moment: the boy counts the square on the corner-to-corner line, four tiles and eight halves, "eight!", and claims the line as his. Second moment, next morning: Meno gives him a square of three, and he either lays its corner-to-corner line himself and counts eighteen, or does what he was told yesterday and counts eight, or says "double". Fun: anticipation (the count running up), agency (his state follows from your questions), discovery (the line), a second discovery (what telling does, seen the next morning). Second play: tell early, tell late, ask throughout, run out of questions: four fates, each one of Plato's rungs.

**B. Be the boy** (subject; discovery). The paper's experiment run on the player. Socrates draws the square of four and asks for the side of the double square; the player taps a peg; the square builds and counts. Then three; then, whenever the player chooses, *I do not know*, which is a move, and after which Socrates does what he did in the dialogue: draws the three squares and asks the player to count the halves. The boy's own path is the ghost at the end, beside the player's. Deliberation: which line; when to admit being stuck. Moment: the player's own square of sixteen, then the tilted square counting to eight. Fun: anticipation, discovery. Second play: little; the answer is now known.

**C. The torpedo** (reckless: a strange agent, real time, no words on the play screen). The player is the torpedo fish of Meno's simile, swimming through an agora where people walk with their certainties glowing over their heads as figures (a doubled square, a crown for "governing", a gentleman for "any gentleman can teach"); a touch numbs a walker, the figure cracks, and the numbed walker starts to search the floor; walkers who search near a figure in the dust find its true form. The score is walkers who reach a true figure before the lamps go out. Deliberation: whom to numb, in what order, given that numbed walkers slow down and crowd. Moment: the first certainty cracking with a spark. Fun: agency, mastery. Second play: routes.

## 9. The first five minutes

**A, the pick.** I land on a black floor laid in tiles, a meander border, three small figures in white tiles: an old man with a stick, a young man in a cloak, a boy. The title, then one line: bring the boy to a square twice this size, by asking, not telling, before Meno has to go. I press *Begin*. A square of two tiles each way sits in the floor, four tiles, and a line says so. Before the boy is asked: "What would you say? Tap where the side of a square twice this big would end." I think "twice as big, twice as long" and tap four tiles along. An ochre line appears, faint, labelled *your answer*; no count. *Now ask him.* I see the boy, his plinth with a little statue holding a long line ("he is sure: the double square has a double side"), five question stones under Meno, and two buttons. I press *Ask him: which line?* The boy lays his line in white, four tiles along, builds the square on it, and counts aloud, a white tile at a time, the column beside the original's four climbing to sixteen. The game says: "His square holds sixteen: four times the first, not twice." The statue topples. I grin: that was my answer too. Now I weigh it: tell him? I don't know the answer myself. I ask again. "Three, Socrates, between two and four." Nine; the column passes the eight mark. "Nine. More than eight. I do not know, Socrates." The plinth is empty; Meno says, have we done him harm? Three stones left, and now I have to think: two gave four, three gave nine, four gave sixteen; eight is none of these; no straight line will do. I tap the peg one tile up and one along, corner to corner: the outline of a tilted square appears, small. I could ask him how big. I try the peg two up and two along instead, the corner of the first square: a tilted square, its point at the top of the floor. *Ask him: how big is the square on this line?* He lays four whole tiles, then eight white halves along the edges, sliding them together two by two: five, six, seven, eight. "Eight! Twice four. It's this line, Socrates, the one from corner to corner." A white statue rises, holding a slanted line, and he ties a cord round it. Meno: were these answers his own? The floor shows white where he laid and counted, ochre where I drew. Then *Next morning*: Meno lays a square of three and asks him for its double. He lays its corner-to-corner line himself, counts twelve whole and twelve halves, eighteen. The statue stays. The verdict: he is on the top rung; what I would have said; what the dialogue's boy did; what Plato concluded about goodness. Five minutes; I want to know what happens if I tell him.

What the player must already understand for the first action to be a decision: that a square is a number of tiles, which the screen shows. The middle action (which line, once he is stuck) needs what the first two questions produced on screen: straight lines give four, nine, sixteen. The last (tell or ask, with a line that might be it) needs what the screen says about telling and about tomorrow.

**B, the runner-up.** The same floor; Socrates asks *me*. I tap four along; sixteen; three; nine; I tap one-and-one up: two; two-and-two: eight. It took a minute and I'm pleased. Then the boy's own path appears beside mine, and a few lines of Plato. I would play it once. If I had known the answer I would have tapped the corner at once and the game would be over in ten seconds: dead for the author.

## 10. The pick, and why

A. The player is inside the paper, holding its instrument (I asked him; I laid the line; I told him), and the paper's mechanism, a mind that has to be broken before it looks and keeps only what it finds, is the thing that reacts. Deliberation is real at the first action (ask or tell, against my own committed guess), in the middle (which line is worth one of my last questions, with four, nine and sixteen on screen) and at the end (ask him or tell him, with tomorrow named). The stranger who does not know the answer finds it with the boy, which pays the debt the Socrates role would otherwise carry; the opening private guess puts the stranger in the boy's place for one tap. **The author test**: Plato knows the answer and still has to decide whether to let the boy fall twice, sees that a line laid before the boy is stuck is ignored, and can tell the boy and watch the statue walk off in the morning; the four fates are his four rungs. Originality: no game in the table has had the player teach, put a mind on a plinth, or ended on a test the player only watches. Runner-up: B, and what would make me switch: a reader who never feels the boy's mistake as their own, in which case the fix is B's hands for the first two questions (the player taps the boy's answer for him), not a change of game. The reckless concept, C, would have given a wordless, real-time screen and the torpedo's spark; its spark is kept as the moment the boy's statue topples.

## 11. The shape

One lesson, then the next morning, then the verdict; four to five minutes. What in the paper asked for it: the demonstration is one continuous examination with a turning point ("I do not know") and a promise about later ("asked again in different forms, he would know", p. 19); Meno's hurry gives the lesson its end.

- **Start**: the floor, the three figures, the aim, *Begin*.
- **Your answer** (one tap, no feedback): the player's own guess, laid faint and kept for the verdict.
- **The lesson**: up to five questions, one per action. The distinct things to find: (1) the double side gives four times; (2) the next guess overshoots too and he is stuck; (3) a line laid while he thinks he knows is not looked at (found only by those who try); (4) no straight line gives eight, the slant does, by halves; (5) telling versus asking, decided with tomorrow named. Five questions is two for his guesses and three for the search among the four lines he has not tried; calibrated in section 18. The lesson ends when the boy holds a line for the square of eight (his own count, or yours told) or the purse is empty.
- **Next morning** (watched, one tap to start): the paper's square of three; the boy's attempt by his state.
- **Verdict.**

Presets are the paper's own figure (two feet, four, eight) and the next morning's form (three feet, nine, eighteen), in that order. No score; the fate is the verdict.

## 12. Controls, mechanism, feedback, moment

**Controls.** Tap a glowing peg to lay a line from the square's corner (along the ground, one to four tiles, or corner to corner, one or two tiles); tap it again to lift it. The outline of the square on the line previews in faint ochre; no count. Two buttons: *Ask him* (its label says which question: "which line?" with no line laid, "how big is the square on this line?" with one) and *Tell him: it's this line* (only with a line laid). A ? on the play screen.

**Mechanism** (detailed after build.md in section 16). The boy is in one of four states: sure (double), guessing (three), stuck, found. Any question while sure or guessing makes him lay and count his own line; a count that is not eight moves him on (sure → guessing → stuck). A question about your line while he is stuck makes him count the square on it; a count of eight moves him to found, by his own count. Telling sets his answer to your line, by your word, and records whether he was stuck when told. Next morning: found by his own count → he lays the corner-to-corner line of the new square and counts it; told after being stuck → he lays yesterday's line where it was and counts it; told while he still thought he knew → he goes back to doubling the side; never got there → "I do not know". Counting: the square's tiles whole (all four corners inside) and half (cut on the diagonal), two halves to a whole.

**Feedback.** Tiles laid one at a time, white, with the count spoken; halves sliding together; the column beside the original square's four climbing past the eight mark or stopping on it; the statue toppling, the plinth empty, a new statue rising and the cord tied; the game's line before the boy's; the question stones going dark.

**The moment.** Four whole tiles and eight halves pairing up, the column stopping exactly on eight, "It's this line."

## 15. Divergences

- **The boy lays his own lines.** In the dialogue Socrates draws everything, including the boy's wrong squares; here the boy lays what he proposes, so that white means his. Declared in the About.
- **Three states of the boy before he finds it** (sure, guessing, stuck), from the dialogue's sequence (double, three, "I do not know"); the rule that he tests only his own line while he thinks he knows is p. 17 ("would he ever have enquired into or learned what he fancied he knew… until he had fallen into perplexity?"), made into a rule of the world.
- **Telling is allowed.** Socrates never tells outright; the game lets the player, to show what the dialogue claims telling does. The next-morning model of each kind of telling is ours, built on pp. 17, 19 and 31.
- **The next morning is ours.** The dialogue promises it ("if he were frequently asked the same questions, in different forms, he would know", p. 19) and does not run it. The square of three is our choice of a different form.
- **Meno's hurry as five questions.** The hurry is the paper's (p. 8); the number is ours. The dialogue's Socrates asked the boy about fifty.
- **Counting by tiles.** The dialogue counts the tilted square as four halves of four-foot squares; the floor counts it in one-foot tiles and halves. Same answer, same reason (a line from corner to corner cuts a square in two).
- **The opening guess** is ours.
- **Daedalus' statue as the picture of every state of belief.** The dialogue uses it for true opinion only.
- **The fates as a ladder.** Plato ranks knowledge above true opinion (p. 31) and knowing one's ignorance above false confidence (p. 17); placing aporia below true opinion is ours, since a true opinion is "as good a guide" (p. 30).

## 16. Mechanism kind and claim boundaries

**Toy.** `mechanism.js` runs a small model of the boy built to illustrate the dialogue's argument; the geometry inside it is exact. The boy is in one of three states before he has an answer (sure, guessing, stuck) and follows four rules, stated on the play screen: he counts truly, whole tiles and halves; while he thinks he knows, any question gets his own line, laid and counted by him; once stuck, he counts the square on whatever line you lay, and a count of eight makes that line his answer; he agrees with whatever you tell him. The next morning: found by his own count → he lays the new square's corner-to-corner line and counts it; told while stuck → he lays the line he was told where it was yesterday; told while he still thought he knew → he goes back to doubling the side; Meno left while he was stuck → "I do not know".

- **Demonstrates**: under these rules, a boy who still thinks he knows does not look at a better line; a boy told an answer agrees and cannot use it on a new square; a boy who counts it himself can count it again. The doubled side gives four times the square, three gives nine, the corner-to-corner line eight (four whole tiles and eight halves, half of each of the four squares of four).
- **Assumes**: the rules, the five questions, the next morning and what each kind of boy does there. The rules are built on three of the dialogue's claims: he would not have looked while he fancied he knew (p. 17); true opinions run away until tied by working out the cause (p. 31); asked again in different forms, he would know (p. 19). The dialogue does not run a next morning and does not say what a told boy would do.
- **Plato establishes** an argument with one demonstration on one boy, offered as evidence that learning is recollection; not a measurement. The About screen says so, and that readers since have argued over how much Socrates led him.

Which discoveries come from the paper and which from the design: the geometry (double side, four times; corner to corner, twice) is the paper's and exact. That telling does not make the boy know is the paper's claim, staged by our rule for the next morning. That he must be stuck before he looks is the paper's claim (p. 17), made a rule. That pure asking without laying anything fails (the purist's fate) follows from the dialogue's own sequence (after "I do not know" Socrates draws) and from our purse. Nothing on screen says "Plato proved"; the ladder's order is the paper's, the ladder is ours, and the About says so.

## 17. Calibration plan and result

Then the rules one by one, every next-morning fate, an exhaustive run of all 25,381 possible lessons (found next morning if and only if he counted eight himself; the purse never below zero), the strategies table, and robustness to the purse.

Result: **41 passed, 0 failed** (`calibration-result.txt`). The exhaustive run caught a design flaw in the first draft: with lines up to three tiles corner to corner on the first day, a boy told "three corner to corner" (wrong on the first day) was right the next morning by luck. A searcher who does not know the answer and tries the four untried lines in random order finds it with three tries (five questions) three times in four; with four questions half the time; with six always. Five questions was chosen so that a sensible searcher (who reads four, nine and sixteen off the column and tries a slant) always finds it, a random one usually does, and the purist who never lays a line does not. Every fate is reached by some obvious way of playing; the wrong branches are the paper's (telling; not looking while sure; nothing laid for him to look at). No option is dead: every line is taken by some strategy, and telling is taken by the knower, who is the author. The trade-off is shown inside the game: telling is cheaper by two questions, and its cost arrives the next morning, before the verdict. Robustness: with a purse of 4, 6, 8 or 10 the asked boy finds it again and the told boy does not; the fates follow from the rules, not the purse.
