The paper. Peter Singer, "All Animals Are Equal", Philosophic Exchange 5(1), 1974, pages 103–116, written at New York University; open access in the SUNY repository (handle 20.500.12648/3306). Parts of it had appeared in Singer's review of Animals, Men and Morals in the New York Review of Books, 5 April 1973; the essay became the first chapter of Animal Liberation (1975). This game was first built from a textbook excerpt of the book chapter, rebuilt the same day against the 1974 paper, and rebuilt four times more the next day and once again the day after, for a smart eleven-year-old; the paper is the authority where the two disagree. Page numbers below are the journal's.

What the paper says

"The rights of animals" was once used to parody the rights of women: if Wollstonecraft's arguments held for women, said Thomas Taylor, they held for dogs, cats and horses, which was absurd (p. 103). Singer's reply is to ask what the case for equality rests on. Not on facts: humans differ in every capacity, and if equality rested on sameness, a sophisticated opponent could draw his line at an I.Q. of 100 and we would have no answer (p. 104). Equality is a prescription, not a description; each to count for one, the like interests of every being affected weighed the same (p. 105). The boundary of that concern is the capacity to suffer or enjoy, which Bentham marked with three questions, "Can they reason? nor Can they talk? but, Can they suffer?" (p. 106); a stone has no interests because it cannot suffer, a mouse has an interest in not being tormented, and sentience is the only defensible boundary; any other, like intelligence, is as arbitrary as skin colour (p. 107). Racists, sexists and speciesists give more weight to the interests of their own group: the pattern is the same in each case (p. 107). Two practices show it: eating animals for the taste, and experimenting on them. The experimenter's test is this: would he perform his experiment on an orphaned human infant if that were the only way to save many lives? If not, his readiness to use animals is simple discrimination, since adult apes, cats, mice and other mammals are more aware of what is happening to them, more self-directing, and at least as sensitive to pain as any infant; and if he answers that the infant will grow into more, the same question returns with a human who has severe and irreversible brain damage (pp. 109–110). Philosophers who defend the species line, Singer says, do so by forgetting these humans or by a move like Stanley Benn's, that rationality is "the human norm"; and he closes with an exercise: take Benn's passage, substitute "white" for "men", "black" for "dog", "high I.Q." for "rationality", and finally change "species" to "race", then re-read it (pp. 113–116). Killing is a footnote (fn. 7).

What the game does with it

The question is the paper's own: whose pain counts as much as yours? That is what the paper means by equality, equal consideration of interests: if two beings are hurt exactly as much, the hurt matters exactly as much, whoever they are. The game asks it in a child's words, per unit of pain ("if one of them were hurt exactly as much as you, would it matter exactly as much?"), so that a player who thinks a pig feels a slap less than a baby does can still answer "as much": that answer is about the weight of a unit of pain, not about how big the unit is. You draw your own line first, with no rule on the board. The ten are the paper's own examples, in a child's words: a grown-up; a baby of a few days with no parents (Bentham's "infant of a day", the paper's "orphaned human infant"); a grown-up whose brain is badly hurt for good (the paper's "severe and irreversible brain damage"); a grown-up who cannot learn much (the paper says "permanently retarded" and, quoting Benn, "imbeciles"); a chimpanzee, a dog, a pig, a horse, a mouse; and the stone.

Then three rules with marks come to the board one at a time: Bentham's "Can they reason?" and "Can they talk?" as the paper quotes them, and the one the paper adds in its own voice, Aware ("aware of what is happening to them, more self-directing"). A rule is a way of deciding whose pain counts as much as yours; Reason's rule is "whoever can think well enough counts as much as you". Each rule sorts the ten by its marks and checks the player's line: it agrees if everyone counted in full has a higher mark than everyone counted for less, and otherwise it objects and names the two beings that are the wrong way round by its marks. The player may move anyone and the rule checks again, or keep the line. The marks are stylised: the orderings the game relies on are the ones the paper states (a horse or dog above the baby on reason and talk; the dog level with the grown-up who cannot learn much on reason, Singer's reply to Benn; apes and mice above the baby on awareness; the chimpanzee, dog and pig above the hurt grown-up, the baby and the grown-up who cannot learn much on awareness and intelligence; the grown-up above all three), and the lengths are ours. One length matters beyond the paper: the hurt grown-up reads none on Reason, Talk and Aware, like the stone, which is why only Suffer gives Singer's line in this cast.

The fourth rule, Suffer, is not shown with marks; it is asked, in Bentham's words, "Can they suffer?", yes or no, and only about the beings the player counts for less (for the others the player has answered already, and the stone is not asked). The Suffer rule then objects wherever the player has said "yes, it can suffer" and still counts its pain for less: the complaint is built from the player's own two answers, which is the paper's argument in one move. Where the player has said "no", the board notes that Singer disputes the fact (every one of the nine can suffer, the mouse "at least as sensitive to pain as any human infant"). The board then names the player's last line by its shape. The four people: Species, the line most people draw, and the sponge writes Race and Sex over the name, which is the paper's own closing exercise (change "species" to "race" and re-read) and its opening (the rights of women mocked with the rights of brutes). Everyone but the stone: Singer's line, which the Suffer rule gives. A set that some rule with marks gives: a Reason line, say, with what it counts for less, and the paper's reply that a mark like intelligence is as arbitrary as skin colour for whose pain counts. The four people plus some animals: Species with favourites. The four people minus some: Species with exceptions. Everything, nobody, or any other set: named as such, with the nearest of the two known lines and what was added or taken out. The verdict reads all five lines, says who moved the player and when, and puts the player's two Suffer answers side by side. The second board is the paper's one thought experiment, asked as the paper asks it ("why the chimpanzee, and not the baby?"), with the paper's follow-up on potential, the paper's reply in paraphrase, and the paper's distinction between the story that saves many lives and real experiments; its two phrases kept in its own words are "simple discrimination" and "the pattern is the same". Killing, which the paper puts in a footnote, is left out.

Mechanism and claim boundaries

Kind: toy. A model built to illustrate the paper's argument, not the paper's model (it has none) and not its data (it has none). What the game demonstrates: that on a cast of the paper's own examples, ranked as the paper ranks them, no rule with marks gives the line most people draw, the four people, while the Suffer rule gives Singer's line, everyone who can suffer; that every one of the 1024 lines a player can draw is either a rule's line, with its price named, or a line drawn by hand; and that a player who says a being can suffer and counts its pain for less has said two things the Suffer rule cannot both accept. The calibration check proves the first two for every set and for five hundred random casts that keep only the paper's stated orderings. What the game assumes: the cast, the four rules, the lengths of the marks, and the wording of the paper's answers. What the paper establishes: the argument, as an argument: it is a philosopher's case, not a finding about the world, and a reader who rejects a premise (that equality is a prescription, that suffering is the boundary) rejects the line too. The game shows where the line goes under the paper's premises; it does not show that the premises are right.

What is left out

The passages on race, sex and genetics (pp. 104–105); the description of intensive farming (pp. 108–109); the inference that animals feel pain, which the game takes as read; the survey of philosophers other than Benn (pp. 111–113); the third form of speciesism, the one in philosophy departments (p. 110); and footnote 7 on killing.
