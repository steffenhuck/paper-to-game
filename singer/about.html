The paper. Peter Singer, "All Animals Are Equal", Philosophic Exchange 5(1), 1974, pages 103–116, written at New York University; open access in the SUNY repository (handle 20.500.12648/3306). Parts of it had appeared in Singer's review of Animals, Men and Morals in the New York Review of Books, 5 April 1973; the essay became the first chapter of Animal Liberation (1975). This game was first built from a textbook excerpt of the book chapter, rebuilt the same day against the 1974 paper, and rebuilt three times more the next day for a smart eleven-year-old; the paper is the authority where the two disagree. Page numbers below are the journal's.

What the paper says

"The rights of animals" was once used to parody the rights of women: if Wollstonecraft's arguments held for women, said Thomas Taylor, they held for dogs, cats and horses, which was absurd (p. 103). Singer's reply is to ask what the case for equality rests on. Not on facts: humans differ in every capacity, and if equality rested on sameness, a sophisticated opponent could draw his line at an I.Q. of 100 and we would have no answer (p. 104). Equality is a prescription, not a description; each to count for one, the like interests of every being affected weighed the same (p. 105). The boundary of that concern is the capacity to suffer or enjoy, which Bentham marked with three questions, "Can they reason? nor Can they talk? but, Can they suffer?" (p. 106); a stone has no interests because it cannot suffer, a mouse has an interest in not being tormented, and sentience is the only defensible boundary; any other, like intelligence, is as arbitrary as skin colour (p. 107). Racists, sexists and speciesists give more weight to the interests of their own group: the pattern is the same in each case (p. 107). Two practices show it: eating animals for the taste, and experimenting on them. The experimenter's test is this: would he perform his experiment on an orphaned human infant if that were the only way to save many lives? If not, his readiness to use animals is simple discrimination, since adult apes, cats, mice and other mammals are more aware of what is happening to them, more self-directing, and at least as sensitive to pain as any infant; and if he answers that the infant will grow into more, the same question returns with a human who has severe and irreversible brain damage (pp. 109–110). Philosophers who defend the species line, Singer says, do so by forgetting these humans or by a move like Stanley Benn's, that rationality is "the human norm"; and he closes with an exercise: take Benn's passage, substitute "white" for "men", "black" for "dog", "high I.Q." for "rationality", and finally change "species" to "race", then re-read it (pp. 113–116). Killing is a footnote (fn. 7).

What the game does with it

You draw your own line first: tap everyone who counts, with no ruler in sight. Then the four rulers come to the board one at a time, and each time you may move anyone in or out, or keep your line; the board says whether that ruler could have drawn the line you left it with. The verdict reads all five lines: who moved you, and when. The ten are the paper's own examples, in a child's words: a grown-up; a baby of a few days with no parents (Bentham's "infant of a day", the paper's "orphaned human infant"); a grown-up whose brain is badly hurt for good (the paper's "severe and irreversible brain damage"); a grown-up who cannot learn much (the paper says "permanently retarded" and, quoting Benn, "imbeciles"); a chimpanzee, a dog, a pig, a horse, a mouse; and the stone. The rulers are Bentham's three questions as the paper quotes them, Reason, Talk and Suffer, and the one the paper adds in its own voice, Aware ("aware of what is happening to them, more self-directing"), in that order, Suffer last. A ruler can draw a line only if everyone in reads higher on it than everyone out; if not, the board shows the two that are the wrong way round. The marks are stylised: the orderings the game relies on are the ones the paper states (a horse or dog above the baby on reason and talk; the dog level with the grown-up who cannot learn much on reason, Singer's reply to Benn; apes and mice above the baby on awareness; the chimpanzee, dog and pig above the hurt grown-up, the baby and the grown-up who cannot learn much on awareness and intelligence; the grown-up above all three), and the lengths are ours. One length matters beyond the paper: the hurt grown-up reads none on Reason, Talk and Aware, like the stone, which is why only Suffer draws Singer's line in this cast.

The board then names your last line by its shape, and says how you got there: no ruler moved you; the rulers drew your line (on every ruler you took that ruler's line); or some rulers moved you, named one by one. The four people: Species, the line most people draw, and the sponge writes Race and Sex over the name, which is the paper's own closing exercise (change "species" to "race" and re-read) and its opening (the rights of women mocked with the rights of brutes). Everyone but the stone: Singer's line, drawn by Suffer. A set that some reading ruler draws: a Reason line, say, with what it keeps out, and the paper's reply that a mark like intelligence is as arbitrary as skin colour for who counts. The four people plus some animals: Species with favourites. The four people minus some: Species with exceptions. Everything, nobody, or any other set: named as such, with the nearest of the two known lines and what was added or dropped. Species is never offered as a ruler; it is the name of a line no ruler draws. The second board is the paper's one thought experiment, asked as the paper asks it ("why not the baby?"), with four answers, a fifth when your own line already put the baby out, the paper's follow-up on potential, and the paper's reply in paraphrase; its two phrases kept in its own words are "simple discrimination" and "the pattern is the same". Killing, which the paper puts in a footnote, is left out.

Mechanism and claim boundaries

Kind: toy. A model built to illustrate the paper's argument, not the paper's model (it has none) and not its data (it has none). What the game demonstrates: that on a cast of the paper's own examples, ranked as the paper ranks them, the line most people draw, the four people, is a line on no ruler, while Singer's line, everyone who can feel pain, is drawn by Suffer; and that every one of the 1024 lines a player can draw is either a ruler's line, with its price named, or a line drawn by hand. The calibration check proves this for every set and for five hundred random casts that keep only the paper's stated orderings. What the game assumes: the cast, the four rulers, the lengths of the marks, and the wording of the paper's answers. What the paper establishes: the argument, as an argument: it is a philosopher's case, not a finding about the world, and a reader who rejects a premise (that equality is a prescription, that suffering is the boundary) rejects the line too. The game shows where the line goes under the paper's premises; it does not show that the premises are right.

What is left out

The passages on race, sex and genetics (pp. 104–105); the description of intensive farming (pp. 108–109); the inference that animals feel pain, which the game takes as read; the survey of philosophers other than Benn (pp. 111–113); the third form of speciesism, the one in philosophy departments (p. 110); and footnote 7 on killing.
