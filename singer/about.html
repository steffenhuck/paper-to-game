The paper. Peter Singer, "All Animals Are Equal", Philosophic Exchange 5(1), 1974, pages 103–116, written at New York University; open access in the SUNY repository (handle 20.500.12648/3306). Parts of it had appeared in Singer's review of Animals, Men and Morals in the New York Review of Books, 5 April 1973; the essay became the first chapter of Animal Liberation (1975). This game was first built from a textbook excerpt of the book chapter, rebuilt the same day against the 1974 paper, and rebuilt four times more the next day, four times again the day after, and once more the day after that, the last four into a new shape in which the player is the scientist first, for a smart eleven-year-old; the paper is the authority where the two disagree. Page numbers below are the journal's.

What the paper says

"The rights of animals" was once used to parody the rights of women: if Wollstonecraft's arguments held for women, said Thomas Taylor, they held for dogs, cats and horses, which was absurd (p. 103). Singer's reply is to ask what the case for equality rests on. Not on facts: humans differ in every capacity, and if equality rested on sameness, a sophisticated opponent could draw his line at an I.Q. of 100 and we would have no answer (p. 104). Equality is a prescription, not a description; each to count for one, the like interests of every being affected weighed the same (p. 105). The boundary of that concern is the capacity to suffer or enjoy, which Bentham marked with three questions, "Can they reason? nor Can they talk? but, Can they suffer?" (p. 106); a stone has no interests because it cannot suffer, a mouse has an interest in not being tormented, and sentience is the only defensible boundary; any other, like intelligence, is as arbitrary as skin colour (p. 107). Racists, sexists and speciesists give more weight to the interests of their own group: the pattern is the same in each case (p. 107). Two practices show it: eating animals for the taste, and experimenting on them. The experimenter's test is this: would he perform his experiment on an orphaned human infant if that were the only way to save many lives? If not, his readiness to use animals is simple discrimination, since adult apes, cats, mice and other mammals are more aware of what is happening to them, more self-directing, and at least as sensitive to pain as any infant; and if he answers that the infant will grow into more, the same question returns with a human who has severe and irreversible brain damage (pp. 109–110). Philosophers who defend the species line, Singer says, do so by forgetting these humans or by a move like Stanley Benn's, that rationality is "the human norm"; and he closes with an exercise: take Benn's passage, substitute "white" for "men", "black" for "dog", "high I.Q." for "rationality", and finally change "species" to "race", then re-read it (pp. 113–116). Killing is a footnote (fn. 7).

What the game does with it

You are the scientist in the paper's thought experiment (pp. 109–110), and the game begins there. Three experiments come to your desk, with rising stakes: one would spare a child a year of illness, one would save a life, one would save a thousand lives. The stories change from play to play (three for each rung); the cost never does: whoever is used will be in bad pain for a day and then be fine again, with no lasting harm. Each experiment has its own menu of three, and across the three menus you meet the nine of the paper's examples that can suffer: a grown-up; a baby a few days old with no parents (Bentham's "infant of a day", the paper's "orphaned human infant"); a grown-up whose brain is badly hurt for good (the paper's "severe and irreversible brain damage"); a grown-up who cannot learn much (the paper says "permanently retarded" and, quoting Benn, "imbeciles"); a chimpanzee, a dog, a pig, a horse and a mouse. The paper's tenth example, the stone, which has no interests because it cannot suffer (p. 107), is on no menu, since no experiment on a stone helps anyone; it appears once, at the Suffer question, as the boundary. The menus are fixed and each is built around one of the paper's clashes: the third is the paper's own test, the baby to save many lives, with Bentham's dog beside it; the second is the paper's follow-up, the hurt grown-up beside the apes; the first puts Benn's "imbecile" beside the horse and the mouse. On each experiment you tick everyone you would be willing to use, or nobody. The pain is the same for all, so the only thing your three choices can be about is who they are.

Then the board tries to make sense of what you did, which is a question of rationalisability: can one criterion have produced these three choices from these three menus? A rule with marks explains an experiment if there is a cutoff such that you ticked exactly the creatures on that menu with marks at or below it; equivalently, no creature you used there has a mark higher than or equal to a creature you spared there (on the board: every tick sits below every cross). It explains your choices if it explains all three experiments. Each experiment is checked on its own; the game does not ask that the cutoffs rise with the stakes, a condition about consistency across stakes rather than about the rule, dropped so that every complaint is about one column the player can see. The board says first how many of its three rules can explain all three choices, without saying which. Then the rules come to the board one at a time: Bentham's "Can they reason?" and "Can they talk?" as the paper quotes them, and the one the paper adds in its own voice, Aware ("aware of what is happening to them, more self-directing"). Each sorts the nine by its marks, shows the three menus as columns with what you did, and before it speaks asks you to tap the experiments you expect it to complain about (or none). Then it tells you, experiment by experiment: where it is fine it draws a short red mark in that column, its cutoff; where it complains it circles that column's worst pair (largest gap in marks), and the tally says which of its complaints you found. The marks are stylised: the orderings the game relies on are the ones the paper states (a horse or dog above the baby on reason and talk; the dog level with the grown-up who cannot learn much on reason, Singer's reply to Benn; apes and mice above the baby on awareness; the chimpanzee, dog and pig above the hurt grown-up, the baby and the grown-up who cannot learn much on awareness and intelligence; the grown-up above all of them), and the lengths are ours. The calibration check shows that with random marks that keep only those orderings, no rule with marks explains the team's choices, animals used and people spared at every experiment.

Then one more question with no marks, Bentham's, "Can they suffer?", asked yes or no for each of the nine; the stone appears here, unasked, as the one thing on which everyone agrees, and the board says that this is where Singer's line runs: between the mouse and the stone, not between the baby and the mouse. The Suffer rule is the paper's: if a being can suffer, its pain counts as much as anyone's, so among beings that can suffer none may be used sooner than another, and whatever cannot suffer may be used freely. It is tested the same way, with your own answers as its marks, so its complaint is built from two things you said yourself, which is the paper's argument in one move. Where you have said "no", the board notes that Singer disputes the fact (the mouse "at least as sensitive to pain as any human infant") and says whether, with his facts, Suffer would object. The board then names your line. Nobody who can suffer, at any price: no quarrel with Singer about animals at all, but a different question, whether a day of pain for one may ever be traded for a thousand lives; that is Philippa Foot's paper, also on this shelf, and the board links to it. Suffer explains your choices with Singer's facts: Singer's line, equal consideration. A rule with marks explains them: a Reason line, say, and the paper's reply that a mark like intelligence is as arbitrary as skin colour for whose pain may be spent. No rule with marks, but the team's rule, animals before people, does: Species, the line most people draw, and the sponge writes Race and Sex over the name, which is the paper's own closing exercise (change "species" to "race" and re-read, p. 116) and its opening (the rights of women mocked with the rights of brutes, p. 103). Suffer explains them only with your own answers: all alike by your own answers, with the fact in dispute. Nothing explains them: your own order, with Singer's question, what do the ones you would use have in common? The verdict is about what you did: your three choices, what each rule said of them, your Suffer answers, the name, where you and Singer part, and the two practices the paper names, experiments and eating.

Mechanism and claim boundaries

Kind: toy. A model built to illustrate the paper's argument, not the paper's model (it has none) and not its data (it has none). What the game demonstrates: that on the paper's own nine examples that can suffer, ranked as the paper ranks them and offered on three menus, the choices most people make, animals used and people spared, are explained by no rule with marks and by the team's rule alone, while the Suffer rule objects to them with Singer's facts; that every one of the 512 sets of choices, with every one of the 512 answer sets to "can they suffer?", gets a name, a check by each rule and a verdict; and that a player who says two beings can suffer and would use one and spare the other has said two things the Suffer rule cannot both accept. The calibration check proves this for every set of choices, matches the per-experiment test against a brute-force search for a cutoff in each experiment, and repeats the first claim for five hundred random casts that keep only the paper's stated orderings. What the game assumes: the nine, the three menus and which creature is on which, the three rungs and their stories, the fixed cost (a day of pain, no lasting harm, which keeps the question about pain and not about death), the three rules, the lengths of the marks, and the wording of the paper's answers. What the paper establishes: the argument, as an argument: it is a philosopher's case, not a finding about the world, and a reader who rejects a premise (that equality is a prescription, that suffering is the boundary) rejects the line too. The game shows where the line goes under the paper's premises; it does not show that the premises are right. A player who would use nobody has not drawn a line between the nine at all; the game sends that player to Foot, not to Singer.

What is left out

The passages on race, sex and genetics (pp. 104–105); the description of intensive farming (pp. 108–109); the inference that animals feel pain, which the game takes as read; the survey of philosophers other than Benn (pp. 111–113); the third form of speciesism, the one in philosophy departments (p. 110); and footnote 7 on killing.
