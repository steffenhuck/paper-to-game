The paper. Peter Singer, "All Animals Are Equal", Philosophic Exchange 5(1), 1974, pages 103–116, written at New York University; open access in the SUNY repository (handle 20.500.12648/3306). Parts of it had appeared in Singer's review of Animals, Men and Morals in the New York Review of Books, 5 April 1973; the essay became the first chapter of Animal Liberation (1975). This game was first built from a textbook excerpt of the book chapter and rebuilt the same day against the 1974 paper, which is the authority where the two disagree. Page numbers below are the journal's.

What the paper says

"The rights of animals" was once used to parody the rights of women: if Wollstonecraft's arguments held for women, said Thomas Taylor, they held for dogs, cats and horses, which was absurd (p. 103). Singer's reply is to ask what the case for equality rests on. Not on facts: humans differ in every capacity, and if equality rested on sameness, a sophisticated opponent could draw his line at an I.Q. of 100 and we would have no answer (p. 104). Equality is a prescription, not a description; each to count for one, the like interests of every being affected weighed the same (p. 105). The boundary of that concern is the capacity to suffer or enjoy, which Bentham marked with three questions, "Can they reason? nor Can they talk? but, Can they suffer?" (p. 106); a stone has no interests because it cannot suffer, a mouse has an interest in not being tormented, and sentience is the only defensible boundary; any other, like intelligence, is as arbitrary as skin colour (p. 107). Racists, sexists and speciesists give more weight to the interests of their own group: the pattern is the same in each case (p. 107). Two practices show it: eating animals for the taste, and experimenting on them. The experimenter's test is this: would he perform his experiment on an orphaned human infant if that were the only way to save many lives? If not, his readiness to use animals is simple discrimination, since adult apes, cats, mice and other mammals are more aware of what is happening to them, more self-directing, and at least as sensitive to pain as any infant; and if he answers that the infant will grow into more, the same question returns with a human who has severe and irreversible brain damage (pp. 109–110). Philosophers who defend the species line, Singer says, do so by forgetting these humans or by a move like Stanley Benn's, that rationality is "the human norm"; and he closes with an exercise: take Benn's passage, substitute "white" for "men", "black" for "dog", "high I.Q." for "rationality", and finally change "species" to "race", then re-read it (pp. 113–116). Killing is a footnote (fn. 7).

What the game does with it

You are the "we" of the paper's first pages: you want to keep every person in and every animal out, and have to say with which ruler. The ten on the board are the paper's own examples: a normal adult; an infant of a few days, an orphan (Bentham's "infant of a day", the paper's "orphaned human infant"); an adult with severe and irreversible brain damage; an adult with a severe intellectual disability (the paper says "permanently retarded" and, quoting Benn, "imbeciles"; the words on the board are ours); a chimpanzee, a dog, a pig, a horse, a mouse; and the stone. The rulers are Bentham's three questions as the paper quotes them, Reason, Talk and Suffer, and the one the paper adds in its own voice, Aware ("aware of what is happening to them, more self-directing"). The marks are stylised: the orderings the game relies on are the ones the paper states (a horse or dog above the infant on reason and talk; the dog level with the intellectually disabled adult on reason, Singer's reply to Benn; apes and mice above the infant on awareness; the chimpanzee, dog and pig above the brain-damaged adult, the infant and the disabled adult on awareness and intelligence; the adult above all three), and the lengths are ours. The fifth ruler is species, and the two words the sponge writes over it are the paper's own: the exercise with "race", and the opening with the rights of women. The second board is the paper's one thought experiment, asked as the paper asks it, with the paper's own follow-up on potential. The paper's replies on that board are its sentences paraphrased; the two phrases in its own words are "simple discrimination" and "the pattern is the same". The footnote on killing is read, not played.

Mechanism and claim boundaries

Kind: toy. A model built to illustrate the paper's argument, not the paper's model (it has none) and not its data (it has none). What the game demonstrates: that on a cast of the paper's own examples, ranked as the paper ranks them, no line on any ruler you can read keeps every person in and every animal out, and that the only line that does is the species itself; and that the experimenter who takes an animal but not the infant has used that line. The calibration check proves the first claim for every line on every ruler, and for five hundred random casts that keep only the paper's stated orderings. What the game assumes: the cast, the four rulers, the lengths of the marks, and the wording of the paper's answers. What the paper establishes: the argument, as an argument: it is a philosopher's case, not a finding about the world, and a reader who rejects a premise (that equality is a prescription, that suffering is the boundary) rejects the line too. The game shows where the line goes under the paper's premises; it does not show that the premises are right.

What is left out

The passages on race, sex and genetics (pp. 104–105); the description of intensive farming (pp. 108–109); the inference that animals feel pain, which the game takes as read; the survey of philosophers other than Benn (pp. 111–113); the third form of speciesism, the one in philosophy departments (p. 110).
