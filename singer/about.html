The paper. Peter Singer, "All Animals Are Equal", Philosophic Exchange 5(1), 1974, pages 103–116, written at New York University; open access in the SUNY repository (handle 20.500.12648/3306). Parts of it had appeared in Singer's review of Animals, Men and Morals in the New York Review of Books, 5 April 1973; the essay became the first chapter of Animal Liberation (1975). This game was first built from a textbook excerpt of the book chapter, rebuilt the same day against the 1974 paper, and rebuilt again the next day for a smart eleven-year-old; the paper is the authority where the two disagree. Page numbers below are the journal's.

What the paper says

"The rights of animals" was once used to parody the rights of women: if Wollstonecraft's arguments held for women, said Thomas Taylor, they held for dogs, cats and horses, which was absurd (p. 103). Singer's reply is to ask what the case for equality rests on. Not on facts: humans differ in every capacity, and if equality rested on sameness, a sophisticated opponent could draw his line at an I.Q. of 100 and we would have no answer (p. 104). Equality is a prescription, not a description; each to count for one, the like interests of every being affected weighed the same (p. 105). The boundary of that concern is the capacity to suffer or enjoy, which Bentham marked with three questions, "Can they reason? nor Can they talk? but, Can they suffer?" (p. 106); a stone has no interests because it cannot suffer, a mouse has an interest in not being tormented, and sentience is the only defensible boundary; any other, like intelligence, is as arbitrary as skin colour (p. 107). Racists, sexists and speciesists give more weight to the interests of their own group: the pattern is the same in each case (p. 107). Two practices show it: eating animals for the taste, and experimenting on them. The experimenter's test is this: would he perform his experiment on an orphaned human infant if that were the only way to save many lives? If not, his readiness to use animals is simple discrimination, since adult apes, cats, mice and other mammals are more aware of what is happening to them, more self-directing, and at least as sensitive to pain as any infant; and if he answers that the infant will grow into more, the same question returns with a human who has severe and irreversible brain damage (pp. 109–110). Philosophers who defend the species line, Singer says, do so by forgetting these humans or by a move like Stanley Benn's, that rationality is "the human norm"; and he closes with an exercise: take Benn's passage, substitute "white" for "men", "black" for "dog", "high I.Q." for "rationality", and finally change "species" to "race", then re-read it (pp. 113–116). Killing is a footnote (fn. 7).

What the game does with it

You start where the paper says most people start: every person counts, no animal does. Singer's question is "measured how?", and the first board hands you four rulers to answer it. The ten on the board are the paper's own examples, in a child's words: a grown-up; a baby of a few days with no parents (Bentham's "infant of a day", the paper's "orphaned human infant"); a grown-up whose brain is badly hurt for good (the paper's "severe and irreversible brain damage"); a grown-up who cannot learn much (the paper says "permanently retarded" and, quoting Benn, "imbeciles"); a chimpanzee, a dog, a pig, a horse, a mouse; and the stone. The rulers are Bentham's three questions as the paper quotes them, Reason, Talk and Suffer, plus the one the paper adds in its own voice, Aware ("aware of what is happening to them, more self-directing"). The marks are stylised: the orderings the game relies on are the ones the paper states (a horse or dog above the baby on reason and talk; the dog level with the grown-up who cannot learn much on reason, Singer's reply to Benn; apes and mice above the baby on awareness; the chimpanzee, dog and pig above the hurt grown-up, the baby and the grown-up who cannot learn much on awareness and intelligence; the grown-up above all three), and the lengths are ours.

A line can go anywhere. The board then says two things about it: who landed on the wrong side, and whether the ruler drew it at all. A line between two equal marks was not drawn by the ruler; your hand drew it. On Suffer, where all but the stone read the same, every line between people and animals is drawn by hand. Species is never offered as a ruler. After the four rulers you draw one line with no ruler, and if it is the line most people draw the board names it; the sponge then writes Race and Sex over the name, which is the paper's own closing exercise (change "species" to "race" and re-read) and its opening (the rights of women mocked with the rights of brutes). The second board is the paper's one thought experiment, asked as the paper asks it ("why not the baby?"), with four answers, the paper's own follow-up on potential, and the paper's reply in paraphrase; its two phrases kept in its own words are "simple discrimination" and "the pattern is the same". Killing, which the paper puts in a footnote, is left out.

Mechanism and claim boundaries

Kind: toy. A model built to illustrate the paper's argument, not the paper's model (it has none) and not its data (it has none). What the game demonstrates: that on a cast of the paper's own examples, ranked as the paper ranks them, no line that a ruler draws keeps every person in and every animal out; that the line which does is drawn by hand; and that the scientist who takes an animal but not the baby has drawn it again. The calibration check proves the first claim for every line on every ruler, and for five hundred random casts that keep only the paper's stated orderings. What the game assumes: the cast, the four rulers, the lengths of the marks, and the wording of the paper's answers. What the paper establishes: the argument, as an argument: it is a philosopher's case, not a finding about the world, and a reader who rejects a premise (that equality is a prescription, that suffering is the boundary) rejects the line too. The game shows where the line goes under the paper's premises; it does not show that the premises are right.

What is left out

The passages on race, sex and genetics (pp. 104–105); the description of intensive farming (pp. 108–109); the inference that animals feel pain, which the game takes as read; the survey of philosophers other than Benn (pp. 111–113); the third form of speciesism, the one in philosophy departments (p. 110); and footnote 7 on killing.
