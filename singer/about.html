The paper. Peter Singer, "All Animals Are Equal", Philosophic Exchange 5(1), 1974, pages 103–116, written at New York University; open access in the SUNY repository (handle 20.500.12648/3306). Parts of it had appeared in Singer's review of Animals, Men and Morals in the New York Review of Books, 5 April 1973; the essay became the first chapter of Animal Liberation (1975). This game was first built from a textbook excerpt of the book chapter, rebuilt the same day against the 1974 paper, and rebuilt four times more the next day and twice again the day after, the last time into a new shape in which the player is the scientist first, for a smart eleven-year-old; the paper is the authority where the two disagree. Page numbers below are the journal's.

What the paper says

"The rights of animals" was once used to parody the rights of women: if Wollstonecraft's arguments held for women, said Thomas Taylor, they held for dogs, cats and horses, which was absurd (p. 103). Singer's reply is to ask what the case for equality rests on. Not on facts: humans differ in every capacity, and if equality rested on sameness, a sophisticated opponent could draw his line at an I.Q. of 100 and we would have no answer (p. 104). Equality is a prescription, not a description; each to count for one, the like interests of every being affected weighed the same (p. 105). The boundary of that concern is the capacity to suffer or enjoy, which Bentham marked with three questions, "Can they reason? nor Can they talk? but, Can they suffer?" (p. 106); a stone has no interests because it cannot suffer, a mouse has an interest in not being tormented, and sentience is the only defensible boundary; any other, like intelligence, is as arbitrary as skin colour (p. 107). Racists, sexists and speciesists give more weight to the interests of their own group: the pattern is the same in each case (p. 107). Two practices show it: eating animals for the taste, and experimenting on them. The experimenter's test is this: would he perform his experiment on an orphaned human infant if that were the only way to save many lives? If not, his readiness to use animals is simple discrimination, since adult apes, cats, mice and other mammals are more aware of what is happening to them, more self-directing, and at least as sensitive to pain as any infant; and if he answers that the infant will grow into more, the same question returns with a human who has severe and irreversible brain damage (pp. 109–110). Philosophers who defend the species line, Singer says, do so by forgetting these humans or by a move like Stanley Benn's, that rationality is "the human norm"; and he closes with an exercise: take Benn's passage, substitute "white" for "men", "black" for "dog", "high I.Q." for "rationality", and finally change "species" to "race", then re-read it (pp. 113–116). Killing is a footnote (fn. 7).

What the game does with it

You are the scientist in the paper's thought experiment (pp. 109–110), and the game begins there. Three experiments come to your desk, with rising stakes: one would spare a child a year of illness, one would save a life, one would save a thousand lives. The stories change from play to play (three for each rung); the cost never does: whoever is used will be in bad pain for a day and then be fine again, with no lasting harm. Four subjects are on offer every time, the paper's own four: a baby a few days old with no parents (Bentham's "infant of a day", the paper's "orphaned human infant"), a grown-up whose brain is badly hurt for good (the paper's "severe and irreversible brain damage"), a chimpanzee (the paper's "adult apes") and a mouse. On each experiment you tick everyone you would be willing to use, or nobody. From the three answers the board reads, for each of the four, the smallest good for which you would use them, or "never". That is your line, and it is revealed by what you did rather than declared; nothing can change it now except playing again. The pain is the same for all four, so the only thing your order of use can be about is who they are.

Then three rules with marks come to the board one at a time: Bentham's "Can they reason?" and "Can they talk?" as the paper quotes them, and the one the paper adds in its own voice, Aware ("aware of what is happening to them, more self-directing"). A rule is a way of deciding whom to use first: Reason's rule is "the less a being can think, the sooner you may use it". Each rule sorts the four by its marks, puts your rung beside each one, and checks the order: it agrees if, whenever you would use one subject sooner than another, the one used sooner has the lower mark (or the same mark); it objects if a subject with the higher mark is used sooner, or if two it cannot tell apart are used at different rungs, and it circles the two. The marks are stylised: the orderings the game relies on are the ones the paper states (a horse or dog above the baby on reason and talk; apes and mice above the baby on awareness; the chimpanzee, dog and pig above the hurt grown-up and the baby on awareness and intelligence; the grown-up above all of them), and the lengths are ours. The calibration check shows that with random marks that keep only those orderings, Aware still objects to every line that uses the animals before the people.

Then one more question with no marks, Bentham's, "Can they suffer?", asked yes or no for each of the four. The Suffer rule is the paper's: if a being can suffer, its pain counts as much as anyone's, so among beings that can suffer none may be used sooner than another. It objects wherever you have said two beings can suffer and would use one of them sooner; the complaint is built from your own two answers, which is the paper's argument in one move. Where you have said "no", the board notes that Singer disputes the fact (the mouse "at least as sensitive to pain as any human infant"). The board then names your line. All four at the same rung and all can suffer: Singer's line, equal consideration. All four at the same rung but some cannot suffer, you say: alike by your own answers, with the fact in dispute. An order some rule with marks gives: a Reason line, say, and the paper's reply that a mark like intelligence is as arbitrary as skin colour for whose pain may be spent. The animals before the people, which no rule gives: Species, the line most people draw, and the sponge writes Race and Sex over the name, which is the paper's own closing exercise (change "species" to "race" and re-read, p. 116) and its opening (the rights of women mocked with the rights of brutes, p. 103). Any other order: your own, with Singer's question, what do the ones you would use first have in common? Nobody at any price: no quarrel with Singer about animals at all, but a different question, whether a day of pain for one may ever be traded for a thousand lives; that is Philippa Foot's paper, also on this shelf, and the board says so. The verdict is about what you did: your order of use, what each rule said of it, your two Suffer answers side by side, the name, where you and Singer part, and the two practices the paper names, experiments and eating.

Mechanism and claim boundaries

Kind: toy. A model built to illustrate the paper's argument, not the paper's model (it has none) and not its data (it has none). What the game demonstrates: that on the paper's own four subjects, ranked as the paper ranks them, no rule with marks gives the order most people use, the animals before the people, while the Suffer rule objects to any order among beings that can suffer; that every one of the 256 lines three experiments can reveal, with every one of the 16 answer sets to "can they suffer?", gets a name, a check by each rule and a verdict; and that a player who says a being can suffer and would use it sooner than another who can has said two things the Suffer rule cannot both accept. The calibration check proves this for every line and for five hundred random casts that keep only the paper's stated orderings. What the game assumes: the four subjects, the three rungs and their stories, the fixed cost (a day of pain, no lasting harm, which keeps the question about pain and not about death), the three rules, the lengths of the marks, and the wording of the paper's answers. What the paper establishes: the argument, as an argument: it is a philosopher's case, not a finding about the world, and a reader who rejects a premise (that equality is a prescription, that suffering is the boundary) rejects the line too. The game shows where the line goes under the paper's premises; it does not show that the premises are right. A player who would use nobody has not drawn a line between the four at all; the game sends that player to Foot, not to Singer.

What is left out

The passages on race, sex and genetics (pp. 104–105); the description of intensive farming (pp. 108–109); the inference that animals feel pain, which the game takes as read; the survey of philosophers other than Benn (pp. 111–113); the third form of speciesism, the one in philosophy departments (p. 110); and footnote 7 on killing.
