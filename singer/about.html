What the paper says

"Animal Liberation" may sound like a parody of liberation movements, as "the rights of animals" was once used to parody the rights of women. Singer asks what the case for equality rests on. Not on facts: humans differ in every capacity, and if equality rested on sameness, a sophisticated opponent could draw his line at an IQ of 100 and we would have no reply. Equality is a prescription, not a description: each to count for one, the like interests of every being affected weighed the same. Equal consideration does not mean equal treatment; concern for a child means teaching it to read, concern for a pig means leaving it with other pigs. The only defensible boundary of concern is the capacity to suffer or enjoy: if a being suffers, there is no justification for refusing to count that suffering, and if it cannot, there is nothing to count. Racists give more weight to the interests of their own race, sexists to their own sex, speciesists to their own species: the pattern is identical. Animals feel pain: the same signs we go on for other humans, the same nervous systems, the same evolutionary reason. "The same amount of pain" is defined by the blow that produces it, a slap for a baby, a heavy stick for a horse, and that amount counts the same whoever feels it. Killing is another matter: the value of a life depends on self-awareness, plans for a future, close ties, so a normal adult is normally saved over a mouse; but whatever criteria we choose, they do not follow the boundary of our species: a chimpanzee, dog or pig surpasses an infant born with massive brain damage, or a person in advanced senility, and the only thing that distinguishes the infant from the animal is membership of Homo sapiens, which is pure speciesism. With one dose of painkiller and two injured humans of different capacities, it is not so clear how to choose: the evil of pain is unaffected by the other characteristics of the one who feels it; the value of life is affected by them. Two practices cause more suffering than anything else we do: experiments on animals, and rearing them for food.

What the game does with it

You are the "we" of the paper's first page: you want to keep every person in and every animal out, and have to say with which ruler. The eleven on the board are the paper's own examples, named in its words: a normal adult; a baby; an infant born with massive brain damage; an adult with a severe intellectual disability; someone in a state of advanced senility; a chimpanzee, a dog, a pig, a horse, a mouse; and an oak, our instance of "a being not capable of suffering", which the paper names as a case and does not instance. The four rulers are the paper's own criteria: reasoning, self-awareness and plans for a future, close ties, and the capacity to feel pain. The marks are stylised: the orderings the game relies on are the ones the paper states (the chimpanzee, dog and pig above the infant and the senile on self-awareness; some intellectually disabled people never reaching the intelligence of a dog; an adult above a mouse on plans and ties; the infant whose parents want it dead, at the bottom on ties), and the lengths are ours. One length we chose without the paper: the adult with a severe intellectual disability stands above the chimpanzee on Plans. The fifth ruler is species, and the two words the sponge writes over it are the paper's own comparison. On the second board the four scenes are the paper's: the slap and the heavy stick (the pig stands in for the horse); the painkiller with enough for one; the human and the mouse; the infant and the chimpanzee, where the paper says "there may be special cases in which the reverse holds true". The paper's answers are the paper's where it gives one, "either" where it says "it is not so clear".

Mechanism and claim boundaries

Kind: toy. A model built to illustrate the paper's argument, not the paper's model (it has none) and not its data (it has none). What the game demonstrates: that on a cast of the paper's own examples, ranked as the paper ranks them, no line on any measured ruler keeps every person in and every animal out, and that the only line that does is the species itself; and that pain and life are counted on two different scales in the paper's examples. The calibration check proves the first claim for every line on every ruler, and for five hundred random casts that keep only the paper's stated orderings. What the game assumes: the cast, the four rulers, the lengths of the marks, the oak, the pig standing in for the horse, the pain amounts in the two dose scenes, and the wording of the paper's answers. What the paper establishes: the argument, as an argument: it is a philosopher's case, not a finding about the world, and a reader who rejects a premise (that equality is a prescription, that suffering is the boundary) rejects the line too. The game shows where the line goes under the paper's premises; it does not show that the premises are right. Where the game says "the paper", it means this excerpt.

What is left out

The argument about experiments (adults would dread being kidnapped, infants and animals would not, so a reason that is not speciesist favours animals over adults, and infants over both); the inference that animals feel pain, which the game takes as read; the paper's remarks on race and sex averages; the list of other practices; the religious note; the two chapters the excerpt announces.
