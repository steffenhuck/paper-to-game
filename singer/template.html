A reading on the board · New York, 1974

Draw a Line with Singer

You are a scientist. Three experiments are on your desk, and each one would help people, some a little, some a lot. Each experiment needs a subject: someone it is done to, and it hurts. Each experiment comes with its own menu of who is on offer, nine creatures in all, from a grown-up to a mouse. You decide who may be used, and for what. Then Singer looks at your three choices and asks whether any one rule can make sense of them. Then he asks one more question of his own.

Whose pain would you spend? Draw the line.

How to play

Whose pain would you spend? Draw the line.

The experiments

You are a scientist, and three experiments come to your desk, one after the other. The first would spare one child a year of illness. The second would save one life. The third would save a thousand lives. Each experiment needs a subject: someone it is done to. Whoever you use will be in bad pain for a day, and then be fine again, with no lasting harm. Each experiment has its own menu of who is on offer, three creatures; across the three menus you meet nine: a grown-up; a baby a few days old, with no parents; a grown-up whose brain is badly hurt, for good; a grown-up who cannot learn much; a chimpanzee; a dog; a pig; a horse; and a mouse. Every one of them is an example from Singer's paper.

On each experiment, tap everyone you would be willing to use for it. You may tap one, several, or nobody at all. Tap again to change your mind. Then tap Done. Nothing you tick is done to anyone; the game only wants to know where your line is.

Making sense of your choices

Then the board tries to make sense of what you did. A rule is a way of deciding whom to use first. Reason's rule is: the less a being can think, the sooner you may use it. A rule with marks explains an experiment if it could have made that choice: that is, if everyone you would use there has a lower mark than everyone you would spare there. It explains your three choices if it explains all three experiments. The board first tells you how many of its three rules can explain all three of your choices, without saying which. Then the rules come to the board one at a time: Reason, Talk and Aware. Each one sorts the nine by its marks (the marks follow what Singer's paper says) and shows what you did on each menu. In each column, the rule is happy if every ✓ sits below every ✗; it complains about a column where a ✓ sits above a ✗. Before it speaks, you guess: tap the experiments you think it will complain about, then tap Check (or tap Check straight away if you think it has no complaint). Then the board tells you, experiment by experiment. Where the rule is fine, it draws a short red mark in that column: its cutoff, with the ones you would use below it. Where it complains, it circles the two creatures in that column it complains about most: one you would use although it has the higher mark, one you would spare although it has the lower mark. The circles are the rule's complaint, not the board's.

Then one more question, with no marks: can they suffer? Tap yes or no for each of the nine. The stone, which was on no menu, appears here once: nobody asks whether it can suffer, and that is where Singer's line runs. The Suffer rule is this: if a being can suffer, its pain counts as much as anyone's, so among beings that can suffer none may be used sooner than another; whatever cannot suffer may be used freely. If you say two beings can suffer and you would use one of them and spare the other, Suffer objects, and the circles are drawn from your own two answers.

The name, and the verdict

The board tells you what your line is called, and what Singer says about it. Then the verdict: what you did, what each rule said, your line and its name, where you and Singer part, and what follows. No score. Play again to draw a different line; the stories change each time.
