== START: Draw a Line with Singer. Who counts? Draw the line. [Pick up the chalk]
== BOARD ONE. Who counts? Tap everyone who counts. The rest are out.
   The ten: grown-up, baby (a few days old, no parents), grown-up (brain badly hurt, for good), grown-up (who cannot learn much), chimpanzee, dog, pig, horse, mouse, stone
   You tap: the grown-up, the baby, the grown-up whose brain is badly hurt and the grown-up who cannot learn much. [Done]
   Your line is drawn. Now the rulers. Could a ruler have drawn your line?

-- Rule 1: Reason comes to the board. Suppose Reason were your rule: whoever can think well enough counts as much as you, and whoever cannot counts for less. The ten sort by it; your dots ride along.
      1. ● grown-up                                 ########## 10
      2. ○ chimpanzee                               ######     6
      3. ○ pig                                      ####       4
      4. ● grown-up (who cannot learn much)         ###        3  (= same as the next)
      5. ○ dog                                      ###        3
      6. ○ horse                                    ##         2
      7. ● baby (a few days old, no parents)        #          1  (= same as the next)
      8. ○ mouse                                    #          1
      9. ● grown-up (brain badly hurt, for good)               0  (= same as the next)
     10. ○ stone                                               0
   At once: red circles on the grown-up whose brain is badly hurt and the chimpanzee. Reason objects. By Reason, the grown-up whose brain is badly hurt has a mark of 0 and the chimpanzee has a mark of 6. Your line counts the grown-up whose brain is badly hurt as much as you and the chimpanzee for less. That is the wrong way round: if Reason were your rule, the chimpanzee, with the higher mark, would have to count at least as much as the grown-up whose brain is badly hurt. The red circles are Reason's complaints. So Reason cannot be the rule behind your line.
   You: [Keep my line]

-- One more question: can they suffer? Asked about the chimpanzee, the dog, the pig, the horse and the mouse. You answer: the chimpanzee yes, the dog yes, the pig yes, the horse yes, the mouse yes.
   Board: Suffer objects. You say the chimpanzee, the dog, the pig, the horse and the mouse can suffer. Your line counts their pain for less than yours. The Suffer rule has no "less" in it: if a being can suffer, its pain weighs the same as anyone's, yours included. The red circles are Suffer's complaints, and this time they are built from your own two answers: it can suffer, and it counts for less.
   You: [Keep my line]

-- No rule moved you. Four rules came to the board, and your line never changed: the grown-up, the baby, the grown-up whose brain is badly hurt and the grown-up who cannot learn much counting as much as you, every time. Three times a rule with marks put an animal above a person you count as much as yourself, and you kept the line where it was. Then Suffer asked, and you kept it again. That is a line drawn by hand, and it has a name. You said nobody can suffer, and when Suffer objected you moved them in.
   Headline: No rule gives your line.  Column headed: SPECIES
   Board: Your full dots are the four people, and nobody else. That is the line most people draw, and it is drawn in red. Three rules with marks objected to it: on every one of them an animal has a higher mark than a person you count as much as yourself. And Suffer objected too, or would have, because the animals can suffer. So no rule you can check gives this line. It has a name all the same. It is the line of the team you happen to be on: Species.
   [And then…] The sponge writes RACE, then SEX, over the four-people line. The pattern is the same in each case. Singer's answer: can it suffer?

== BOARD TWO. Now you are the scientist. One experiment would save many lives. It needs one subject: someone it is done to, and it will hurt.
   Singer asks: One experiment would save many lives. It needs one subject: someone it is done to, and it will hurt. Who?
   You: chimp
   Singer asks: On your last line the baby's pain counts as much as yours, and the chimpanzee's counts for less. Why the chimpanzee, and not the baby?  [Because the baby is one of us, and the chimpanzee is not. | Because the baby will grow into more than the chimpanzee ever will. | Because the chimpanzee's pain counts for less than the baby's. | No reason. The baby would have done just as well.]
   You: human
   Board: You used the chimpanzee, not the baby, because the baby is one of us and the chimpanzee is not.
   Singer: On every rule with marks, the chimpanzee was above the baby: it knows more about what is happening to it, and it can suffer just as the baby can. The only thing that put the baby out of reach is the team it is on. Singer's name for that is Species, the word the sponge wiped off the board, and he says it is the same pattern as the racist's.  (status: species)

== VERDICT. Large: No rule moved you. Your hand drew the line.
   What Singer says: Whose pain counts as much as yours? Singer's answer: everyone's who can suffer. Not because they are like you in any other way, but because pain is pain, whoever feels it. No rule you can check gives the line most people draw, the four people only. Reason, Talk and Aware all object to it, and Suffer objects to it too, because the animals can suffer. That line is drawn by hand. The team you are on is not a rule.
   Your lines: Your first line, with no rule on the board: the grown-up, the baby, the grown-up whose brain is badly hurt and the grown-up who cannot learn much counting as much as you (Species). Reason did not move you. Talk did not move you. Aware did not move you. Suffer: You said the chimpanzee, the dog, the pig, the horse and the mouse can suffer, and by the end you still counted the chimpanzee, the dog, the pig, the horse and the mouse for less. Those are your own two answers, side by side. Suffer did not move you. Your last line: the grown-up, the baby, the grown-up whose brain is badly hurt and the grown-up who cannot learn much counting as much as you. No rule gives your line. Its name: Species.
   What you did: You used the chimpanzee, not the baby, because the baby is one of us and the chimpanzee is not. On every rule with marks, the chimpanzee was above the baby: it knows more about what is happening to it, and it can suffer just as the baby can. The only thing that put the baby out of reach is the team it is on. Singer's name for that is Species, the word the sponge wiped off the board, and he says it is the same pattern as the racist's.
   Where you part: At the line. Three rules with marks showed you an animal above a person you count as much as yourself, and Suffer asked you whether the animals can suffer; none of it moved you. Singer's word for counting pain by the team it belongs to is speciesism, and he says it is the same pattern as racism and sexism. At the experiment: you drew the team's line again. You spent the chimpanzee's pain and kept the baby's, and the only difference between them you could name was the team.
   What follows: Singer has one more thing for you to do. Say out loud why the baby's pain counts in full and the chimpanzee's for less. Then say it again, with "white" where you said "human" or "person" and "black" where you said "animal". He says what it sounds like the second time is the reason to give the line up. Singer names two things that do not survive his line. Experiments on animals that nobody would do on a baby: he says almost none would be left. And eating animals because they taste good: he says that should stop, since the pleasure of the taste is small and the pain behind it is not.
