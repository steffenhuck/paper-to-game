HOW TO PLAY

WHO YOU ARE

The leader of the big centre-right party. The radical right is the small, loud party that wants every election to be about immigration. The pink balloon is its share of the vote.

WHAT YOU DO

  • Each day, three reporters ask a question. Tap the one you will answer.
  • If it's about immigration, pick how you answer: like them (say what the radical right says), your usual line (what your party said last time) or against them (take them on).
  • Anything else you answer with your party's line.

WHAT YOU SEE

After each answer the balloon grows or shrinks: that is what your campaign so far hands the radical right. The dashed ring is the 1-point line. Under it: held. Over it: not held. Tomorrow's headline is what voters read.

TWO CAMPAIGNS

A quiet year first, then the refugee crisis, when some days every question is about immigration. Six days each, about two minutes each.

ABOUT

Swen Hutter & Hanspeter Kriesi (2022). Politicising immigration in times of crisis. Journal of Ethnic and Migration Studies 48(2): 341–365. doi:10.1080/1369183X.2020.1853902. Game built from this paper; the paper is the authority where the two disagree.

What kind of game this is

A toy built on the paper's estimate. The rule that moves the balloon is the paper's Table 3 (Model 3): across 37 elections in 15 European countries, the change in the radical right's vote, set against how much more the main centre-right party talked about immigration and which way its position moved. The game treats that pattern as if it were a lever you pull. The paper's data are not an experiment.

It demonstrates what that estimate implies, answer by answer: talking more about immigration while sounding like the radical right goes with a bigger radical right; talking more on your old line goes with a somewhat bigger one; talking more against them goes with no change; moving position without talking more goes with no change.

It assumes, under this game's own rules: that each answer is worth 3.5 points of your campaign's attention to immigration (six of six reaches the edge of the paper's range); that "like them" and "against them" are the full one-point position shifts drawn in the paper's Figure 5; that the estimate can be read after each answer as if the campaign ended there; that the 1-point line, the questions and the crisis deck are ours. With smaller position shifts (the distances between the party groups' average positions in the paper's Table 2), "like them" is still worst and the forced crisis answers "against them" still stay under the line, but taking them on every single day would hand them about two points. That "against them" is harmless rests on the paper's interaction term, estimated from 37 elections (t = −2.18); without it, talking about immigration on any line helps them.

The paper establishes the association with country fixed effects, and reads it as the centre-right's accommodating strategy "contributing" to the radical right's success, provided the centre-right gives immigration increasing attention. It also shows that the radical right's own campaigning raises how much every other party talks about immigration, more so during the crisis, without moving their positions. That is why some days in the crisis every question is about immigration.

How the paper measured it

Two newspapers per country, two months before each national election, 2003–2018. Every sentence in which a party says something about an issue was coded as who, which issue, which way, about 100,000 in all. A party's attention to immigration is the share of its statements on it; its position is their average direction. Your answers are scored the same way.

Also in the paper, not in the game

The refugee crisis was neither necessary nor sufficient for a fight over immigration: Greece, the hardest-hit port of entry, barely argued about it in 2015, while Switzerland and Poland, hit less or not at all, did. Refugee arrivals helped radical-right upstarts much more than parties already established. The radical right is the main force putting immigration into campaigns everywhere.

What is cut or changed

Countries and real parties (yours is unnamed); talking less about immigration than last time (each campaign starts from zero immigration headlines); the previous-vote and country terms of the model (the balloon shows only the part of their gain the estimate attributes to your campaign; the 12% start is for display). The questions, answers, headlines and the radical right's lines are ours.
