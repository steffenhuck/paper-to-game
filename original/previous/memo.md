# The Original or the Copy — design memo

## 1. Paper

Swen Hutter & Hanspeter Kriesi (2022). "Politicising immigration in times of crisis." *Journal of Ethnic and Migration Studies* 48(2): 341–365. doi:[10.1080/1369183X.2020.1853902](https://doi.org/10.1080/1369183X.2020.1853902). Open access, CC BY.

Available: the PDF only, including the appendices. There was no data and no code. All values in Act 1 are read off Figure 3. All coefficients in Act 2 are copied from Table 3.

## 2. What the paper hands over

**Estimates**, from a content analysis of about 100,000 statements in newspaper coverage of 61 national election campaigns in 15 European countries, 2003–2018 (PolDem data). They come in three blocks:

- **Systemic (Figs. 1–3):** politicisation of immigration (salience × polarisation) by election, region and country, before and after the 2015 crisis.
- **Party level (Tables 1–2, Fig. 4):** the radical right is the main force putting immigration into public debate. It also raises the *salience* of immigration for every other party, but it does not shift their *positions*.
- **Electoral (Table 3, Fig. 5):** the change in the radical right's vote depends on two things. One is the inflow of refugees, which helps upstarts more than established parties. The other is the main centre-right party's strategy, through an interaction between how much it talks about immigration and which way it shifts.

Primary: Table 3 Model 3 and Figure 3. Secondary: the party-level results, which appear as text only.

## 3. The discovery, and its kind

There are two linked discoveries.

1. **Counterintuitive result (Fig. 3, p. 350).** The crisis is "neither a necessary nor a sufficient condition" for politicising immigration. Greece was the hardest-hit port of entry and did not politicise it. Switzerland and the Netherlands were hit less, and Poland was a bystander, yet all three politicised it heavily. What mattered was partisan mobilisation.
2. **A mechanism with a trade-off (Table 3 Model 3, Fig. 5).** If the centre-right talks more about immigration *and* moves towards the radical right's position, the radical right gains. Le Pen's adage: "the French choose the original, not the copy." Moving tougher without talking more makes no difference. Talking more while moving the other way makes no difference either.

## 4. The core narrative

In 2015 a great many refugees came to Europe. You might expect the countries that received the most to be the ones where the next election became a fight about immigration. That isn't what happened. Some countries that hardly saw a refugee fought about little else, and the country where most boats landed barely talked about it. The difference was a political party that wanted the fight. Then there is the big mainstream party on the right, which is afraid of losing voters to that newcomer. Its natural move is to sound like the newcomer and talk about immigration all the time. When it does that, the newcomer grows. Voters choose the original, not the copy.

## 5. Fidelity level and claim boundaries

This is a **mixed** game, and each act carries its own label.

- **Act 1: statistical, showing observations.** The bars are the paper's own measurements for real elections, read off Fig. 3 to about ±0.3. **Demonstrates:** where immigration was and was not politicised in the crisis elections. **Assumes:** our read-offs, and the paper's own benchmark (mean + 1 s.d.) as the line that counts as "a fight". **Establishes:** the paper's measurements from newspaper coverage. The one-line reasons per country paraphrase the paper's discussion on p. 350, plus its classification of Fidesz and PiS as radical right (note 6).
- **Act 2: illustrative, built on a statistical estimate.** The response surface is Table 3 Model 3 exactly. **Demonstrates:** what that estimate implies if each campaign choice is read as a lever. **Assumes:** that the association across 37 elections is causal; that the linear model holds for the three talk steps and three lean steps offered; and an illustrative starting vote share of 12%. **Establishes:** the paper reports an association with country fixed effects in 37 elections, not an experiment. Its own reading is causal in tone ("contributes to"). The game says, at the moment of the reveal, that this is a pattern across 37 elections, not an experiment.

The game reports point estimates, and the uncertainty is wide (Fig. 5 bands). The game says so.

## 6. The aim and the verdict

**Aim (Act 1):** *Bet on where the 2015 crisis turned the next election into a fight over immigration.*
**Aim (Act 2):** *You run the big centre-right party. In each campaign, keep the radical right from gaining more than 1 point.*

The start screen carries one combined aim: *First bet where the refugee crisis set off a fight over immigration. Then run three campaigns against a rising radical right, and keep it from gaining more than a point each time.*

**Verdict (end screen, first lines):**
- What you did: "You called X of 8 countries. You held the radical right in Y of 3 campaigns."
- What the paper says: "The refugees didn't decide where immigration became the fight; parties did. And when the centre-right talks it up while sounding like the radical right, the radical right grows."
- What that means: "Voters choose the original, not the copy."

## 7. Concepts

1. **Two acts, Forecaster then Controller (picked).** Act 1 is a bet and a card, over eight real countries. The player deliberates over the obvious rule ("hit hard → fight") and has to commit to it. Act 2 is a challenge with two controls, talk and lean, whose *interaction* is the finding. Three campaigns follow. The first is free. The second fixes the lean at "tougher", so the player has to find out whether talk matters. The third forces "talk more", so the player has to find out whether lean matters. Fun comes from anticipation (the bars rise), agency (two dials, visible response), discovery (Greece; the copy backfires) and some mastery (campaigns 2–3 use what campaign 1 taught).
2. **Experimenter on the party system (not picked).** The player toggles the radical right on and off in a country and watches other parties' salience rise (Table 2 Model 5). That is salience contagion. It is honest but abstract, there is little to decide, and the coefficients are on a scale strangers can't read.
3. **The radical right as agent (not picked).** The player plays the newcomer party, choosing how hard to push immigration, while refugee inflows arrive. The payoff is votes, from Table 3 Model 1 (declining marginal returns: upstarts gain, established parties gain little). It is attractive, but the vote equation has only 45 observations and needs a refugee inflow scale the paper does not report. The lesson ("you've already maxed out") is weaker than "the copy feeds the original".

## 8. The first five minutes (pick)

*0:00.* A front page reads "Refugee crisis: Europe's elections loom". The title is *The Original or the Copy*, with the aim on its own line. They tap Start.

*0:20.* Eight country cards appear. Each has a country name, the election year and one line on how the crisis hit it: "Greece: hardest-hit port of entry." "Poland: a bystander." The question is "Did the campaign turn into a fight over immigration?", answered with two buttons on each card. They think "obviously the hard-hit ones", and hesitate over Switzerland. They tap all eight and press "Reveal".

*1:10.* Bars grow from each card to the paper's measure, with a dashed line marking "a fight". Greece barely moves. Switzerland goes off the top. The score reads "5 of 8" in big type. A line underneath: "Hit hard didn't mean a fight; a bet on 'hit hard' scores 4 of 8. What the fighters had was a party that made it its campaign." Tapping a card shows its reason.

*1:50.* Act 2. "You run the big centre-right party. A radical-right party is rising on immigration." The radical right's bar stands at 12%. There are two dials. *Talk about immigration:* same / more (+10 points of your campaign) / much more (+20). *Your line:* tougher, closer to theirs / hold / more open. The aim reads "keep them from gaining more than 1 point". Most players think "steal their issue". They pick much more and tougher, and a headline mock-up shows it: "CENTRE-RIGHT VOWS TOUGH LINE ON MIGRANTS".

*2:20.* The radical right's bar swells from 12 to 25.8%. A red "Not held". The line: "Voters chose the original, not the copy." A frown, and "really?"

*2:50.* Campaign 2: "Your base demands a tougher line." The lean is locked at tougher. The thinking: "talking about it is what hurt?" They try "same" and the bar barely moves, +0.8: held. The penny drops a little.

*3:30.* Campaign 3: "The crisis is all over the news. You must talk about it more." Talk is locked at more or much more. Which line? Tougher just hurt. They try "more open" and the bar goes down a hair: held. A note follows: "This rests on the paper's main estimate across 37 elections, a pattern, not an experiment."

*4:10.* The end screen. The verdict comes first in large type, then the 3 × 3 map of all nine campaigns from the paper's estimate, then "Play again" and "About".

**Where the grin is:** Greece, and the backfire. **Where the frown is:** "I wasn't allowed to say less." That is answered on the Help and About screens: in the crisis campaigns, dropping the issue was hardly an option (p. 345).

## 9. The pick, and why

Concept 1. It has two real commitments before seeing, both discoveries land within four minutes, and the two controls are justified because their interaction *is* the finding. The runner-up is concept 3. I would switch if the author wants the declining-marginal-returns result to carry the game.

## 10. The shape

- **Screens:** Start, Act 1 bets, Act 1 reveal, Act 2 campaign (×3: choose → result), End, Help, About.
- **No presets beyond the paper's:** Act 1 uses eight of the paper's fifteen countries. They are the ones its text classifies by how hard the crisis hit (p. 350), shown in an order that mixes the hit levels. Act 2's dial values are the ±1 lines in Fig. 5 and salience steps inside its axis range.
- **Order:** Act 1 comes first because its surprise is quick and sets up "parties decide". In Act 2 the free campaign comes first, so the intuitive copy strategy is available to be tried and to fail.
- **Score:** Act 1 is X of 8, in the aim's units. Act 2 gives held or not held per campaign, with the change in points shown. There is no running total.

## 11. Controls, mechanism, feedback, moment

- **Controls:** Act 1 has two buttons per country. Act 2 has two three-way selectors and a "Run the campaign" button.
- **Mechanism:** Act 1 is the observed values against the paper's benchmark. Act 2 is ΔRR = 31.92·Δs − 0.757·Δp − 33.05·Δs·Δp, from Table 3 Model 3.
- **Feedback:** Act 1 uses the reveal, with bars growing from the cards. Act 2 uses the race: two bars, the radical right before and after, plus a headline mock-up of the chosen campaign.
- **Moments:** the Greece and Switzerland bars, and the 12 → 25.8 swell.

## 12. What is cut

- Salience vs polarisation as separate measures (Fig. 1); regional trends (Fig. 2); the pre-/post-crisis difference bars (Fig. 3, dark).
- The party-level regressions (Tables 1–2), which are described in About only. These show that the radical right drives salience contagion but not position contagion.
- Refugee inflow and declining marginal returns (Table 3 Model 1), in About only.
- Seven of the fifteen countries. Spain, Italy, France, the UK, Portugal, Romania and Latvia are left out because the paper does not classify their crisis "hit" as clearly. Italy is hit hard and politicised; it is cut to keep eight cards on a phone.
- A "talk less" option in Act 2.

## 13. Divergences

- **No "talk less".** The paper's reading is that in the crisis campaigns, ignoring immigration "was hardly an option" for mainstream parties (p. 345). Its own sentence concerns a centre-right that "does not pay increasing attention". Extending the linear interaction to large *cuts* in salience would imply that "quiet and tough" shrinks the radical right by about 12 points. Fig. 5 draws that, but the paper's text does not claim it, so we leave it out rather than let the game's optimum rest on extrapolation.
- **Lean steps of ±1** are the values Fig. 5 plots. That is half the full −1..+1 scale, a big shift. We label it as "a big step".
- **The radical right starts at 12%.** This is our choice, for display only. The model predicts *changes*.
- **The "1 point" limit** is our choice. Any limit from 0.77 to 3.18 gives the same wins and losses.
- **Campaign constraints** (base demands tougher; crisis forces more talk) are our scenarios. They select which half of the paper's interaction the player must use. They are not results.

## 14. Visual world

Newsprint: the paper's data *is* newspaper coverage. Rules:
1. An off-white newsprint ground, black ink, and one serif-free bold headline face from system fonts.
2. Two party colours only: radical right in a hot red-brown, centre-right in deep blue. Countries are neutral.
3. Few, big things. Eight country cards, each with one big bar, in Act 1. In Act 2, one headline and two big bars, before and after.
4. Every bar is labelled with its value in the aim's units ("12% → 25.8%").

**Legibility:** in Act 1 the player looks at eight full-width cards, each 60 px tall, answering "did it become a fight?". In Act 2 they look at a 70 px headline answering "what did I just do?" and two 44 px bars answering "did it work?".

## 15. Fidelity plan

`fidelity.js` runs `mechanism.js` in Node and checks the following.
- Model 3 against Fig. 5 read-offs: the slope of the −1 line (~65 pp per unit salience), its endpoints (~−11, ~+15), the flatness of the +1 line, and the gap at zero.
- The paper's sentences on p. 355: no effect of position without rising salience; loud + tough is the largest gain; adversarial + loud brings no gain.
- Act 1's split against the paper's text on p. 350 (Austria, Germany, Hungary heavy; Greece not; Netherlands, Switzerland, Poland yes; Ireland not). The naive rule scores 4 of 8.
- Robustness: the Act 1 split under other fight lines; the Act 2 wins and losses under other hold limits; Act 2 under Model 2 (no interaction).
- Determinism.

**Robustness.**
- The Act 1 split is the same for any fight line from 0.6 to 5.7.

- **Not robust:** "talk more but lean open is safe" depends on Model 3's interaction. Under Model 2 it would be +2.25. The game says so in the result text at that moment, and on the About screen.
