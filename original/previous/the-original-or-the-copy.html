How to play

The setting. In 2015 Europe faced what came to be called the migration crisis. Elections followed across Europe over the next three years. A study read the newspapers' campaign coverage in 15 countries and measured how much parties fought about immigration.

Part 1. Eight countries, each with a line on how hard the crisis hit it. Bet whether its next election turned into a fight over immigration: one of the top issues, with parties loudly on opposite sides. The study's yardstick is how much parties talked about it, times how far apart they stood. A dashed line marks the point where an issue is usually among a campaign's top three. Reveal shows the study's measurement. You score how many of the eight you called right.

Part 2. You run the big centre-right party (think Christian democrats or conservatives). A radical-right party is campaigning against immigration. For each of three campaigns you set two dials:

  • Talk: how much of your campaign is about immigration: the same as last time, 10 points more, or 20 points more.
  • Move: tougher (a big step towards the radical right's line), hold, or more open (a big step away from it).

The radical right's vote then changes as the study's estimate says it goes with your choice. Your aim in each campaign is to keep its gain to 1 point or less. Campaigns 2 and 3 fix one of the dials, so you have to get the other one right. There is no "talk less" option. In the crisis campaigns, ignoring the issue was hardly an option for a big party.

What the game is trying to show. What decided where immigration became the fight, and what a mainstream party's campaign does to the radical right.

About the paper

The paper in one paragraph

Hutter and Kriesi coded about 100,000 statements by parties in newspaper coverage of 61 national election campaigns in 15 European countries, from the early 2000s to 2018. They ask three questions. Did the 2015 migration crisis make immigration a bigger fight? Is the radical right still the party driving it? And when does the radical right profit at the ballot box?

What it found

The crisis raised the fight everywhere, but did not decide where. After 2015 immigration became one of the most fought-over issues across Europe. Yet the crisis was "neither a necessary nor a sufficient condition". Greece, the hardest-hit port of entry, hardly argued about it, because the bailout crowded out everything else. Switzerland, the Netherlands and Poland, which were hit less or not at all, fought hard. Partisan mobilisation decided.

The radical right drives it. It is the main party putting immigration into the debate, against immigration. When it does, every other party talks about immigration more, especially during the crisis, but other parties do not shift their positions towards it.

The centre-right's strategy matters, through an interaction. When the main centre-right party gave immigration more attention and moved towards the radical right's position, the radical right gained. Moving without more attention made no difference, and neither did more attention combined with a move the other way. The paper quotes Jean-Marie Le Pen: "the French choose the original, not the copy." The paper also finds that a rise in refugee numbers helped new radical-right parties much more than established ones. That result is not in the game.

Fidelity

Part 1 is statistical, and shows observations. Each bar is the paper's own measure for that country's first election after the crisis, read off its Figure 3 (to about ±0.3). The measure is salience × polarisation of immigration in campaign coverage. The dashed line is the paper's benchmark: the mean plus one standard deviation over all 17 issues. The hit levels and the reasons are paraphrased from the paper's discussion (p. 350). The paper counts Fidesz and PiS as radical right, and says this is a contested choice. It demonstrates where immigration was and was not fought over. It assumes our read-offs. The split between fight and no fight is the same for any line from 0.6 to 5.7. The paper establishes these measurements from two newspapers per country.

Part 2 is illustrative, built on the paper's estimate. The radical right's change in vote is Table 3, Model 3, exactly: 31.92 × (change in centre-right attention) − 0.757 × (change in its position) − 33.05 × (the two multiplied). Attention is the share of the party's campaign statements on immigration. Position runs from −1 (fully against) to +1 (fully for). It demonstrates what that estimate implies if each choice were a lever. It assumes that the pattern is causal, that the straight-line model holds for the steps offered, and a starting vote of 12%, which is ours and for display only. The paper establishes an association across 37 elections with country fixed effects, not an experiment, and its uncertainty is wide. The "move more open" result rests on the interaction term alone (t = −2.18). A simpler model without it (Model 2) would show talking more always helping the radical right, by about 2 points with a move away from them. The "copy" result holds in both models.

Our choices

The 1-point aim is ours. Any limit from 0.8 to 3.1 gives the same wins and losses. There is no "talk less" option. The paper notes that in the crisis ignoring immigration "was hardly an option" for mainstream parties, and its claim concerns a centre-right that does or does not pay increasing attention. Stretching the straight line to big cuts in attention would claim more than the paper does. The tougher and more-open steps (±1) are the two lines the paper plots in its Figure 5. Campaigns 2 and 3 each fix one dial. These are our scenarios, not findings. We show eight of the 15 countries: the ones whose crisis "hit" the paper describes.

What is cut

Salience and polarisation shown separately; regional trends; the before-and-after comparison by country; the party-level regressions (Tables 1–2), which are summarised above; refugee inflows and the upstart-versus-established result (Table 3, Model 1).
