About

The paper. G. W. Leibniz, "On the Way of Perfecting Medicine": an extract from a letter to Germain Brice, printed in the Journal des sçavans, 26 July 1694, pp. 338–340. Read in Lloyd Strickland's translation from the French (© 2019), published at leibniz-translations.com; the game's words for Leibniz's ideas lean on his. No DOI. Game built from this paper; the paper is the authority where the two disagree.

What the paper says

Leibniz asks whether anyone in Paris is still keeping the registers of baptisms and funerals, and points to the Bills of Mortality in London, from which able people have drawn observations. One could go further: an annual history of medicine for each province, on the plan of Bernardino Ramazzini in Modena, who writes first of the seasons and the constitution of the air, then of how the grains and fruits did and what diseases the animals had, then of the chief matter, the health of human bodies, and above all of which medicines had the best effect, what harms and what helps. In two such years Ramazzini has already seen great variations, "as it were changes from black to white", quinquina (cinchona bark, the remedy for intermittent fevers) succeeding "incomparably better in one year than in the other". And years similar to past ones often return, so the past would be of the greatest use in deciding; "whereas now we are reduced to learning almost always from scratch at the expense of the sick". What a treasure trove there would be if this had been done for a while; but it is always time to begin good things. It costs scarcely any trouble: a letter of a few pages a year from an able practitioner in each province, to the First Doctor of the King, by public order. France, with its police, could do it best, and the king would deserve more by it than the kings who gave their names to a few medicines, or than Alfonso and Ulugh Beg by their astronomy. The soul and the body, he ends, are the first things that should be thought about, and the last that are.

What the game does

It gives you the practitioner's winter, ten times. A winter has a kind, and the kind is two things you can see: cold or mild, dry or wet. A hundred sick come with the winter's fever and you give them one remedy: bark, bleeding or rest. Left to itself the fever kills about twenty of a hundred, and the game says so. In every kind of winter one remedy helps (five die), one is no better than the fever alone (twenty), and one harms (thirty), and which is which depends only on the kind; rest is a treatment like the others, bed, warmth and broth by the doctor's order; the same bark helps in one kind and harms in another. You see what the remedy you gave did and nothing about the others. For four winters you have only your own past winters to go on, and you can see all of them: your memory is not the problem, your sample is. One province meets one winter a year, and a kind you have never had tells you nothing. Then Leibniz's letter arrives, and by public order every physician in every province writes a few lines a year to the First Doctor, who sends the collection back. From 1695 the box under the two cards says what the letters so far show for this kind of winter, twenty-four provinces' worth a year, and the whole collection is behind a button; by 1696 it holds the remedy that helps in every kind. The hard part dissolves, and that dissolving is what the game is for. The other physicians choose at random in the first year of letters and from then on give what the letters show helped in their kind, choosing at random only where the letters show nothing yet; so the first year's letters show what harmed as well as what helped, and the later ones converge. The draw is checked so that the letters of 1694 hold the remedy for your 1695 winter and the letters of 1694 and 1695 hold it for every kind.

The plan of your own ten winters is fixed and the kinds are drawn fresh each game: a winter that repeats, when trusting last winter is right; a lookalike, one card the same and the other different, when last winter's remedy harms; a new kind with nothing to read; and from 1695 winters the letters answer. At the end the game sets the dead of your five winters with nothing to read against the dead of your five with the letters, and your ten beside four other doctors on the same winters: one just guessing, a remedy at random every winter; one learning only from her own evidence, her own winters by kind, what helped there, what harmed, what is untried; one in a world with registers since 1690, as Leibniz wished it had been done "for a while now"; and an omniscient one, who knows what helps in every kind of winter from the start and so loses five of a hundred every winter, the floor. The score, the dead out of a thousand sick, is the game's; the unit, the sick, is Leibniz's.

What kind of mechanism this is

Toy. The game runs a model built to illustrate the paper's argument, and where the argument had to be made exact, the toy chose: a winter's kind is two cards; a remedy's effect depends on the kind and on nothing else; five, twenty and thirty of a hundred die under the remedy that helps, is no better than the fever alone, and harms, twenty being the fever's own toll; twenty-four other provinces, each with its own winter and a doctor who chooses at random until the letters show him what helps; and the plan of which kind comes to you when. There is no noise: the same remedy in the same kind of winter does the same thing every time, everywhere.

  • What the game demonstrates: that under these rules a doctor with only his own winters is right when the winter repeats and wrong when a lookalike comes; that a collection of every province's winters answers, within a year or two, what one province cannot learn in a decade; and that the collection cannot answer for the winters before it began. The headless check runs the four doctors over three hundred draws and finds the same order every time, with other sizes for the dead and with noise added.
  • What the game assumes: everything in the paragraph above, and in particular that the other doctors go on choosing at random after the letters arrive, so that the collection keeps showing harm as well as help. A doctor with only his own winters does about as well over the ten as one who gives the same remedy every winter; that is the plan's doing, and the order of the four doctors does not depend on it.
  • What the paper establishes: an argument, from Ramazzini's two years, that remedies vary from year to year with the constitution of the times, a claim that similar years often return, and a proposal: a few lines a year from every province, collected. It does not establish how remedies map to winters or any number in this game. On screen the game says "in this game", never "as Leibniz showed".

What is cut, and where the game departs

The Bills of Mortality and the Paris registers are a line on this screen. Ramazzini's seasons, grains and beasts are two cards; his dedication of his second year to Leibniz, the German doctors "curious about nature" and their Ephemerides, the Lieutenant of Police, Louis XIV, Mithridates, Juba and Gentius, Alfonso and Ulugh Beg are read here, not played. No patient is diagnosed: one fever a winter, a hundred sick, one remedy. The collection comes back the winter after it is written; Leibniz says only "every year". Bark is Leibniz's; bleeding and rest are the game's, so that the choice has three live options. The look is three hundred years after the letter, on purpose.

Making of

Built with Claude under the paper-to-game skill, November 2026, as one of a suite of Leibniz games. A first build (in previous/) had four kinds of year drawn from four seasons, a register of your own province only, and a memory that faded on screen; the author found the register thin and the fading beside the point, and this build replaced it. Three later revisions, after he played: the fever's own toll on screen; the First Doctor's digest on the play screen, twenty-four provinces and physicians who read; a verdict in two halves. Every number the game uses sits in a commented block at the top of mechanism.js, with the headless check that proves the plan's promises and the doctors' order over three hundred draws (calibration.js).
