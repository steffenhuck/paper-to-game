The Coal Question · 1866

Burn Bright
with Jevons

It is 1865. Britain runs on coal: its mills, its engines, its ships. Every year it burns more than the year before. The dotted line is what happens if that goes on.

All the coal Britain has is in the ground, drawn here as black squares. Burn it fast and it makes a big fire that runs out. Burn it slow and it makes a small fire that lasts. You decide the pace, one generation at a time, for ten generations; a ledger keeps what each one got, and the score is how well the typical generation did.

Choose for Britain: a brief greatness, or a long mediocrity.

How to play

Choose for Britain: a brief greatness, or a long mediocrity.

The sheet

It is graph paper, ruled like Jevons's own. The years run along the black line in the middle, from 1850 to 2070.

Above the line is Britain's fire: how much coal it burns in a year. The higher the curve, the bigger the fire. The black under the curve is coal that has been burnt, and you can count it in squares. If the fire grows taller than the paper, more paper is added at the top.

Below the line is the coal still in the ground: 167 black squares, in eight layers, down to 4,000 feet. One square below is the same amount of coal as one square above. When a square is burnt above, a square goes from below. The top layer is dug first. The deeper the digging, the dearer the coal.

The dashed line is America's coal. The hatched bar is people who went over the sea because there was no work for them. Orange is whatever is burning now.

The ledger at the bottom says what each generation got: its income per head (the bar and the big figure) and Britain's whole income (the small figure), both against 1865. Income comes from power, and power comes from coal and from how much work a ton of it does. Heads are all the people Britain would have had, those who went over the sea counted in. The last box, after, is what the coal still in the ground in 2065 could give ten more generations, burnt evenly.

Each generation

You set ten turns of twenty years, from 1865 to 2045. Slide along the ruler under the sheet to say how much coal Britain should burn twenty years from now: cut back (half as much), a stand (the same), slower (half as much again), as now (twice as much: Jevons's own pace in 1865) or faster. Under each mark the ruler says how long the coal would last at that pace. The dotted line on the sheet is your plan, and the crossed squares are the coal it would take. Then press Burn. The solid line is what actually burnt, and it may leave your dotted line.

The engines

In 1885 and 1925 a new engine appears that does the same work on less coal, but costs more to build. It pays where coal is dear, and it gets cheaper every year. Britain's works fit it as soon as it pays for them; America fits it when it has become cheap enough. Nobody chooses. Watch what the fire does when an engine comes in.

The price

The price of coal is told as the days of a year's earnings that fuel takes: about 18 days in 1865. Deeper pits make it more; a better engine makes it less. Works go where fuel takes fewer days.

The evening

If coal has grown so dear that the fire could not grow at any pace, the evening has come: the fire falls whatever you set, and the rest of the sheet draws itself.

The score

Your score asks: what even share, given to every generation alike from 1865 to the ones after 2065, would be worth as much as your sheet? A rich generation cannot make up for a poor one: we count so that ×4 for one generation and ×1 for the next is worth no more than ×2 for both. Your even share against the best path's, out of 100, is your score.

The last page shows the best path we found, dotted on your sheet and in hollow bars in your ledger, and says why it goes as it does. Then the curve, continued, and why it stopped.
