# Jevons's Noon: design memo

W. Stanley Jevons, *The Coal Question* (second edition, 1866): the preface, chapter I, chapter VII and chapter XVIII, with the frontispiece. Built under `SKILL.md` and `build.md`.

## 0. Given

**Mine.** Steffen's signature and counter (build.md, section 7).

**Ideas, from the author.**

> The text is The Coal Question (1866 edition); the argument is in the preface, chapters I and VII, and the last chapter. No biography. What people know is "the Jevons paradox", a tidy rule that efficiency raises consumption; what the text does is ask whether a nation should prefer a brief greatness to a long mediocrity, with coal running out either way. The game is about that question, not about climate, and the words stay in the 1860s.
> The look is Jevons's own: ruled graph paper, cream with faint blue rules, soot black for everything drawn, one hot colour, furnace orange, for whatever is burning. Curves that climb off the top of the sheet. No worlds, no branches, one sheet. The present enters once, on the last screen: the same curve continued past 1865 on the same paper, with the date, and nothing else said.

What this binds:

- **Content.** The four parts named, and the frontispiece. The game is built on XVIII's question ("Are we wise in allowing the commerce of this country to rise beyond the point at which we can long maintain it?") and its last sentence ("the momentous choice between brief greatness and longer continued mediocrity"). Chapter VII's economy of fuel is one beat inside it, a way out that the player may try and that fails as Jevons says it fails. It is not the spine. No Jevons as a person: he is named only as the author of the sentences quoted. Nothing about climate. Every word on screen is one an 1860s reader would use: tons, pits, works, engines, the trade, over the sea. No oil, no electricity, no wars, nothing after 1866, until the last screen, which has no words.
- **Look.** The register (Jevons's own statistical plate), the palette (cream, faint blue rules, soot, furnace orange), the curve leaving the top of the sheet, one sheet for the whole game, and the last screen as specified. **Override of precedent:** cream paper with blue rules is close to Goodall's squared notebook (one of the last three). The Given outranks the precedent rule, and the other dimensions are made to differ instead: solid soot instead of graphite hatching, an engraved Didone instead of a typewriter and a pencil hand, engraver's double-ruled labels instead of pencil-boxed slips, and one hot colour instead of red pencil.
- **Interpretation.** "No branches": one run of play, no story forks, no alternative endings drawn as worlds. The verdict changes with what the player did, but it is written on the same sheet. "The same curve continued past 1865": Jevons's curve is the coal raised from British pits each year (his 83.6 million tons for 1861 is the production return), so the last screen continues it with the United Kingdom's production, 1865 to 2024, drawn on the play sheet, ground and all, ending at the day's date. No heading, caption, legend or sentence on that screen.

## 1. Paper

W. Stanley Jevons, *The Coal Question: An Inquiry Concerning the Progress of the Nation, and the Probable Exhaustion of Our Coal-Mines*, 2nd edition, revised (London: Macmillan, 1866); first edition 1865. No DOI. Available: a PDF of the preface, chapters I, VII and XVIII and the frontispiece, from Patrick Draper's 2009 transcription (CC BY-SA 3.0). The full text can be read at the Online Library of Liberty / Econlib (econlib.org, *The Coal Question*) and in many scans on archive.org. One number the game uses comes from chapter II, outside the attached chapters (Hull's estimate of the coal within 4,000 feet); it is flagged where it is used.

## 2. What the paper hands over

Primary: **an argument**, with numbers. In a free country the consumption of coal multiplies at a constant rate (3½ per cent a year, the frontispiece); the coal within reach is fixed; exhaustion comes not as an empty cellar but as deeper pits and dearer coal (preface, ch. I); dear coal sends manufactures "to where fuel is cheapest" (preface), so Britain's lead passes to countries with cheap coal (America, Pennsylvania); economy of fuel does not spare coal, it multiplies its use and is open to rivals as much as to us (ch. VII); bringing a growing nation to a stand is "galling and perilous", it means "a sore pressure of population and a great exodus of our people" (preface); so the nation must choose "between brief greatness and longer continued mediocrity" (ch. XVIII), and whichever it chooses, the wealth of the day should pay "the moral and the social debts ... before the evening" (preface).

Secondary: **an estimate**, the frontispiece. Past consumption shaded, and the "supposed future consumption of coal at same rate of progress, shewing the impossibility of a long continuance of that progress": 83.6 million tons in 1861, 117.9 in 1871, 166.3, 234.7, 331, 466, 658, 929, 1,310, 1,848, and 2,607 in 1961. Plus the engine figures of chapter VII (a good engine burns about 4 lb of coal per horse-power per hour; an air-engine has worked at about half; the perfected air-engine would burn "less than one-third of the present consumption"), the duty table (efficiency up ten-fold in under a century), Scottish iron (a third of the coal per ton, ten times the coal), and the preface's 95 million tons for 1865.

The game formalises the argument as a toy (section 16).

## 3. The discovery, and its kind

**A trade-off**, with a counterintuitive beat inside it. The coal in the ground is fixed, so the shape of the nation's fire is the only thing it chooses: high and short, or low and long. Every way out that the 1860s offered to have both fails as the text says it fails. Better engines make the fire burn faster, not slower. Holding the fire level sends the young over the sea. Rivals with cheap coal pass you whatever you do. The end is not an empty pit but coal too dear to burn. The player is put on the frontier and chooses where to stand on it. The game does not rank the two ends, because Jevons does not: chapter XVIII argues each side in turn and closes on "the momentous choice".

## 4. The core narrative

A country has found a black stone that burns, and with it everything gets easier: engines turn, mills spin, ships cross the sea, and every year there is more work and more people. But the stone is buried in the ground, and there is only so much of it. The faster the country burns it, the bigger the country gets, and the sooner it has to dig very deep for the last of it, where it costs so much that the mills move to a country with stone lying near the top. If the country burns it slowly, the stone lasts for ages, but there is less work than there are people, and the young sail away to find it. Clever new engines that need less stone don't help: they make burning so cheap that people find a hundred new things to burn it for. So the country has to choose: a short, bright fire, or a long, small one.

## 5. Who lives in the paper

- **The nation** (the House, the two parties of ch. I): sets how far trade is let run. Conservatives would forbid coal exports, restrict trade and spare fuel; Liberals would leave consumption "unchecked even to the last". Controls the pace of the fire.
- **The engineer and the inventor** (Savery, Newcomen, Smeaton, Watt, "a second Watt" with an air-engine): makes each ton do more work. Controls the duty of the engine.
- **The ironmaster** (the blast furnace of ch. VII): less coal per ton of iron, more profit, more furnaces. Controls economy within one trade.
- **The coal-owner and the pit** (Mr Vivian's deep mining, Monkwearmouth, Dukinfield): goes deeper, at a cost that rises faster than the depth.
- **The householder at the grate**: the one consumer whose saving is real ("laid up for the use of posterity", ch. VII).
- **The people**: they multiply at a constant rate; without work they go over the sea or sink into poverty.
- **The rivals** (America, Pennsylvania): their coal is cheap and plentiful and they adopt every new engine.
- **Posterity**, to whom the debts are owed.
- **Jevons with his statistics**, extending a rate on ruled paper.

**What this game does differently.**

- **Position: agent, and the agent is a nation.** Not an experimenter measuring, not an animal or a soul being studied: the player is the country deciding how far it lets its trade run, generation by generation. *I let the fire double, fitted the air-engine, and held it level in 1905; it fell anyway when the pits got deep.*
- **Format: allocating a fixed stock across time, drawn as a curve.** The ground holds a fixed number of squares of coal, and the player spends them over six generations. The shape of the spending is the outcome. There is no score, and the verdict reads the shape in Jevons's words. Augustine was a run of measurements, Goodall a skill against a clock, Weil a life against a pendulum. Here the player draws.
- **Device: one of our own, the conservation sheet.** One graph-paper sheet with the time axis as the surface of the ground. Above it, the fire (tons a year), with the area under the curve shaded soot; below it, the coal still in the ground, in squares the same size as the squares above. Every square burnt above is a square gone below. The player's plan is drawn dotted (the pencil), and what actually burns is drawn solid (the ink). The gap between them is how the world answered: the ink climbs over the pencil when an engine comes in, and falls short of it when the pits get deep. No bars in memory, no stem or ruler, no figure or scales.
- **Chassis: Jevons's own statistical plate** (Instrument family: the paper's own diagram, drawn live). After a Bauhaus poster, a pencil notebook and a red-figure vase: lithographed chart lettering in an engraved Didone, solid soot fills, one hot colour, engraver's double-ruled labels.

## 7. The aim and the verdict

**The aim, as the player reads it:** *Choose for Britain: a brief greatness, or a long mediocrity.*

It is a decision, not a maximum, because Jevons ranks neither (SKILL.md, "The ending"). The yardstick is the sheet: the height of the curve (greatness), how far it runs before it falls (length), and the ground beneath (what is left). It is drawn in the aim's units at every step.

**The verdict** leads with the shape, named in Jevons's words, then three lines:

1. *What you did:* "You let the fire double every generation and fitted both new engines."
2. *What became of it:* "Your noon came in 1938, above the top of the sheet, at about 11 times the fire of 1865. By 1945 the pits were at about 2,000 feet and coal about half as dear again as in 1865; the works went where coal was cheap, and the fire fell whatever pace was set. In 1985 it was less than half its noon. America passed Britain in 1928. About 21 million people went over the sea for want of work, nearly all of them after your noon."
3. *What the paper says:* "We have to make the momentous choice between brief greatness and longer continued mediocrity."

Then Jevons's sentence for that end of the choice. The shape is named "a brief greatness" when the noon is at least five times the 1865 fire, "a longer continued mediocrity" when it is at most twice, and "between the two" otherwise (XVIII on greatness, "in fearlessly following our instincts of rapid growth ...", or on littleness, "Britain may contract to her former littleness ..."). Then the preface's sentence about noon: "We are now in the full morning of our national prosperity, and are approaching noon. Yet we have hardly begun to pay the moral and the social debts to millions of our countrymen which we must pay before the evening." Nothing is scored, ranked or called better.

## 8. Three concepts

**A. Jevons's Noon** (agent: the nation).
- *Format:* allocation of a fixed stock across six generations, drawn as a curve; no score.
- *Controls:* one ruler under the sheet sets the pace for the next twenty years, from "cut back" through "a stand" to "as now" and "faster". At 1885 and 1925 a new engine is offered (Jevons's air-engine, then the air-engine perfected): fit it, or refuse it.
- *Before pressing:* how high do I let it go this generation? I can see the ground this pace would take, the squares marked. Is this the generation to hold it, when holding means people leave and America draws closer? The engine does the same work on half the coal: will it make the coal last?
- *After:* the generation burns year by year. The ink follows the pencil or leaves it, the ground empties square by square, America's dashed line climbs, the hatched bar of people gone over the sea lengthens, and a line from the game says what happened.
- *Discovery moment:* at 1885 the player fits the engine to make the coal last and the ink climbs over the pencil, half as high again, with twice the squares gone. Later the ink falls short of an "as now" pencil because the pits are deep. And holding the fire level sends people over the sea.
- *Fun:* anticipation (each burn plays out over twenty years), agency (the curve is the player's pencil), discovery (the engine, the deep pits, the boulder), and the sight of the curve leaving the top of the sheet.
- *Second play:* the other end of the choice. A player who burnt high comes back to hold the fire low and sees the other costs: America early, the steady exodus, the ground nearly full. Or to refuse the engines this time.

**B. The Damper** (controller; *the reckless one*).
- *Format:* real time, one run of about ninety seconds, no words on the play screen.
- *Controls:* one: press and hold anywhere on the sheet to close the furnace damper (check the trade); let go and the fire runs at the free nation's pace.
- *Before pressing:* nothing to read; the curve is accelerating, equal times with growing additions, and the ground is going. When do I hold it, and for how long can I keep my thumb down?
- *After:* while held, the curve flattens and small marks fly off the right edge of the sheet (people going over the sea); the moment the thumb lifts, it climbs again. Late in the run it falls whatever the thumb does.
- *Discovery moment:* the freedom to grow is the default, and restraint is a held effort that costs people every second. "It is easy to set a boulder rolling ... it is perilous to try to stop it."
- *Fun:* the climb is felt in the hand; anticipation is a race.
- *Second play:* hold early, or never.

**C. The Saved Ton** (agent: a ton of coal; the non-obvious one).
- *Format:* strategic, eight rounds.
- *Controls:* each round a new engine saves a ton somewhere; the player is that ton and tries to stay in the ground for posterity by hiding in a deeper seam (dearer to dig) or being left in a thin one. Each round a new use bids for it: a steam-plough, a sewage pump, a steam-ship, a new blast furnace.
- *Before pressing:* which seam is too dear for this bidder?
- *After:* the bidder digs or passes; most saved tons are burnt within a round.
- *Discovery moment:* "coal thus saved is not spared; it is only saved from one use to be employed in others."
- *Fun:* a chase, and a strange agent.
- *Second play:* other bidders.

## 9. The first five minutes

**A, Jevons's Noon.**

0:00. A cream sheet ruled in faint blue fills the phone. At the bottom left, a black shaded hump of past coal ending at 1865 in a small orange coal; from it a thin dotted line climbs and climbs and leaves the top of the paper, lettered along its length in an engraver's italic: *supposed future consumption ... shewing the impossibility of a long continuance of that progress.* Title: JEVONS'S NOON. Two lines: Britain burns more coal every year; the coal under it is fixed. You set the pace for each generation. Then the aim on its own line. I press BEGIN.

0:20. The play sheet. Above a ruled double line marked 1850 to 2050: the shaded past and the orange coal at 1865. Below the line, eight bands of black squares labelled 500 to 4,000 feet: "the coal under Britain, each square about 500 million tons." A thin dashed line far below my coal: America. Under the sheet, a ruler with five marks: cut back, a stand, slower, as now, faster. The text says: set where the fire should be in 1885. I touch "as now". A dotted line rises from the coal to twice its height at 1885, and five squares at the top of the ground get a pale mark: "these twenty years would take about 5 squares." I slide to "faster": seven squares. Back to "as now". BURN 1865–1885, in orange.

0:45. The pencil is inked year by year: the curve climbs, an orange sliver glows at its tip and turns black behind it, and squares at the top of the ground flash orange and go blank. America's dashed line rises too. "1865–1885. You set the fire to double, and it doubled. About five squares came out of the ground; the pits are still shallow and coal is no dearer. America burns a third as much as Britain. Nobody had to leave for want of work." NEXT GENERATION.

1:10. 1885. A new engine: "an air-engine that does the same work on half the coal: 2 lb for each horse-power an hour, where the best engines now burn 4." FIT IT or REFUSE IT. Half the coal: surely that makes it last. I fit it. Then the ruler: I go for "a stand", thinking the engine will do the rest. BURN.

1:40. The ink does not follow my level pencil. It climbs above it, to half as high again, and more squares flash than I had marked. "You fitted the new engine and set a stand. Each ton did twice the work, power grew cheap, and new works were built on it, ploughs, pumps and ships; the fire grew by about a third instead of standing still. America fitted it too." Jevons: *It is wholly a confusion of ideas to suppose that the economical use of fuel is equivalent to a diminished consumption. The very contrary is the truth.* I laugh.

2:20. 1905. The ground is a fifth gone. I let it run: "as now". The curve climbs towards the top of the sheet; the ink starts to fall short of the pencil. "Coal from 1,000 feet costs a little more; some works went to America where it is cheap." America is close behind.

3:00. 1925. The second engine. I refuse it this time, having learnt. America's dashed line leaps; my ink falls well short; the hatched bar of people starts to grow: "Trade grew slower than the people; about two million went over the sea." Jevons: *To secure a safe smallness we should have to go back, and strangle in their birth those thoughts and inventions ...*

3:40. 1945. Half the ground is gone. I try "a stand". The fire falls anyway, the bar of people grows fast, and the game says the evening has come: from here the fire falls whatever pace is set. The rest of the sheet draws itself to 1985.

4:10. The verdict: BETWEEN THE TWO, or A BRIEF GREATNESS, with my noon marked on the sheet, the three lines, Jevons's two sentences. THE CURVE, CONTINUED.

4:40. The same sheet, my curve gone pale. A black line leaves 1865 and climbs, not off the sheet but to under half its height by 1913, wavers, and falls slowly towards the axis; the ground beneath empties a little, and most of it stays black. At the end of the line, on the axis: *4 November 2026.* Nothing else. I sit with it.

**B, The Damper.**

0:00. The sheet, the ember, no words but the title and "hold to check the fire". I press START. The curve leaves 1865 at once and climbs, slowly, then faster.

0:10. I hold my thumb on the sheet. The curve bends level; little soot marks fly off the right edge. I don't know what they are: a legend says "people, over the sea", but I'm busy. I let go; the curve leaps up.

0:30. The curve is near the top, and the ground is going fast. I hold; the marks pour off. My thumb covers the coal I am trying to watch.

0:50. The curve falls although I'm not holding. I don't know why: I didn't see the ground run deep.

1:20. The end card. I understood "growth costs coal; restraint costs people", but I never chose anything I could put into words, and I could not see the parts move while my thumb was on the glass.

## 10. The pick, and why

**A, Jevons's Noon.** The player sits where chapter XVIII puts the question: as the nation deciding how far to let its commerce rise, with the consequences laid out on Jevons's own kind of paper. Every generation is a commitment, a pace set before the burn, and the burn shows how the world answered. The fixed ground makes the trade-off visible as area: a high curve eats the squares fast. Each of the 1860s' ways of having both is something the player can actually try. The engine fails on the sheet. A stand fails in the hatched bar. The rivals pass whatever the player does. The discovery lands inside two minutes (the engine) and the question inside four (the evening). The sentence afterwards has no "guess": *I let it double, fitted the air-engine and watched it climb over my line, refused the second one and lost the works to America, and the evening came in 1945 with half the coal still in the ground but too dear to burn.* Originality: no game in the table has had the player draw a time series, or made area the thing spent.

**Author test.** Steffen knows the paradox and knows the question; what he will not know until he plays is how the two interlock. The engine pushes the curve towards the brief end whatever the pencil says. Restraint has its price in people, not just in glory. And the real curve on the last screen is neither of Jevons's two answers: it rose less than a third as high as his own projection, held for half a century, and was let down with two-thirds of Hull's coal still in the ground. An economist will enjoy fitting the air-engine "to make the coal last".

**Runner-up: B, The Damper.** Switch if the author wants the boulder in the hand, a ninety-second piece for a lecture, and will give up the engine choice and reading the sheet while it moves.

**What the reckless concept would have given.** The free nation's momentum felt as the default, with restraint as a held effort. Kept: the burn plays year by year with its growing additions, and the default pace in Jevons's own terms ("as now") sits on the ruler as a mark. Given up: the thumb, real time, and a play screen without words.

## 11. The shape

One run, at most six turns: 1865, 1885, 1905, 1925, 1945, 1965, each a generation of twenty years, ending in 1985; then the sheet runs on by itself at the last pace set, to 2065, so that a fire that runs out is seen to run out (revision 1). At 1885 and 1925 an engine is offered before the pace is set. If the evening comes first (the trade could not grow even at the hardest push), the game says so and the rest of the sheet draws itself at the last pace set: a brief greatness is also a brief game. No presets: the regimes of the paper (Jevons's 3½ per cent, a stationary condition, the restrictive party's check) are marks on the ruler. The discovery comes early (the first engine, at the second turn). No score. Then the verdict, then the last screen.

## 12. Controls, mechanism, feedback, moment

- **Controls.** The pace ruler under the sheet (continuous, from halving the fire in a generation to multiplying it by about 2.4; marks at cut back, a stand, slower, as now, faster). It must be touched each generation before BURN works; it starts at the same place every generation (unset). Two engine decisions: FIT IT / REFUSE IT. The help (?) on every screen.
- **Mechanism.** A toy (section 16): a pace sets how fast the trade the nation builds would grow; the fire actually burning is that trade less the works that dear coal sends to America; coal dearer as the ground empties; an engine fitted raises the trade it builds; an engine refused leaves British power dearer than American; the people grow at a constant rate and a share of those without work go over the sea. America's coal grows on its own and is always cheap.
- **Feedback.** On the sheet: pencil (dotted, the plan), ink (solid, what burnt), soot under the ink, orange at the tip and in the squares being dug, the ground emptying, America's dashed line, the hatched bar of people. Below the sheet: what happened, in the game's voice, in rough sizes; then one sentence of Jevons's, marked as his.
- **Moment.** The engine: the ink over the pencil.

## 13. The look

**Register: Jevons's own plate** (Instrument family): the frontispiece of *The Coal Question*, a lithographed statistical chart on ruled paper with hand-engraved italic lettering, a shaded past and a thin line climbing into the future. The game draws its whole world as that chart, live.

- **Palette:** paper cream `#F2EBD9` (the sheet and the page); rule blue `#C3D2DE` for the faint rules, with a stronger `#94AEC3` for every fifth line and the frame; soot `#1D1B18` (everything drawn: curves, coal, type); ash `#8B847A`, the same soot thinned, for the pencil, secondary lettering and emptied squares' outlines; furnace orange `#E2611B` (whatever is burning, and only that: the tip of the curve, the squares being dug, the BURN button).
- **Type:** Bodoni Moda (Owen Earl / indestructible type, SIL OFL), roman and italic, variable with an optical-size axis. Titles in spaced italic capitals, as the plate's "SUPPOSED CONSUMPTION OF COAL IN BRITAIN"; chart labels in small italic; body text in roman at 16–17 px with the optical size set for text. Embedded as data.
- **Buttons:** engraver's labels: soot italic capitals inside a double rule (an outer 2 px rule and an inner hairline 3 px inside it), square corners, on cream. BURN is the one filled button, furnace orange inside the same double rule, because it lights the fire. A disabled button is the double rule in ash.
- **The one visual rule:** *black is coal, wherever it is; orange is fire.* A black square below the line is coal still in the ground; the black under the curve is coal burnt; a square is orange only for the moment it passes from one to the other.
- **The play screen at 380 px.** The aim, one line, 14 px italic, with ? at the right. The generation strip: six small engraved year labels (1865 ... 1965) with the current one underlined in soot and past ones struck through with a hairline. The sheet, 348 px wide: 22 columns of ten years (1850–2070), about 15.8 px a square. Above the surface: 12 rows of 50 million tons a year (to 600) to begin with, labelled inside the grid at the right in italic ("100 million tons a year", "300", "500"); when a fire climbs higher, more rows of paper are added at the top with a dashed join where the first sheet ended, so the top of every curve is seen (revision 1). Here are the shaded past, the curve, the orange tip, the pencil and America's dashed line, lettered "America" at its tip. This half answers "how big is the fire, and where is it going?" The surface: a ruled double line with the years 1850 / 1900 / 1950 / 2000 / 2050 in small italic. Below the surface, under an engraved heading ("COAL STILL IN THE GROUND: 167 SQUARES, 8 LAYERS DEEP"): 167 black squares of exactly 500 million tons in 8 layers, the last nine cells of the bottom layer empty rock; depth tabs at the left (1,000 ... 4,000 ft); emptied squares are cream; planned squares carry a cream cross; a "digging here" label sits at the dig face. The burnt coal above the surface is filled with the same ruled squares, so a square above is visibly a square below. This half answers "how much is left, and how deep are we?" Under it, a thin ash band: "deeper: dearer than any trade will pay". Then the people: a hatched soot bar on the same grid, one square for two million people, "gone over the sea", which answers "who has paid for a stand?" Under the sheet: the ruler (engraved scale, 348 × 54, five named marks, a soot pointer), the reading line, the result text, the Jevons line, the buttons.
- **The cover:** the frontispiece reproduced: the shaded past, the dotted 3½ per cent line with Jevons's ten-yearly figures (117.9 ... 2,607) climbing off the top, his caption lettered along it, the title in spaced italic capitals, two short lines, the aim, BEGIN, How to play, the signature.
- **The verdict:** the whole sheet, small (the same drawing), with *morning*, *noon* and *evening* lettered at the right places on the curve; the shape's name as a heading in spaced italic capitals; three lines; two sentences of Jevons's; THE CURVE, CONTINUED; ANOTHER SHEET; How to play; About; the signature.
- **The last screen:** the sheet only. The player's curve in ash, the real curve drawn in soot from 1865 with its area shaded, the ground emptied by what was really raised, and the date lettered at the end of the line. No heading, no legend, no America, no people bar, no words. A small ‹ back mark and the signature at the foot of the page, outside the sheet.

## 14. What is cut

- The domestic grate (a third of the coal, the one saving Jevons says is real), Mr Hunt's cannel coal and petroleum, Tyndall's letters, the Mining Record Office, the second Crete. The About screen mentions the grate.
- The two parties of chapter I as named actors: their positions are on the ruler (check, a stand, as now) without the party names. The 1860s party labels would confuse a stranger today, since Jevons's Conservatives are the restrainers.
- Substitutes for coal (ch. VIII), imports (ch. XIII), iron (ch. XV), the national debt (ch. XVII): not in the chapters given. The debt survives in the preface's sentence on the verdict.
- The preface's duties (schooling, ending child labour in the brick-yards, paying down the debt) are read on the verdict, not played. They are a coda on how to spend the day, and playing them would make the game rank the shapes by what they paid for.
- Population and imports as curves of their own (the frontispiece has both). People appear only as those who leave.
- Jevons's own 3½ per cent line on the play sheet. It is on the cover; on the sheet it is a mark on the ruler, so that no line on the sheet looks like advice.

## 15. Divergences

- **Clock.** Twenty-year turns; six of them, to 1985. Jevons's horizon is about a century (1961 on the frontispiece). Six generations let a slow path run long enough to differ visibly from a fast one.
- **The ground.** Hull's 83,544 million tons within 4,000 feet (ch. II) drawn as 168 squares of about 500 million tons, spread evenly over eight bands of 500 feet and dug from the top. Real coal is not evenly spread, and real mines do not empty in order of depth.
- **Price.** Flat at first (ch. I: "no recent rise of importance"), twice the 1865 price with about three-fifths of the coal gone, without limit as the last is approached ("we cannot get to the bottom"). The curve is ours.
- **Why the fire falls.** Jevons says manufactures pass to where fuel is cheapest. The toy makes the trade Britain keeps fall with the price of British power against American, to the power 2½. The exponent is ours.
- **The engines.** Jevons's numbers for the work per ton (4 lb, then 2, then 1⅓). The size of the rebound (twice the work per ton → about 1.4 times the coal over the generation) is ours, smaller than either magnitude chapter VII reports, because his figures mix in ordinary growth. A refused engine leaves British power dearer by the gain to the power 0.2: ours.
- **America.** A toy rival: a fifth of Britain's coal in 1865, growing about 6 per cent a year towards a ceiling far above the sheet, always cheap, fitting every engine. Not a series. The present does not enter before the last screen.
- **The people.** 30 million in 1865, increasing 1.2 per cent a year; when the trade grows slower than that, a quarter of those it cannot employ go over the sea. The share is ours. Jevons gives the alternative, poverty, without a split.
- **The noon and the evening.** The noon is the highest point of the curve; the evening is declared when the trade could not grow even at the hardest push. Both are our definitions of the preface's metaphor.
- **Presentation.** The sheet runs to 2070 and starts 600 million tons tall, so Jevons's rate leaves the top about 1920 as his own curve does; the player's own curve adds paper above instead of leaving (revision 1).

## 16. Mechanism kind and claim boundaries

**Toy.** `mechanism.js` runs a small model built to illustrate the argument of the preface and chapters I, VII and XVIII, set to the numbers they and the frontispiece give.

- **A pace** (the ruler) sets how fast the trade the nation lets itself build would grow: from halving in a generation to about 2.4 times; "as now" is Jevons's 3½ per cent a year.
- **The ground** is Hull's 83,544 million tons within 4,000 feet, drawn as 168 squares; the coal burnt each year comes out of it, shallowest first.
- **The price** of British coal rises with the share burnt: 1 + x²/(1 − x). That is flat at first, twice the 1865 price at x ≈ 0.62, and without limit near the bottom.
- **The fire actually burning** is the trade built, times (price of British power ÷ price of American power)^(−2.5). Dear coal sends works to America.
- **An engine fitted** raises the trade built by (old lb ÷ new lb)^0.5 over the generation. An engine refused leaves British power dearer than American by (lb Britain ÷ lb America)^0.2. America fits every engine.
- **The people** increase at 1.2 per cent a year. When the fire grows slower than that, about one in seven of those without work go over the sea.
- **America** grows logistically at 6 per cent a year from a fifth of Britain's coal, and its coal stays cheap.
- **The evening** is declared when even the hardest push could not make the fire grow; the rest of the sheet to 1985 is then drawn at the last pace set.

**On the About screen, in plain words:**

- *Demonstrates* that the argument can work as Jevons describes it: with a fixed store, a higher fire is a shorter one; a better engine raises the coal burnt; holding the fire level sends people over the sea; a rival with cheap coal passes you whatever you do; the end is dear coal, not no coal.
- *Assumes* everything the toy chose: the price curve, the pull of cheap power, the rebound's size, the emigrating share, America's growth, coal evenly spread to 4,000 feet.
- *The text establishes* the argument and its numbers (3½ per cent; 2,607 million tons by 1961 on the frontispiece; "rather more than a century" to 4,000 feet; Hull's estimate; 4 lb, about 2 lb and under a third in chapter VII), not any one future, as Jevons says himself.

The game says "under this game's assumptions" in spirit throughout: no screen claims that the toy's numbers are Jevons's, and the verdict quotes him rather than paraphrasing a finding. **Which discoveries come from the paper and which from the rules.** The trade-off between height and length follows from the fixed ground, which is the paper's premise. The rebound's direction is Jevons's (ch. VII), and its size is ours. The cost in people of a stand is Jevons's (preface), and its size is ours. America passing on every path is Jevons's ("ultimately passing it"). The *timing* of the evening and the *numbers* in the verdict are the toy's. The game's own choices are the six turns, the two engine years, the evening rule and the shape names. None of them stages a finding. The evening rule only decides when the player stops being asked.

## 21. Revision 2: one production engine, engines that come when they pay, and a ledger of the generations

Steffen's questions after revision 1: why would a nation ever refuse a new engine (it wouldn't: the choice was a quiz with one answer), and shouldn't the game measure what a chosen path meant for each generation? His answer to both: put a simple engine underneath that computes income and income per head (what is now called GDP), and read everything else off it.

**The production engine** (`mechanism.js`, all of it "our choice", with Jevons giving the direction and not a function):

- *Power* = coal burnt × what a ton does (1 in 1865; 2 with the air-engine; 3.08 perfected; from chapter VII's 4 lb, 2 lb and under 1⅓ lb per horse-power per hour).
- *Income* = people × (power per head, against 1865)^0.7. Income per head follows. On screen these are "income" and "income per head"; the About screen says GDP once.
- *Fuel's share of income* = price × coal ÷ income, anchored at a twentieth in 1865, and told as **days of a year's earnings** (18 in 1865). This is the price, measured against income per head as asked. One number explains three things: works go where fuel takes fewer days; an engine pays when it saves more than it costs; the evening is when fuel takes a third of the year or more.
- *Works go where power is cheap*: the share of the trade Britain keeps moves a tenth of the way (in proportion) each year towards (price of British power ÷ price of American power)^−2.5, capped at one: a lead in engines lets Britain keep its works ("neutralize the evils of dearness … for a time", ch. VII), not draw America's.
- *An engine is fitted when it pays* (preface: "the question is one of capital against current expenditure … a wasteful engine pays better where coals are cheap"). Each engine pays, when it appears, where coal costs three times the 1865 price; it cheapens 4 per cent a year, so it pays at the 1865 price about 27 years later, which is when America fits it (1913 and 1953). Britain fits it as soon as it pays there: earlier only if its coal is dearer. Nobody chooses. The card announces the engine, what it would take to pay, and when America will have it; the burn reports the year it was fitted and marks it on the curve.
- *The people* increase at the census rate while income per head holds and more slowly as it falls (the preface's "prevalence of marriage" following trade); one in seven of those the trade cannot employ leave.

**What the engine does to the play.** The price is flat while the shallow coal lasts ("no recent rise of importance"), so the air-engine reaches Britain and America in the same year on every path (1912–13). The perfected engine reaches a fast burner earlier (1939–44 against 1953), because its coal is dear by then; that is the fast burner's temporary edge, and it hastens the end ("the end would only thus be hastened"). The noon of "as now" is now about twenty times the fire of 1865, in 1952, because Jevons's rate kept up for eighty years, with the rebound of two engines, is that high; the sheet grows to hold it. A first build of this revision let works move linearly and a one-year lead in engines made the fire jump five-fold for a year; the move is now proportional, and burning is capped at what is left in the ground.

## 22. Revision 3: ten generations, a box for after, and a score

Steffen's notes on revision 2: the sheet's run-on to 2065 replayed the same animation (a bug: the animation took its start from the end of the full history rather than from the generation's own year); the run-on itself, a projection at the last pace, could go; and the game needed a final verdict, a real score fitting an economics game: average income per head achieved, over what could have been achieved.

**Ten generations.** The player now sets every generation from 1865 to 2045, ten turns; the sheet still reaches 2065, every generation scored is the player's, and after the evening the rest is drawn at the last pace set as before. The strip above the sheet shows the ten years. The real-curve screen stays.

**What "could have been achieved" turned out to mean.** Three searches over the paths (optimum.js, a grid over the ruler's five marks for the first five generations, then coordinate ascent over all ten in finer steps) decided the score's definition:

1. *Average income per head of the people at home* is maximised by cutting back hard in 1865 and then pushing faster than ever: thinning the people first raises income per head later. A score that rewards sending people away is not one Jevons would sign. So heads are now **the people Britain would have had at the census rate, the gone counted in** (`naturalPop` in the mechanism), in the ledger and in the score; the people at home still decide the fire's growth and the exodus.
2. With that denominator, the *plain average* to 2065 is maximised by burning everything: "as now" scores 98, "slower" 90, a stand 41. The coal left after 2065 counts for nothing in it, and the late poverty of a fast path is only averaged in. True of a finite horizon, and not what the game is for.
3. The **typical generation**, the geometric mean of income per head over the ten generations and a box for after, gives an interior optimum: push early (×2.4), hold for three generations, push again towards the end. This is the planner who counts every generation the same, discounts nothing, and lets a doubling count the same for a poor generation as for a rich one (log utility), the standard of Ramsey and Stern. The **box after** values the coal left in 2065 as if burnt evenly over ten more generations, with the engines of 2065, for the people of 2065 (`afterBox`); without it the planner burns the last squares in the last generation, the finite-horizon artefact. The box is the ledger's eleventh cell, with its bar and the squares left.

**The score** is yours ÷ the best path found, out of 100, with the figure and two sentences on the verdict, above "How high" and "How long". The best path is searched by `optimum.js` at build time from `mechanism.js`, written to `optimum.json`, embedded in the page as `OPTIMUM`, and checked by `calibration.js` against a fresh search (within 0.5 per cent). At the game's parameters: best typical ×1.52 on 2.4 1.5 1 1 1.25 1.25 1.75 2.4 2 2, leaving 19 squares; the steady paths score slower 90, as now 70, a stand 56, faster 52, cut back 36. The About screen says the score is ours, the one place the game ranks what Jevons would not, and that a planner who weighed the present or the far generations differently would rank otherwise.

**Words on screen.** "Income per head" stays the phrase; the help and About say whose heads. The score's explanation, for a child: "Count every generation the same, the one after 2065 too, and let a doubling count the same for a poor generation as for a rich one. That gives the typical generation. On your sheet it had ×1.3 the income per head of 1865 (the ledger's figures, taken together). On the best path we could find it has ×1.5. Your score is the one against the other." (The first wording compared the two in words, "the same as the income per head of 1865 … half as much again as it", which Steffen found incomprehensible; the ×-figures are the ledger's own.) The ledger's caption names the box after.
