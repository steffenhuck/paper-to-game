# Leibniz's Flatboat — design memo

Built 24 November 2026 from Leibniz, "How to Cross a Large River in View of an Enemy Army" (c. 1705), in Lloyd Strickland's translation. The game: an engineer's sheet, white lines on blueprint blue. One flatboat drawn in section, a river in plan above it with the enemy's guns on the far bank, and three dials: feet of earth at the bow, guns, men. Every load moves the water line, because a boat carries the weight of the water it could hold. Push off, watch the ball stop in the earth or go through the planks, and watch the same earth come ashore as the parapet the men dig in behind. Three crossings, three pushes before dark in each; the rest of the army passes once the first boat has made the far bank safe for open boats.

## 0. Given

Mine: Steffen's signature and counter. Author title: *Leibniz's Flatboat* (a thing from the game). Third of a Leibniz suite, beside *Leibniz's Fullest World* and a game on *On Controversies* being built at the same time; not a Pantheon game. Lloyd Strickland's translation, credited by name on the end screen. Ideas given: a look of its own, "something like an engineer's drawing", since the text is about plans and models; the substance is the hollowness rule and what goes into the hollow, men, cannon or earth, with the earth as the trick (armour on the water, a parapet the moment you land); secrecy, mortars and landing upstream are detail the engine may use or drop; a puzzle about loading and landing, not a battle: nobody dies on screen, and the enemy stays a line of guns on the far bank. Where the Given overrode precedent: the position and the format (section 6).

## 1. Paper

G. W. Leibniz, "How to Cross a Large River in View of an Enemy Army", c. 1705, manuscript LH 36 Bl. 184–185 (Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz Bibliothek, Hanover). Read in Lloyd Strickland's translation from the French (© 2023), published at leibniz-translations.com; the page is most likely `river.htm` there, but that site could not be reached from the session that built this game, so the address is not checked. No DOI. Available: the translation as a PDF, three pages, with the manuscript's deletions in four notes; no figures, no data, no code, and one number-shaped sentence (the carrying rule). The game is built from the text alone; every quantity in it is ours.

## 2. What the paper hands over

An **argument** (primary): a method for crossing a river under fire that is "always applicable" whatever the terrain, built on one physical rule, Archimedes' in Leibniz's words: a vessel "carries without sinking the weight of the water it could contain", so a boat meant to carry earth and cannon "must have sufficient hollowness". The method: flatboats made cannon-proof by the earth they carry, "equipped with artillery and men", designed so that "the very earth they carry can be used to quickly cover and entrench themselves" on landing; the parapet of earth-filled sacks or baskets, moved by small carts; once enough men are entrenched "the rest will pass without difficulty under the cover of this entrenchment and with the help of the artillery that has already crossed or is still in these floating batteries". Then the cases: the enemy can answer only with bombs, which can be avoided by landing up- or downstream "where the enemy's mortars cannot follow"; where the batteries can be avoided the boat "does not need to be cannon-proof", which is "the most expedient method when there is no time to spare", but armouring it with the same earth "could yield more significant advantages, even dislodging the enemy from their position and landing right under their noses, at the most advantageous spot"; secrecy (build in a closed yard, load only when ready); flosses for the cavalry once the crossing is held. In the deletions: choose a bend with the convex side towards the enemy, "which forces the enemy to divide their army", and the Danube between Donauwörth and Ingolstadt as the way "into the heart of Bavaria". A **model** (secondary): the carrying rule, one line, which the game runs as written.

## 3. The discovery, and its kind

**A mechanism made intelligible**, with a counterintuitive edge. The intelligible mechanism: a boat's cargo is a weight budget set by its hollowness, and the one cargo that spends that budget twice is earth, which is armour while the boat is on the water and a parapet the moment it lands. The edge: the thing to put in a boat that must cross under cannon is not more cannon but dirt; a gun weighs twenty men and keeps nothing off, four feet of earth keeps everything off, and one gun behind that earth is worth a battery before it. The corollaries the game plays: where only muskets reach, one foot does, and the rest of the budget is men; and a shallow boat cannot carry both the armour and the gun, so it must land where armour is not needed. The game does not invent a reversal; it lets the player load guns first and see the planks.

## 4. The core narrative

An army has to get across a wide river, and on the other side there is a row of cannon pointing at the water. You have a flat wooden boat. A boat can carry exactly as much as the water that would fill it weighs, and no more, or it sinks. What do you put in: soldiers, cannon, or sacks of earth? Soldiers are light but a cannonball goes straight through wooden planks. Cannon are heavy and do not stop a ball either. Sacks of earth are heavy too, but a thick enough wall of them stops the ball, and when the boat touches the far bank the men carry the same sacks ashore and stack them into a wall to crouch behind. Behind that wall, one cannon of yours drives the enemy's cannon away, and then all the other boats can come over full of soldiers. The trick is that the earth does two jobs for one weight. Where only muskets are waiting, a thin wall will do and the boat can be nearly all soldiers; and if your boat is too shallow to carry the thick wall and a cannon, you row to where there are only muskets.

## 5. Who lives in the paper

- **The general** (and Leibniz, who writes up his method): decides what the boats carry and where they land. Controls: the load, the landing, the timing. The natural player.
- **The flatboat**: its hollowness is a design variable; it could be the player's object to design. Cut: with nothing in the text to set against hollowness except the depth of the river, a deeper hull is always better, and a choice with one live option is no choice. The hull is given, and differs by crossing.
- **The enemy's battery**: fires at what it can reach; "not as mobile as these boats"; can be dislodged. Stays a line of guns on the far bank, as the Given asks.
- **The earth**: the thing that moves from the boat to the bank. Personifying it was considered (the sack's day: carried on, shot at, carried off, stacked) and rejected: a sack decides nothing.
- **The rest of the army**: passes once the foothold is made. A counter, not a player.

## 7. The aim and the verdict

**Aim**, as the player reads it: *Load the first boat so that its men land, dig in, and make the far bank safe for the rest to follow.*

**Yardstick** during play, in the aim's units: men across by dark, counted on the sheet after every push, and the far bank's state in words (who is dug in behind how much earth, with what, and whether the rest are passing). Three pushes before dark in every crossing; a push is one boat loaded and sent.

**Verdict**, the lines the end screen leads with:
- What the paper found: a boat carries the weight of the water it could hold, so earth and cannon need hollowness; the earth is the armour on the water and the parapet on the bank; once the first boat's men are dug in with a gun beside them, the rest pass; where the batteries can be avoided, the boat need not be cannon-proof.
- What you found: for each crossing, what your first boat that made the bank carried, and when the rest passed, in words; and the men across by dark against the most this boat could have put across.
- Where you part: the loading that cost you most (guns before earth; earth to the brim; four feet where one would do; a shallow boat sent at a battery), named with the sentence of the paper it ignores.
- What would have followed: a fate for the army in words, from the share of the best (over by morning; over by the next evening; over in the end, on flosses, a week late; waiting on the wrong bank).

## 8. Three concepts

**A. The drawing board (agent, challenge).** The player is the general with the sheet: three dials (feet of earth, guns, men), one hull, the water line as the budget, and a far bank with something on it. Push off, watch the ball meet the drawing, watch the earth come ashore. Deliberation: what their guns will do to what I carry, how much of the budget the wall needs, whether a gun is worth twenty men here, whether this hull can carry both. After: the ball stops or goes through; the wall becomes a parapet; the rest pass or wait. Discovery moment: the ball stops in the hatching the player drew, and the same hatching stands on the bank with the men behind it. Fun: anticipation (will it float, will it stop the ball), agency (the water line answers every tap), discovery (dirt beats guns), mastery (the second crossing is loaded in ten seconds, correctly, for a reason). Second playthrough: the loadings the first run never tried; a boat sunk on purpose at the bank; the small boat sent at the battery to see what it can and cannot do.

**B. The sack (subject, discovery). The reckless one.** The player is one sack of earth, and the screen has no words: a sack is picked up at the yard, tapped onto a place in the boat (bow, side, floor, left on the quay), and the day plays out around it in pictures: a ball comes, stops or does not; the bank comes, the sack is stacked or left; the rest come or do not. The discovery is the sack's double life. Walkthrough: first minute delightful, the sack carried on, the water line climbing. Second minute: the player is thinking "where do I put myself", which is a real question once, and then not again, because the sack cannot decide how many sacks there are, which is the paper's whole question. Hands, no decision after the first. It loses, and what it gave up: the wordless screen and the comedy of being cargo. The verdict's picture of the earth going ashore is kept from it.

**C. The spy (experimenter, discovery).** The player is the enemy's spy watching the closed yard, with a glass: each day a boat is shown loaded, one glimpse, and the player must report to the battery what will come (men, guns, earth, how much) from the water line alone, then see the boat cross. The hollowness rule run backwards: the draught tells the cargo's weight but not its kind. Deliberation real (reading a draught, betting on kind), the paper's secrecy paragraph operated. Loses on the author test: the player never loads anything and never lands, and the earth's double life is observed, not made; and its ending is a reveal, which section 3 of the skill calls the weak form. Kept from it: the secrecy line in the coda.

**The hybrid** (A's decision carried out by B's hands) was written: the general loads by dragging sacks, guns and files of men onto the plan. It is *Leibniz's Tiles* again, the spatial packing the author rejected on the first Leibniz game; dials it is, with the drawing filling itself.

## 9. The first five minutes

**The pick (A).** 0:00 A blue sheet with white lines: "Leibniz's Flatboat", a drawing of a flat hull in section, three lines about what the hands will do, the aim on its own line, a button that says Begin. 0:15 Crossing 1 of 3: a river in plan, a row of red guns on the far bank, a boat in section below with an empty hull and a dotted water line at the bottom of it; under the drawing, three dials at zero and a line: their guns, a ball goes through planks and stops in four feet of earth; the boat holds twelve tuns. The player presses Men +; ten small figures appear in the hull and the water line rises a sliver; the sum under the drawing reads "6 men... 1 tun of 12". Grin: the boat sinks a little with every tap. 0:40 They press Guns +: a gun appears, the water line jumps: "a gun: 2 tuns". They press Earth +: hatching appears at the bow, "1 ft", the line jumps again. They play the dials up and down for a while and watch the line; at thirteen tuns the water comes over the gunwale and the drawing says SINKS; they laugh and take something off. 1:30 They decide. Most will send sixty men and a gun or two, and some earth because the sheet said so, but not four feet, because four feet is eight tuns and that leaves almost nothing. Push off. 1:45 Plan: the boat crosses the river on a dotted track; a red line from a gun to the boat; in the section the ball enters from the right, meets two feet of hatching, and comes out the other side across the hull: THROUGH. The boat turns back. The sheet says what happened in one line: the ball went through two feet of earth and the planks; the boat turned back; nobody across; two pushes left. Frown, then the thought: four feet, then. 2:15 Second loading: four feet, and now the arithmetic bites: eight tuns of twelve; a gun is two; twenty men. Or forty men and no gun. Many take the forty. Push. 2:40 The ball stops in the hatching; the boat lands; the hatching slides onto the bank and stands up as a parapet with the forty behind it. By dark: 60 across, where 620 was possible. 3:40 Crossing 2: their battery has gone up to the bend; here only muskets. **The runner-up (C, the spy).** 0:00 A glass on a yard across the river; a boat slides out; its draught is marked; the player reports "men" or "earth" or "guns" to the battery and sets the guns. 0:40 The boat crosses; the report was wrong; the ball goes through anyway, or stops. 1:30 The player is reading draughts well and betting on kinds, which is a game, and by 2:30 is asking what the point of the betting is, since the battery fires the same way whatever the report. The paper's method is seen through a glass and never done. Loses.

## 10. The pick, and why

A. The dials are the paper's own variable (what goes into the hollow), the water line is the paper's own rule drawn, and the three crossings are the paper's three cases in its own order of importance rather than the order it writes them in (section 11). The decisive deliberation has live wrong branches the paper names: guns before earth (the planks), earth to the brim (the rule), four feet where one would do (the boat need not be cannon-proof), a shallow boat sent at a battery (sufficient hollowness). The author test: the earth's double life is operated, not told, and the arithmetic is the general's. Originality: no game in the table has had the player load a vessel and watch it sink; none has used a section drawing as the whole play surface. Runner-up: C, and what would make me switch: a reader who finds the first crossing too easy from the briefing alone, in which case the fix is to withhold the exact feet their ball needs and let the first push find it. B's loss: the wordless screen and the sack's comedy.

## 11. The shape

Three crossings, each with up to three pushes before dark; a push is one loading and one boat. The crossing ends when the rest pass or at dark. Four to nine actions in all; first builds run long, and this one is held to the number of things to find out: (1) earth stops the ball and becomes the parapet, and a gun behind it drives the battery off, so the rest pass; (2) where only muskets reach, one foot and a boat of men; (3) a shallow boat cannot carry both the armour and the gun, so it lands where armour is not needed. Presets are the paper's cases: crossing 1, the battery faced directly ("which can even be faced directly"); crossing 2, their foot only, the battery drawn away to the bend (the deleted note's convex bend "forces the enemy to divide their army"); crossing 3, shallow boats of six tuns ("must have sufficient hollowness"), with a choice of landing, opposite their light guns or a mile upstream "where the enemy's mortars cannot follow". Ordered so the discovery lands in the first crossing. Score: men across by dark, per crossing, against the most that boat could have put across at that spot, found by exhaustive search; the final fate from the share of the best over all three.

## 12. Controls, mechanism, feedback, moment

**Controls:** three dials with − and +: Earth in feet of wall at the bow (0 to 6; a foot is two tuns), Guns (0 to 3; two tuns each), Men (0 to 60 by tens; a tenth of a tun each). In crossing 3, a landing to tap on the plan (opposite the battery, or upstream). Push off. **Mechanism:** load ≤ the hull's tuns, else it sinks at the bank; the ball fired at the landing stops in the wall if the wall is thick enough for that fire (one foot for muskets, two for light guns, four for field guns), else the boat turns back; a boat that lands with fewer than ten men has nobody to raise the parapet and comes back; a landed wall stands on the bank at its thickness; men and guns accumulate there; a gun fires only with five men ashore to serve it; a battery is driven off by one served gun behind earth; the rest pass, five open boats of sixty a push for every push left, once nothing on their side can fire on an open boat (the battery driven off, or only foot, kept off by men dug in). **Feedback:** the section: water line on every tap; the ball in the drawing; the wall redrawn on the bank; the fleet streaming on the plan. **Moment:** the ball stops in the hatching the player drew, and the same hatching stands on the bank with the men behind it.

## 13. The look

**Register: austere, an engineer's blueprint.** Where it came from: the Given ("this is a text about plans and models, so something like an engineer's drawing would fit") and the paper's first sentence, "the plans and models that a highly experienced general presented". The game is one sheet from those plans. **Palette:** Prussian blue `#163A6B` (the sheet), white `#F4F7FB` (the lines), pale cyan `#9FD3E6` (water and the water line), carmine `#E8403A` (the enemy and the ball), sand `#E9C877` (earth). **Type:** Barlow Condensed 700 for the title, the sheet's headings and the dials; Barlow 400 and 600 for everything else; both embedded. **Buttons:** square corners, a one-pixel white rule, uppercase condensed text, like the cells of a title block; the Push off button filled white with blue text. **The one visual rule: the sheet never shows a thing it does not draw.** Every quantity the player acts on is a line on the drawing with a dimension on it: the hull's tuns as a depth mark, the wall's feet across the hatching, the water line with its sum, the ball as a red line that ends where it stops. No gauges beside the drawing; the drawing is the gauge. **The play screen:** the plan strip at the top (340 × 90: near bank with the fleet waiting, the river, the far bank with the guns in red and the landing mark), the section under it (340 × 170, the one big thing), the sum line under the section, the three dials in a row, Push off; all within 700 px. Pictures answer: the plan, "what is over there and where am I landing?"; the section, "what have I loaded and does it float?"; after the push, the section again, "did the ball stop, and where is my earth now?"; the plan again, "are the rest coming?".

## 14. What is cut

The particular parapet invention the general kept secret (Leibniz does not describe it either); the carts (the sacks move themselves on screen); mortars as a mechanism (bombs are a random draw the paper calls inaccurate and unexpected; kept as a line on the upstream landing and in the coda); secrecy and the closed yard (coda); the flosses for cavalry (coda); the height of the bank and the islands; the hull's depth as a control; casualties of any kind: a boat the ball goes through turns back, a boat too heavy sinks at the bank and its men wade ashore, a parapet that does not stand sends its men back to the boat.

## 15. Divergences

- **Units.** Tuns (a tun of water, about a tonne), feet of wall, files of ten men. The paper gives none; ours, period-shaped.
- **Weights.** A man with arms and kit a tenth of a tun; a light field gun with its carriage and shot two tuns; a foot of wall across a bow of eight feet and four high, in sacks, two tuns. Ours, from the look of period pieces and the density of earth, rounded for play; the author-note says so.
- **What stops what.** One foot for musket balls, two for light pieces, four for field guns. On the light side of period field practice (redoubts used more), chosen so that a twelve-tun boat can be cannon-proof and carry a gun and twenty men, and a six-tun boat cannot be both. The paper claims cannon-proof flatboats are feasible with "sufficient hollowness" and gives no feet.
- **Crews and hands.** Five men serve a gun; ten men raise a parapet. Ours.
- **The rest.** Five open boats of sixty, every push left in the day, once the bank is safe for open boats. Ours; the paper says "the rest will pass without difficulty".
- **One gun drives off a battery.** Ours, reading "even dislodging the enemy from their position": a served gun behind earth against guns on an open bank.
- **The clock.** Three pushes to a day. Ours.
- **The cases.** The battery drawn off to the bend in crossing 2 uses a deleted passage; the shallow boats of crossing 3 are ours, built to make "sufficient hollowness" bite.

## 16. Mechanism kind and claim boundaries

**Toy.** A model built to illustrate the paper's method, with the one rule the paper states (the carrying rule) run as written and everything else chosen by us and listed in CONFIG. The About screen says what the game **demonstrates** (under this game's assumptions: a boat's cargo is a weight budget; earth spent on a wall thick enough for the fire is the one cargo that works twice; a gun behind that wall is what lets the rest pass; where only muskets reach a thin wall does, and a hull too shallow for wall and gun must land where the wall can be thin), what it **assumes** (every weight, thickness, crew and clock above; that a ball either stops or goes through; that the rest pass in open boats once the bank is safe), and what the paper **establishes** (an argument from a general's plans, with one physical rule; no trial, no numbers). The discovery comes from the paper's mechanism, not the rules: the earth's double use and the budget are the text's; what our choices add is where exactly the lines fall (section 17 checks that the lessons survive moving them).

## 17. Calibration plan and result

`node calibration.js`: (1) the carrying rule as written (sinks iff load exceeds the hull's tuns; the water line is load over tuns); (2) the three crossings' best loadings by exhaustive search over all loadings and push sequences, equal to the memo's (620, 660, 640) and unique in their first push; (3) the heuristics table (section 18), with every wrong branch the memo names scoring below the best for the reason the paper names; (4) robustness: the lessons hold when the feet a ball needs are moved one up or down, when a gun weighs one or three tuns, when the rest pass at thirty or ninety a boat; (5) the words: every outcome has a sentence and no sentence contains a decimal. Result in `calibration-result.txt`.

Read for the paths: every wrong branch the memo names loses for the reason the paper names. All men and firepower put nobody across anywhere (a boat of planks to a ball; a gun keeps nothing off on the water). Earth to the brim lands nobody (no hands). The spreader (two feet and a gun) is shot through under field pieces and is fine under muskets. The physicist, who armours and forgets the gun, digs in under the battery and the rest never pass: 120 against 620. The crossing-1 recipe carried everywhere is best at the battery, short at the bend (twenty men where sixty would float) and sinks the shallow boat. The paper's loading sent at the battery with the shallow boat cannot carry the gun with men to serve it, so it goes without and the rest wait. Learning after one wrong push costs exactly one push's worth of the rest (300) in every crossing. Read for the options: every dial value the bests use is taken (no gun and one gun; one foot and four; twenty, forty and sixty men; both landings land a boat), and no option is dead. The score has no loophole: the best is found by exhaustive search over loadings and push sequences, is reached on the first push in every crossing, and is unique in the first push where the memo says so (crossing 2 is indifferent to a gun or a foot more, and the verdict's "best" names the lightest).

**Robustness** (calibration §4): the core lessons (earth then a gun at the battery; a wall without a gun leaves the rest waiting; a thin wall and a boat of men where only muskets reach) hold with a field piece needing three feet instead of four, a gun of one or three tuns, the rest at thirty or ninety a boat, a fleet of one. They fall if the wall, a gun and its crew no longer fit the great hull (five feet), which is the paper's own clause about hollowness and is reported, not hidden. The shallow-boat lesson (crossing 3) needs the light pieces' wall and a gun not to fit six tuns; it changes with a one-tun gun, a one-tun foot or light pieces stopped by one foot, and the About screen says so.
