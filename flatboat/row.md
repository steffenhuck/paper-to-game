| **Leibniz's Flatboat** | Leibniz (c. 1705), *How to Cross a Large River in View of an Enemy Army*, LH 36 Bl. 184–185, in Lloyd Strickland's translation (2023). A boat carries the weight of the water it could hold, so what goes into it: soldiers, cannons or earth? As an engineer's sheet in blue ink on white paper: the river from above with the enemy's cannons in red on the far shore, the flatboat from the side below, three dials (feet of earth at the front, cannons, soldiers), and the water line climbing with every ton until it comes over the side. The general's own boat of sixty soldiers goes first and is shot straight through. Then you load, and a push plays one step at a time: the enemy fires; the cannonball sticks in the heap, and a detail view shows how far it went in, which is the only way to learn how much earth a shot needs, or it goes through the planks and the boat sinks in mid-river with everyone aboard, and the day is lost, to be played again with the dead counted or left; the soldiers carry the sacks ashore and crouch behind them; a cannon behind that wall drives the enemy's off; the rest of the army crosses in ordinary boats. Three crossings, three pushes before dark in each: the enemy cannons faced directly, the bend where only muskets wait, and shallow boats of six tons that cannot carry both the wall and a cannon, so you land where a thin heap will do. The verdict in words: soldiers across, soldiers lost, the army's fate and its cost, and where your loadings parted from the paper's. | [play](https://steffenhuck.github.io/paper-to-game/flatboat/leibnizs-flatboat.html) · [memo](flatboat/memo.md) |
