| **Leibniz's Flatboat** | Leibniz (c. 1705), *How to Cross a Large River in View of an Enemy Army*, LH 36 Bl. 184–185, in Lloyd Strickland's translation (2023). A boat carries the weight of the water it could hold, so what goes into it: soldiers, cannons or earth? One engineer's drawing in blue ink on white paper, the river seen from the side: your shore on the left with the rest of the army waiting, the enemy's cannons on the right, and the flatboat on the water between, sinking lower with every ton. The general's own boat of sixty soldiers goes first and is shot straight through. Then you load, and a push plays in three or four steps on the same drawing: the enemy fires from the bank; the cannonball sticks in the heap, and the sheet measures how far it went in, which is the only way to learn how much earth a shot needs, or it goes through the planks and the boat sinks in mid-river with everyone aboard, and the day is lost, to be played again with the dead counted or left; the soldiers carry the sacks up the bank and crouch behind them; a cannon behind that wall drives the enemy's off; the rest of the army crosses in ordinary boats. Two crossings, three pushes before dark in each: the enemy cannons faced directly, and the narrows, with shallow boats of six tons that cannot carry both the wall and a cannon, so you land where a thin heap will do. The verdict in words: soldiers across, soldiers lost, the army's fate and its cost, and where your loadings parted from the paper's. | [play](https://steffenhuck.github.io/paper-to-game/flatboat/leibnizs-flatboat.html) · [memo](flatboat/memo.md) |
