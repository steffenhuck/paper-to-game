Leibniz's Flatboat

The verdict

What the paper found

A boat carries the weight of the water it could hold, so a boat that is to carry earth and cannons must be hollow enough. Flatboats made cannon-proof by the earth they carry, with cannons and soldiers aboard, cross a river in the face of its cannons; the moment they land, the same earth becomes the wall the soldiers crouch behind, and the rest of the army crosses under that cover and with the help of the cannons that have crossed. Where the enemy's cannons can be avoided, the boat need not be cannon-proof at all.

Leibniz's other remarks

Against earth boats the enemy can only throw bombs from mortars, which are not easily kept off soldiers; but mortars are not accurate, the enemy will not expect it, and one can land up- or downstream where they cannot follow. Build the boats in a closed yard far from the river, prepare the earth elsewhere, and load only when ready, so that spies cannot learn it in time. Choose a bend with its bulge towards the enemy, so that they must divide to watch it. And once the crossing is held, the cavalry and the rest come over on rafts of floating timber, as the Danube boatmen use, which carry many men and horses at once.

How to play

Load the first boat so that its soldiers land, dig in, and make the far shore safe for the rest of the army to follow.

The sheet

The top drawing is the river seen from above: your shore at the bottom with the ordinary boats waiting, the enemy's shore at the top with whatever they have there drawn in red (cannons, or soldiers with muskets), and the landing marked with a letter. The big drawing is your boat seen from the side, front to the right, as you load it.

The one rule

A boat carries as much as the water it could hold weighs. The big flatboat holds 12 tons of water, so it carries 12 tons; the shallow one holds 6. Every tap sinks the boat a little lower: watch the dashed water line. Load more than the hull holds and the water comes over the side: the boat sinks at the bank and that push is lost.

The dials

  • Earth: a heap of sacks at the front, in feet. A foot weighs 2 tons. The line under the dial says whether it stops what the enemy fires: 1 ft stops musket shots, 2 ft a small cannonball, 4 ft a cannonball.
  • Cannons: 2 tons each. A cannon stops nothing while the boat is on the water. Once ashore, behind a wall of earth, one cannon drives the enemy cannons off.
  • Soldiers: a tenth of a ton each, by tens, up to 60. Somebody has to be aboard to carry the sacks ashore.

Push off

Then the crossing plays out one step at a time: tap Next after each. The boat sets off; the enemy fires; the shot sticks in the earth or goes straight through the earth and the planks; a boat that is shot through turns back with nobody landed; a boat that gets across lands its soldiers, who stack the sacks into a wall and crouch behind it. Nobody dies on this sheet.

The rest of the army

The rest cross in ordinary boats as soon as nothing on the far shore can fire on the river: enemy cannons have to be driven off by a cannon of yours behind the wall; enemy soldiers with muskets keep away from a wall with your soldiers behind it. Until then the rest wait. There are 3 pushes before dark in each crossing, and no push after.

Three crossings

Below the Lech, opposite the enemy cannons, with the big flatboat; it opens with the general's own first plan, a boat of sixty soldiers, so you can see what happens to it. At the bend, where only enemy soldiers with muskets wait. The narrows, with shallow boats of 6 tons and two landings to choose from: A, opposite the enemy's small cannons, or B, a mile upstream, out of reach of their cannons and their mortars. Tap A or B before you push.

The verdict

After each crossing: how many soldiers you put across by dark, and the most this boat could have put across here, with the loading that does it. At the end: the army's fate, in words, and where your loadings parted from the paper's.
