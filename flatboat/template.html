The verdict

What the paper found

A boat carries the weight of the water it could hold, so a boat that is to carry earth and cannon must have hollowness enough. Cannon-proof flatboats, made so by the earth they carry, with artillery and men aboard, cross a river in the face of its batteries; the moment they land, the same earth covers and entrenches the men, and the rest pass under that cover and with the help of the guns that have crossed. Where the batteries can be avoided, the boat need not be cannon-proof at all.

Leibniz's other remarks

Against earth boats the enemy can only throw bombs, which are not easily kept off men; but mortars are not accurate, the enemy will not expect it, and one can land up- or downstream where they cannot follow. Build the boats in a closed yard far from the river, prepare the earth elsewhere, and load only when ready, so that spies cannot learn it in time. Choose a bend with its convex side towards the enemy, so that they must divide to watch it. And once the crossing is held, the cavalry and the rest come over on flosses, the rafts of floating timber the Danube uses, which carry many men and horses at once.

How to play

Load the first boat so that its men land, dig in, and make the far bank safe for the rest to follow.

The sheet

The top drawing is the river in plan: your bank at the bottom with the open boats waiting, the far bank at the top with whatever the enemy has there drawn in red (a battery of guns, or a line of foot with muskets), and the landing marked with a letter. The big drawing is the boat in section, bow to the right, as you load it.

The one rule

A boat carries the weight of the water it could hold. The great flatboat holds 12 tuns of water, so it carries 12 tuns; the shallow one holds 6. Every tap moves the dashed water line up the hull. Load past the hull's tuns and the water comes over the gunwale: the boat sinks at the bank and that push is lost.

The dials

  • Earth at the bow: a wall of sacks, in feet. A foot weighs 2 tuns. The line under the dial says what the wall stops: 1 ft a musket ball, 2 ft a light ball, 4 ft a six-pound ball.
  • Guns: 2 tuns each, with their shot. A gun keeps nothing off on the water. Ashore, behind earth, with 5 men to serve it, one gun drives a battery off an open bank.
  • Men: a tenth of a tun each, by tens, up to 60. It takes 10 men to raise the parapet; a boat that touches the bank with fewer, and nobody already there, comes back.

Push off

The boat crosses. Whatever the far bank has fires at it: the ball enters the section from the right. If the wall is thick enough for that ball, it stops in the earth and the boat lands; the men carry the sacks ashore and the same earth stands on the bank as a parapet, with the guns behind it. If not, the ball goes through the earth and the planks and the boat turns back. Nobody dies on this sheet; a boat that is shot through comes home.

The rest

The rest of the army crosses in open boats, 5 boats of 60 for every push left in the day, as soon as nothing over there can fire on an open boat: a battery has to be driven off by a gun of yours ashore; foot is kept off by your men dug in behind earth. Until then the rest wait. There are 3 pushes before dark in each crossing, and no push after.

Three crossings

Below the Lech, opposite their battery of field pieces, with the great flatboat. At the bend, where only their foot waits. The narrows, with shallow boats of 6 tuns and two landings to choose from: A, opposite their light pieces, or B, a mile upstream beyond their guns, where their mortars cannot follow. Tap A or B before you push.

The verdict

After each crossing: how many men you put across by dark, and the most this boat could have put across here, with the loading that does it. At the end: the army's fate, in words, and where your loadings parted from the paper's.
