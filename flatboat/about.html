What the paper says

Leibniz has seen the plans and models a highly experienced general presented to a great prince for crossing a large river in view of an enemy army, and sets down the method that is "always applicable" whatever the terrain. Make flatboats that are cannon-proof, equipped with artillery and men, and designed so that the very earth they carry can be used to cover and entrench the men the moment they land. Since earth and cannon are very heavy, the boats must have hollowness enough, because a vessel carries without sinking the weight of the water it could contain. The parapet is of earth-filled sacks or baskets, moved by small carts. With such boats one crosses despite the enemy's artillery, which can even be faced directly; once enough men are entrenched, the rest pass under the cover of that entrenchment and with the help of the artillery that has crossed or is still afloat. The enemy can answer only with bombs, which are not easily kept off men, but mortars are inaccurate, the enemy will not expect it, and one can land up- or downstream where they cannot follow. Build the boats in a closed yard and load them only when ready, so that spies learn nothing in time. Where the batteries can be avoided, the boat need not be cannon-proof, and that is the quickest way when there is no time to spare; but armouring it with the same earth yields greater advantages, even dislodging the enemy and landing under their noses at the best spot. Then the cavalry and the rest come over on flosses, the rafts of the Danube. In passages Leibniz struck out: choose a bend with its convex side towards the enemy, which forces them to divide; and the Danube between Donauwörth and Ingolstadt leads into the heart of Bavaria.

What the game does with it

You are the general at the sheet. One flatboat in section, three dials (feet of earth at the bow, guns, men), and the one rule the paper states, drawn: the water line climbs with every tun aboard, and past the hull's tuns the boat sinks at the bank. Push off, and whatever the far bank has fires at the drawing: the ball stops in the earth or goes through the planks. A boat that lands carries its earth ashore as the parapet the men dig in behind; a gun behind that earth drives a battery off an open bank; once nothing over there can fire on an open boat, the rest pass. Three crossings, three pushes before dark in each: the battery faced directly; the bend, where only foot waits; the narrows, with shallow boats and a choice of landing. Nobody dies on the sheet; a boat shot through turns back.

The kind of mechanism, and what the game may claim

Toy. A model built to illustrate the paper's method. The carrying rule is the paper's, run as written; every other number is ours and sits in the config block at the top of the game's source with its reason: a man a tenth of a tun, a gun two, a foot of wall two; one foot stops a musket ball, two a light ball, four a six-pound ball; five men serve a gun, ten raise a parapet; three pushes to a day; the rest pass five open boats of sixty a push.

Demonstrates (under this game's assumptions): a boat's cargo is a weight budget set by its hollowness; earth spent on a wall thick enough for the fire is the one cargo that works twice, armour on the water and parapet on the bank; a gun behind that wall, not a gun on the water, is what lets the rest pass; where only muskets reach, a thin wall does and the rest of the budget is men; a hull too shallow to carry both the wall and a gun must land where the wall can be thin.

Assumes: all the numbers above; that a ball either stops in the earth or goes through; that one served gun behind earth drives a battery off an open bank; that the rest pass in open boats once the bank is safe; that nothing is lost but pushes. The lessons were re-run with the lines moved (three or five feet for a field piece, a gun of one or three tuns, the rest at thirty or ninety a boat); the earth-then-gun lesson needs the wall, a gun and its crew to fit the great hull, and the shallow-boat lesson needs the light pieces' wall and a gun not to fit the shallow one; the memo says so.

The paper establishes: an argument from a general's plans and one physical rule. No trial, no numbers, no claim about how many feet of earth stop a ball. Nothing in the game is evidence for the paper; the game shows what the paper's method does under our numbers.
