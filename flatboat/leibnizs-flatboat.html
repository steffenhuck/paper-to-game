The verdict

What the paper found

A boat carries the weight of the water it could hold, so a boat that is to carry earth and cannons must be hollow enough. Flatboats made cannon-proof by the earth they carry, with cannons and soldiers aboard, cross a river in the face of its cannons; the moment they land, the same earth becomes the wall the soldiers crouch behind, and the rest of the army crosses under that cover and with the help of the cannons that have crossed. Where the enemy's cannons can be avoided, the boat need not be cannon-proof at all.

Leibniz's other remarks

Against earth boats the enemy can only throw bombs from mortars, which are not easily kept off soldiers; but mortars are not accurate, the enemy will not expect it, and one can land up- or downstream where they cannot follow. Build the boats in a closed yard far from the river, prepare the earth elsewhere, and load only when ready, so that spies cannot learn it in time. Choose a bend with its bulge towards the enemy, so that they must divide to watch it. And once the crossing is held, the cavalry and the rest come over on rafts of floating timber, as the Danube boatmen use, which carry many men and horses at once.

How to play

Load the first boat so that its soldiers land, dig in, and make the far shore safe for the rest of the army to follow.

The sheet

One drawing: the river seen from the side. Your shore is on the left, with the rest of the army waiting in ordinary boats. The enemy's shore is on the right, with whatever they have there drawn in red: cannons, or soldiers with muskets. Your flatboat sits on the water between them, seen from the side, front to the right, and everything that happens, happens on this drawing.

The one rule

A boat carries as much as the water it could hold weighs. The big flatboat holds 12 tons of water, so it carries 12 tons; the shallow one holds 6. Every tap sinks the boat a little lower into the river. Load more than the hull holds and the water comes over the side: the boat sinks at the bank and that push is lost.

The dials

  • Earth: a heap of sacks at the front, in feet. A foot weighs 2 tons. How many feet stop the enemy's shot, nobody tells you: a shot that sticks shows on the heap how far it went in, and the sheet keeps what you have measured for each kind of shot. Too much earth costs soldiers on every push; too little costs the boat.
  • Cannons: 2 tons each. What a cannon does, you will see.
  • Soldiers: a tenth of a ton each, by tens, up to 60. Somebody has to be aboard to carry the sacks ashore.

Push off

Then the crossing plays out in three or four steps: tap Next after each. The boat sets off; the enemy fires; the shot sticks in the earth, with its depth measured on the heap, or goes through the earth and the planks. A cannonball that goes through holes the boat at the water line: it sinks in mid-river with everyone aboard, and the day is lost. Musket shots that go through hit one soldier in six, and the rest, with nothing to crouch behind, are driven back. A boat that gets across lands its soldiers, who carry the sacks up the bank, stack them into a wall and crouch behind it.

A day lost

When a boat is sunk you choose: play this crossing again, or go on to the next. Playing again starts the river afresh, but the dead stay counted, the attempt is counted, and what you measured is still on the sheet. The verdict counts soldiers across and soldiers lost, and the attempts.

The rest of the army

The rest cross in ordinary boats as soon as nothing on the far shore can fire on the river. Until then they wait. There are 3 pushes before dark in each crossing, and no push after.

Two crossings

Below the Lech, opposite the enemy cannons, with the big flatboat; it opens with the general's own first plan, a boat of sixty soldiers, so you can see what happens to it. The narrows, with shallow boats of 6 tons and two landings to choose from: A, opposite the enemy's small cannons, or B, a mile upstream, out of reach of their cannons, where only soldiers with muskets wait. Tap A or B before you load.

The verdict

After each crossing: how many soldiers you put across by dark. At the end: the army's fate, in words; the most each boat could have put across, with the loading that does it; and where your loadings parted from the paper's.

What the paper says

Leibniz has seen the plans and models a highly experienced general presented to a great prince for crossing a large river in view of an enemy army, and sets down the method that is "always applicable" whatever the terrain. Make flatboats that are cannon-proof, equipped with artillery and men, and designed so that the very earth they carry can be used to cover and entrench the men the moment they land. Since earth and cannon are very heavy, the boats must have hollowness enough, because a vessel carries without sinking the weight of the water it could contain. The parapet is of earth-filled sacks or baskets, moved by small carts. With such boats one crosses despite the enemy's artillery, which can even be faced directly; once enough men are entrenched, the rest pass under the cover of that entrenchment and with the help of the artillery that has crossed or is still afloat. The enemy can answer only with bombs, which are not easily kept off men, but mortars are inaccurate, the enemy will not expect it, and one can land up- or downstream where they cannot follow. Build the boats in a closed yard and load them only when ready, so that spies learn nothing in time. Where the batteries can be avoided, the boat need not be cannon-proof, and that is the quickest way when there is no time to spare; but armouring it with the same earth yields greater advantages, even dislodging the enemy and landing under their noses at the best spot. Then the cavalry and the rest come over on flosses, the rafts of the Danube. In passages Leibniz struck out: choose a bend with its convex side towards the enemy, which forces them to divide; and the Danube between Donauwörth and Ingolstadt leads into the heart of Bavaria.

What the game does with it

You are the general at the sheet. One drawing, the river seen from the side: your shore on the left with the rest of the army waiting, the enemy's shore on the right with its cannons, and your flatboat on the water between them. Three dials (feet of earth at the front, cannons, soldiers), and the one rule the paper states, drawn: the boat sinks lower with every ton aboard, and past the hull's tons it sinks at the bank. Push off, and the crossing plays out in three or four steps, a sentence to each, all on the same drawing: the enemy fires from the bank; the shot sticks in the earth or goes through the planks; a boat that lands carries its earth up the bank as the wall the soldiers crouch behind; a cannon behind that earth drives the enemy cannons off; once nothing over there can fire on the river, the rest of the army crosses. Two crossings, three pushes before dark in each: the enemy cannons faced directly, after the general's own boat of sixty soldiers has been sunk; the narrows, with shallow boats and a choice of landing, opposite the enemy's small cannons or a mile upstream where only muskets wait. A cannonball that goes through sinks the boat in mid-river with everyone aboard and the day is lost, to be played again or left; musket shots that go through hit one soldier in six and the rest are driven back. The verdict counts soldiers across, soldiers lost and attempts.

The kind of mechanism, and what the game may claim

Toy. A model built to illustrate the paper's method. The carrying rule is the paper's, run as written; every other number is ours and sits in the config block at the top of the game's source with its reason: a soldier a tenth of a ton, a cannon two, a foot of earth wall two; one foot stops a musket shot, two a small cannonball, four a cannonball; three pushes to a day; the rest cross in five ordinary boats of sixty a push; a cannonball through the planks sinks a flatboat with all aboard, and musket fire through costs one in six.

Demonstrates (under this game's assumptions): a boat's cargo is a weight budget set by its hollowness; earth spent on a wall thick enough for the shot is the one cargo that works twice, armour on the water and a wall on the shore; a cannon behind that wall, not a cannon on the water, is what lets the rest cross; where only muskets reach, a thin wall does and the rest of the budget is soldiers; a hull too shallow to carry both the wall and a cannon must land where the wall can be thin.

Assumes: all the numbers above; that a shot either sticks in the earth or goes through; that one cannon behind earth drives enemy cannons off an open shore; that the rest cross in ordinary boats once the shore is safe; that a sunk boat loses everyone aboard and a replayed crossing carries its dead. The lessons were re-run with the lines moved (three or five feet for a cannonball, a cannon of one or three tons, the rest at thirty or ninety a boat); the earth-then-cannon lesson needs the wall and a cannon to fit the big hull with soldiers to spare, and the shallow-boat lesson needs the small cannons' wall and a cannon not to fit the shallow one; the memo says so.

The paper establishes: an argument from a general's plans and one physical rule. No trial, no numbers, no claim about how many feet of earth stop a cannonball. Nothing in the game is evidence for the paper; the game shows what the paper's method does under our numbers.
