# Leibniz's Flatboat — notes to self on the illustrations (25 November 2026)

Written after going through every screenshot of revision 2 at phone width, before revision 3. The register stays: an engineer's sheet, blue ink on white paper. A drawing shows an event by annotating it, so the fixes below are the draughtsman's own devices: trajectories with arrowheads, detail views, damage drawn into the outline, dimensions on what matters. Each note says what is weak, why, and what to draw instead.

## The river from above (the plan)

1. **The enemy's fire is a thin vertical red line from the cannons to the boat**, and it sits on the landing track, so it reads as part of the track. Draw a trajectory: a dashed red arc from the cannon muzzle to the boat with an arrowhead, and a burst (a small star of short strokes) at the muzzle while they fire. Musket fire: three short arcs from the line of ticks.
2. **The boat is a 22 × 8 rectangle.** Too small to be the hero of the drawing. Draw it 32 × 11 with a pointed front towards the enemy and the heap of earth hatched at the front, so the plan shows which way it faces and that it is armoured.
3. **The rest of the army are small rectangles that turn into dominoes when they cross.** Draw them as boat outlines (pointed), and when they cross, a row of them along the track with a count that ticks up on the sheet ("300 CROSSED").
4. **"40 BEHIND A WALL" is a 32 × 4 hatched bar at the far shore.** Make it 44 × 7, with two or three crouching figures beside it, so the far-shore state is a picture before it is a label.
5. **Crossing 3's top band is crowded**: "ENEMY SHORE", B's muskets, "ENEMY SOLDIERS", A's cannons, "SMALL CANNONS", the letters, the red note. Put the shore label in the middle of the band (x 170, anchored middle), keep B's enemy to the left of B and A's to the right of A, and show the red note only for the chosen landing (already so).
6. **The "still firing on the river" dashed red line is good**; add its label on the line ("STILL UNDER FIRE") rather than only in the big label.

## The boat from the side (the section)

7. **The cannonball flies above the boat.** Its path was drawn at floor − 30, above the gunwale, so a shot that "goes through the planks" passes over the hull outline and through the soldiers' heads; the author read it as sailing over. Draw it at hull height (floor − 11, inside the 22 px of hollow), so it enters at the front planks, passes through the heap or not, runs among the soldiers' legs, and leaves at the stern. Ball radius 4.5, a red dotted trajectory with an arrowhead behind it.
8. **Nothing shows the damage.** When the shot goes through, draw a splintered notch in the front plank where it entered (a jagged red-and-blue stroke across the outline) and a hole at the stern where it left; the hull outline itself changes, which is what a drawing can do.
9. **The shot that sticks is a red dot at the heap's edge, with nothing measured.** This is the one picture the game now depends on, because the feet are withheld and the player must read how far the shot went in. Draw a detail view, as a draughtsman would: a circle on the main drawing around the front of the boat and, in the empty upper half of the section, the same spot drawn three times larger in a circle labelled "DETAIL A": the heap in hatching, the ball embedded at its depth, a dimension line from the heap's face to the ball reading "WENT IN 3½ FT", and the heap's own thickness beside it. On a through shot the detail shows the ball leaving the back of the heap and the planks splintered, with "WENT THROUGH 2 FT".
10. **Sixty soldiers at seven pixels are a comb.** One figure stands for ten soldiers: at most six figures, 22 px tall, with the count written beside them. Fewer, bigger things.
11. **The water line's label collides with the heap at full load.** Keep it right-anchored but at 9.5 px, "12 OF 12" on one line, and move the heap's dimension line two feet higher so the two never meet.
12. **The sinking is adequate** (the hull drops, the hollow floods, SINKS in red). Add the water coming over the gunwale as a short wave stroke at the lowest point, so the eye sees where it comes in.

## The landing

13. **"They crouch behind it" shows men standing.** A second figure symbol, crouching (a hunched back and bent knees, 14 px tall), used only behind the wall; the wall at 44 px is then taller than the men, which is the point.
14. **Your cannon firing is a straight line up and to the right.** Draw a burst at the muzzle and a dashed blue trajectory arcing over the wall off the right edge of the sheet; on the plan, the enemy cannons slide off with "DRIVEN OFF" (already so).
15. **The emptied boat on the left is an odd quadrilateral.** Draw it as the same hull as the section, smaller, with its water line, nose against the shore.
16. **The cargo slides from the boat to the shore in one piece.** Good; keep. The sacks could arrive one stroke at a time (hatching that fills from the bottom), which would read as stacking; do it if the frame's two seconds allow.

## Chrome around the sheet

18. **The About screenshot in the session is the end screen underneath the overlay** (the fixed overlay does not appear in a full-page capture). Use the overlay trick from the Leibniz session test, or capture the viewport only.
19. **The start motif has the same comb of soldiers.** Use the ten-per figure there too.

## The withheld feet (the game change that these pictures serve)

20. Nothing on the sheet says how many feet their shot needs. The plan's red note says what fires ("ENEMY CANNONS: EARTH STOPS A CANNONBALL IF THERE IS ENOUGH OF IT"); after a shot the note becomes the player's record ("LAST CANNONBALL WENT 3½ FT INTO THE EARTH" or "WENT THROUGH 2 FT"), kept per kind of shot across crossings, because that is what the general could know. The Earth dial's line says the same record, or "no shot measured yet". A shot stops if the heap is at least the whole feet it needs; it goes in the needed feet less a half, so the detail's dimension gives the answer to the player who reads it. The general's boat still goes first, so the player has seen that no earth fails before loading anything.
