## 1. Paper

Ronald H. Coase, "The Problem of Social Cost", *Journal of Law and Economics* 3 (October 1960): 1–44. DOI 10.1086/466560. Available: a 23-page typeset reprint (PDF only; the case-law discussion of section VI is abbreviated in it with an ellipsis, and sections IX and X of the original, the Pigovian tradition and the change of approach's second half, are not in it; section VIII is). The reprint is OCR'd: the herd table on p. 2 prints "Z" for 2, and no number in it was trusted without being read twice against Coase's prose, where every number of the two arithmetical examples is also stated in words. Online: JSTOR (stable 724810) and the University of Chicago Press. No code, no data: the paper is an argument with two worked arithmetical examples, which is what the game is built on.

## 2. What the paper hands over

**Primary: an argument.** (i) Harm is reciprocal: to stop A harming B is to harm A, so the question is not "how do we restrain A?" but "which harm is the lesser?" (section II). (ii) If bargaining is costless, the legal assignment of liability does not change what gets produced, only who pays whom: the cattle-raiser who must pay for trampled crops and the cattle-raiser who may be paid to keep a steer at home face the same cost of the third steer (sections III–IV). (iii) Bargaining is not costless, and once it is not, the initial assignment of rights does decide the outcome, and the alternatives (the market, the firm, direct regulation, doing nothing) all cost something, so the choice is among whole social arrangements by their total product (sections V–VI). (iv) The Pigovian reflex (make the harmer pay, or tax him) can therefore make things worse, as it does in the railway example, where liability stops the trains and the farmers plant the strip that burns (section VII). (v) The change of approach: compare the total product of alternative arrangements, starting from where we are (section VIII).

**Secondary: two arithmetical examples, ported.** The farmer and the cattle-raiser: crop loss of 1, 3, 6 and 10 tons for herds of 1 to 4 steers at $1 a ton, so marginal damage 1, 2, 3, 4; a fence at $9 a year; a tract whose crop is worth $12 and costs $10 to grow, net $2; a strip worth $10 costing $11; footnote 4's crop that pays $1 instead of $2 and takes $1 of damage instead of $3. The railway: one train a day is worth $150 and costs $50, two are worth $250 and cost $100; one train burns $60 of crops, two burn $120; under liability the farmers plant the land by the line too, and the damage doubles to $120 and $240; the abandoned land would have grown $160 of crops but releases $150 of labour and capital to be used elsewhere, so running the railway unliable is worth $20 more than no railway, and liability shuts it. The paper gives no meat values for the steers; the game supplies them and tags them as ours.

## 3. The discovery, and its kind

**A counterintuitive result**, twice. First: the public and the field believed that the one who causes the harm should pay; Coase shows that when neighbours can talk, making him pay changes who pays whom and nothing else, and that the "victim" may end up paid to give way. Second: when neighbours cannot talk, the reflex of making the harmer pay can lower the total: the railway stops. Underneath both, **a mechanism made intelligible**: bargaining over a right, and the cost of bargaining as the thing that makes the law matter.

## 4. The core narrative

A farmer grows corn on one side of a brook, and a rancher keeps cows on the other, and the cows walk over and eat the corn. The two of them come to you, the judge, and ask who is in the right. You say: the cows may roam. The farmer goes home, thinks, and offers the rancher a few dollars to keep his third cow at home, because the corn that cow eats is worth more than the cow. Next year you say the opposite: the corn is protected, the rancher must pay for every ear. And the rancher keeps two cows, exactly as before. Your ruling changed who paid whom and nothing else. Then the quarrels get bigger: a lawyer who charges for every deal, a hundred houses under one chimney, a railway whose sparks burn the fields along the line. Now nobody can make a deal, your ruling decides what happens, and the one that feels fair (the railway must pay) stops the trains and leaves everyone poorer. The right ruling is the one that counts the whole valley.

## 5. Who lives in the paper

- **The cattle-raiser** (and the railway, the factory, the confectioner): chooses how much of the harmful activity to run (steers, trains, machines), whether to fence or fit a device, whether to pay or to be paid. Could be the player: an agent setting a herd under a law handed down.
- **The farmer** (the doctor, the households): chooses whether to plant, what to plant, whether to fence, whether to accept a payment. Could be the player: an agent choosing crops under cows.
- **The court, the legislature** (section VI): assigns the right: may the cows roam, or is the corn protected, or are cattle banned? Coase says the courts are "in effect, making a decision on the economic problem". Could be the player: the one who rules.
- **The Pigovian economist** who taxes; **the government** which zones; **the firm** that buys both farms. Settings of the arrangement.
- **The cows** themselves, whom the law in Boulston's case holds guilty instead of their owner ("the atavistic idea that the animals are guilty"). A joke the paper makes; not a player.

## 7. The aim and the verdict

**Aim, on the start screen, at the top of the play screen, and first in How to play:** *rule on six quarrels so the valley is worth the most.*

The yardstick is in the aim's units: after each quarrel, what the valley is worth under the player's ruling, in dollars, beside the most any ruling could have got for that quarrel; at the end, the sum of the six against the sum of the six bests.

**Verdict (the end screen leads with these, in words):**
- *What you did:* "your six valleys were worth $N of the $M they could be" and one line per quarrel in the player's own terms ("you let the cows roam; the farmer paid the rancher to keep one at home").
- *What the paper says:* "when neighbours can talk for free, a ruling changes who pays whom and nothing else. when they can't, the ruling decides what the valley is worth, and the ruling that feels fair, make the one who did the harm pay, can leave everyone poorer."
- *What that means:* "there is no culprit to find. there are two neighbours, and the question is which harm is the smaller. the right ruling counts the whole valley."

A fate in words follows (section 11), and the paper's own caveat: the valley is measured in dollars because Coase measures it so, and he says himself that the real choice is wider than that.

## 8. Three concepts

**A. The Rancher (agent; strategic).** The player is the cattle-raiser. Each season the court hands down a law (cows may roam / corn protected) and the player picks the herd, one to four steers, seeing the meat each steer brings and the corn each eats. The farmer (the mechanism) reacts: pays for a steer to stay home, or sends a bill, or gives up the strip. Deliberation: how many steers, given the law and the farmer's likely offer. Discovery: under either law the player ends with the same herd and the money runs the other way. Fun: agency, mastery (the second season's herd is picked faster), discovery. Second playthrough: play the other law. Weakness: the deliberation is arithmetic with the numbers on screen; the law is handed to the player instead of chosen; and the railway and the chimney, where the paper's second discovery lives, cannot be played from the saddle.

**B. The Judge (dilemma / agent; strategic). The pick.** The player is the court. Six quarrels come to the bench, each a pair of neighbours and a harm, with the facts in pictures: what each cow brings, what each eats, what a fence costs, whether the neighbours can talk and what talking costs. Three rulings, the paper's own three courses (p. 1): the harmer may carry on; the harmer must pay for the harm; the harmful thing is banned here. Touching a ruling puts its sign up in the field and says what it means; "rule" commits. The neighbours then act on the ruling, beat by beat, in the picture: cows cross, the farmer plants or gives up, a fence goes up, a deal is struck and coins pass, or no deal can be made and the lawyer's fee or the hundred households say why. Then the valley's worth against the most any ruling could get, and the other two rulings as ghost lines. Deliberation: who deserves the right, at first; then, once talking costs, which ruling leaves the valley best without a deal; then, with the railway, what the whole valley is worth under each ruling, including the strip that gets planted because it pays either way. Discovery: quarrels one and two, the ruling changes who pays whom and nothing else, and the protected farmer is paid to give way; quarrel three, the ruling begins to matter; quarrel five, making the railway pay stops the trains and the valley is poorer. Fun: anticipation (what will they do with it?), discovery, mastery (by the chimney the player is reading the facts the way Coase reads them), agency. Second playthrough: rule the other way on every quarrel and watch the purses change where the valley does not, and the valley change where it does.

**C. Two Thumbs (agent, both at once; reckless).** No words on the play screen. The player's left thumb drags cows one by one over the brook; the right thumb drags corn rows into the field; two purses fill and empty as coins fly between the two neighbours' faces; a gavel in the corner flips the law, and nothing in the field changes when it does, only the direction of the coins. The player finds the arrangement with the most coins in the valley's chest by feel. Deliberation: pattern and experiment. Discovery: the gavel changes nothing. Fun: agency, discovery. Second playthrough: a different valley. Written out in full below; it loses because the gavel's indifference is the paper's first half only and a wordless screen cannot stage the second half (the cost of talking, the hundred households, the strip that gets planted), which is where the paper's harder discovery lies; and because without words the player infers prices from coin animations, which is reading a sentence in a worse language.

## 10. The pick, and why

**B, The Judge.** It is the paper's own question handed to the player: "should A be allowed to harm B or should B be allowed to harm A?" (p. 1), asked six times with the paper's own cases, and the player's first instinct is Pigou's. Both of the paper's discoveries land inside play, the first by quarrel two and the second at quarrel five, and the rulings in between carry real deliberation about the thing the paper is actually about: whether the neighbours can talk, and at what price. The sentence afterwards has no "guess": *I protected the corn and the rancher kept two cows; I let the cows roam and the rancher kept two cows; I protected the strip and the farmer was paid to give it up; I made the railway pay and the trains stopped.* Originality: no game in the table has put the player on the bench, and none has made the cost of a deal the thing the player learns to read.

**Author test.** Coase would enjoy watching a stranger rule for the corn, see the purses swap and the herd stay, rule for the cows next time to test it, and then be caught by the railway. The quarrels are his own arithmetic, in his order of difficulty rather than his order of exposition, and the verdict's caveat is his own section VIII.

**Runner-up: A, The Rancher.** Switch if the author wants the player to feel the cost of the third steer in their own purse rather than see it on a ledger, and accepts losing the railway as play.

**What the reckless concept would have given.** Kept: the coins flying between the two purses as the deal is struck, and the sign in the field as the whole statement of the law, which came from imagining a screen with no words. Given up: the silence, and the thumbs.

## 11. The shape

Six quarrels, one screen each with a result sequence; then the verdict. About five to seven minutes, an estimate. Each quarrel is one of the paper's cases, ordered for the player:

1. **The cows and the corn** (section III–IV; marginal damage 1, 2, 3, 4; fence $9; meat values ours; the corn's worth ours at $8 so that both neighbours stay). Talking free. Discovery one: the ruling changes who pays whom.
2. **The strip by the brook** (section III: the tract worth $12 costing $10, net $2, "the cattle have a well-defined route, say, to a brook"). Talking free. The protected farmer is paid to give way.
3. **The lawyer** (section V, the costs of market transactions, and footnote 4, the crop that cows do not eat). A deal costs $3. The ruling begins to matter: the right should go where no deal is needed.
4. **The chimney** (section V, the smoke nuisance "which may affect a vast number of people", the smoke-preventing device). No deal possible. The harmer can fix it cheaply: making him pay works.
5. **Sparks** (section VII, Coase's railway numbers). No deal possible. Making the railway pay stops the trains; letting it spark is better; one train would be best and no ruling gets it.
6. **The orchard** (section VII: "by altering the figures, it could be shown that there are other cases in which it would be desirable that the railway should be liable"; numbers ours). No deal possible. Letting it spark burns the orchard; making it pay is right.

Score: the valley's worth under the player's ruling against the most any of the three rulings could get, per quarrel and summed at the end. The criterion is the paper's own (the value of production, sections V–VIII), and the About screen says that Coase himself calls it narrower than the real choice. Fates in words at the end, by the share of the possible: all six at their best, "the valley paints your face on the signpost"; most, "the farmer and the rancher both send you a card. so does the railway."; about half, "the valley is doing all right, and nobody is sure whose fault that is"; less, "the cows have asked for a new judge". A last screen reads the paper's change of approach. No retry past the sixth quarrel; "rule again" starts a new run.

## 12. Controls, mechanism, feedback, moment

**Controls.** One: the ruling, three live options on every quarrel (the harmer may carry on / the harmer must pay / the harmful thing is banned here), which are the paper's own three courses of action from its first paragraph (liability, a tax on the damage equivalent to it, exclusion from the district). Touching one previews it: the sign on the bank changes and one line says what the ruling means for the two neighbours (what each now owes or holds); nothing about what they will do with it, nothing about the valley's worth. "rule" commits. Every quarrel starts with no ruling selected.

**Mechanism** (`mechanism.js`, ported from the two examples and generalised): a quarrel is a harmer with activity levels and a marginal profit for each (steers, trains, the machine), a sufferer with one or more plots each with a standalone worth and a damage per unit of activity (and, in quarrel 3, a second crop with no damage), a device (fence, filter) at a price that removes the damage and can be bought by either side, and a cost of talking (free, a fee, or impossible). Under each ruling the mechanism computes the status quo (each side's own best move given the law, the sufferer's planting decided by what he keeps: all of the plot's worth if protected, the worth less the damage if not), the efficient arrangement (the maximum of the valley's worth over everything both sides could do), and the deal: if the gain from moving from the status quo to the efficient arrangement exceeds the cost of talking, they move, the fee is paid, and the surplus net of the fee is split evenly (Coase: "would depend on the shrewdness of the farmer and the cattle-raiser as bargainers"; the even split is ours), the loser compensated first. The ban sets the activity to zero and allows no deal (a decree, section V). The output is a list of beats, each a state of the picture and one line in the game's voice, plus the purses and the valley's worth, for the player's ruling and for the two others.

**Feedback.** The sign and the purses (section 6): the valley picture redraws beat by beat, coins pass, and the valley's worth is drawn as a bar against the most any ruling could get, with the two other rulings as ghost lines beneath.

**Moment.** Quarrel 2's ghost line, or its played line: the farmer whose corn the court protected takes the rancher's money and leaves the strip unplanted; and quarrel 5's beat: "no trains run."

## 13. The look

**Register: a picture book in the manner of Dick Bruna** (Drawn family; by the Given, and the discussion in section 0). From the paper's subject, which is a child's first farm: cows, corn, a fence, a train, a chimney; and from the paper's temperament: one clean line, the fewest marks, and an argument about where a line is drawn. Bruna's idiom: a white page; one heavy, even black outline round everything; flat colour inside it and no shading, no texture, no perspective; figures frontal and centred, eyes two dots, a mouth one short line or a small cross; objects reduced to the two or three shapes a four-year-old recognises; all text in lowercase. In the manner of, not the character: no rabbit, no Miffy (though Coase has rabbits in section VII, and the About screen says the temptation was resisted).

- **Palette:** page white `#FFFFFF`; line black `#1B1B1B` (every outline, 4 px at phone scale; every word); bruna red `#E2231A` (the one button that commits, the farmer's shirt, the train's engine); bruna yellow `#F6C000` (corn, coins, the sign's board); bruna green `#3FA535` (grass on the farmer's side, turnip tops, the filter); bruna blue `#1E63B5` (the brook, the rancher's overalls, sky-blue nothing else); brown `#8C5A2B` (cows, soil, the fence). - **The one visual rule:** *the law is a sign in the field.* Nothing about the ruling is stated anywhere but on the signpost on the bank of the brook: blank before the player touches a ruling, lettered when they touch one, and standing in the picture through every beat that follows. ## 15. Divergences

- **Meat values for the steers** ($5, $4, $2, −$1 for the first to fourth): ours; Coase gives none and says the unconstrained herd is three (p. 6: "the size of the herd that would be maintained if crop damage was not taken into account"), which these reproduce, with four never paying.
- **The corn's worth in quarrel 1** ($8): ours, so that the first quarrel ends with both neighbours in business and the deal is about the third cow; Coase's own tract (net $2) is quarrel 2, where the land is given up, as in his text.
- **The lawyer's fee** ($3) and **the turnips** ($6, no damage): the fee is ours; the second crop is footnote 4 with its sizes changed so that the fee bites (the paper's own crop pays $1 less and halves the damage; ours pays $2 less and removes it).
- **The chimney's numbers** (running the works $20; the hundred houses' loss $15; a filter $5): ours; section V gives the setting and the device and no figures.
- **The railway's numbers**: Coase's, exactly, with the far fields' standing crop set at $200 so that totals are absolute (he works in differences); the orchard's ($300 standing, $200 of sparks a train, no strip): ours, built on his remark that other figures make liability right.
- **Bargaining**: an even split of the surplus after the fee; Coase leaves the split to shrewdness. The ban allows no deal, as a decree.
- **The clock**: six quarrels in a row; Coase's farmer and rancher are in long-run equilibrium.
- **Presentation**: Bruna.

## 16. Mechanism kind and claim boundaries

**Kind: ported, with a toy around it.** The two arithmetical examples run with Coase's own numbers (the crop-loss column, the $9 fence, the $12 crop costing $10, the railway's $150/$50 and $250/$100, the $60 a train that doubles to $120 under liability, the $160 of crops against $150 of released factors), and the game generalises them into one structure (a harmer with marginal profits, a sufferer with plots, a device, a cost of talking) so that the six quarrels are instances of one mechanism. What the paper does not give, the game adds and tags as ours in `CONFIG`: the steers' meat values, the corn's worth in quarrel 1, the lawyer's fee and the turnips, the chimney's three figures, the orchard's two, the even split of a bargain's surplus, and the fate bands.

- **The game demonstrates** that under Coase's arithmetic with free bargaining every ruling but the ban ends in the same herd and the same value with the money running the other way; that a cost of bargaining makes the ruling matter, and the right ruling is then the one under which the party who can act cheaply holds the right; that with Coase's railway figures liability stops the trains and lowers the total while with other figures it is right; and that no ruling reaches the one-train optimum.
- **The game assumes** the added numbers; that neighbours who can talk reach the efficient arrangement when the gain exceeds the cost of talking and split the surplus evenly; that a hundred households or farms cannot bargain at all; that the sufferer, when made whole, plants whatever is worth most standing; that the harmer moves first and the sufferer responds; that the ban allows no deal; and that the valley's worth is the value of production.
- **The paper establishes** the argument and the two examples as arithmetic, not as facts about any valley. It gives no lawyer's fee, no filter price, no household's washing. It says itself (section VIII) that the choice between arrangements should be made in broader terms than the value of production, and the verdict screen quotes that.

**Where the discoveries come from** (build.md §1). Discovery one (the tie between rulings when talking is free) follows from the mechanism, not from any chosen number: the calibration check reproduces it for 300 random meat and corn values. Discovery two (the lawyer: the ruling matters) follows from the fee exceeding the gain, and holds for any fee above $2; at a fee of zero the rulings tie again. Discovery three (the railway: liability stops the trains, and letting it spark is better) is Coase's own figures and holds for any standing crop on the far fields (our one added number there) and for a strip worth up to $29; at $30 and above the ruling flips, which is what Coase says altered figures would do, and the game plays that as quarrel 6 rather than hiding it. The chimney's result (liability gets the filter fitted) rests on our figures and on the device being the works' alone to fit; the About screen says so. The score is the paper's criterion; the fate in words, by each valley's share of the possible counted equally, is ours, and the loop table shows it ranks the obvious strategies as the paper would (section 18).

**The two traps.** No correlation is played as a cause: the mechanism is a model, and every lever is a variable in Coase's own model. No average is played as an individual: there are no averages.
