how to play

rule on six quarrels so the valley is worth the most.

six quarrels come to you, one at a time. each is two neighbours and a harm: cows that eat corn, a chimney that spoils washing, a railway whose sparks burn fields. you are the judge.

the picture

the valley is drawn at the top. the one doing the harm is on the right (the rancher, the works, the railway); the one harmed is on the left (the farmer, the houses, the farms). the signpost on the bank is where your ruling will stand.

the facts

four cards under the picture say what each thing is worth: what each cow brings in, what the corn is worth and how much of it the cows eat, what a fence or a filter costs, and whether the neighbours can talk, for free, through a lawyer, or not at all. you need these to rule well: the valley's worth is what both neighbours produce, less what the harm spoils and what any fence, filter or lawyer costs.

the ruling

three rulings, every time: the harmer may carry on; the harmer must pay for the harm; the harmful thing is banned here. touch one and the sign on the bank shows it, with a line saying what each neighbour now owes or holds. nothing yet says what they will do with it. press rule to commit.

what happens

press next to watch the neighbours act on your ruling, one step at a time: who plants what, who keeps how many cows, who pays whom, whether a deal is struck. a deal can only be struck if the neighbours can talk and the deal is worth more than talking costs.

the tally

three money bags: the harmed neighbour's purse, the harmer's purse, and the valley's chest, which is both together less any fee. the bar shows the valley's worth against the most any ruling could have got for this quarrel. the two rulings you did not give are shown as dashed cards, with what they would have ended in.

the end

after six quarrels, the verdict: what your valleys came to against what they could have, valley by valley; what the paper says; and what that means. then rule again, and try the other rulings.

about

Coase's Cows is a game built from Ronald H. Coase, "The Problem of Social Cost", Journal of Law and Economics 3 (October 1960): 1–44. DOI 10.1086/466560. On JSTOR as stable 724810. Game built from this paper; the paper is the authority where the two disagree.

what the paper says

Harm between neighbours is reciprocal: to stop the rancher's cattle harming the farmer's crops is to harm the rancher. The question is not "how do we restrain A?" but "which harm is the smaller?" If the two can bargain for free, it does not matter whom the law protects: the cattle-raiser who must pay for trampled corn and the cattle-raiser who may be paid to keep a steer at home face the same cost of the third steer, and the same herd results. The ruling changes who pays whom and nothing else. But bargaining is never free, and once it costs something the ruling does decide what happens; the choice is then among whole arrangements (the market, the firm, direct regulation, doing nothing), each of which costs something, and the right one is the one with the largest total product. The reflex of making the harmer pay can make things worse: in Coase's railway example, liability for sparks leads the farmers to plant the strip beside the line, which burns, and no train pays for its sparks; the trains stop and the valley is poorer.

what the game does with it

You are the court. Six quarrels come to you: the paper's own farmer and cattle-raiser (sections III–IV), the strip the cows cross to drink (section III), the cost of bargaining (section V and footnote 4), the smoke nuisance with its vast number of sufferers and its smoke-preventing device (section V), the railway with Coase's own figures (section VII), and the same railway with other figures, as Coase says other figures would make liability right (p. 17). Each time, the paper's three courses of action from its first paragraph are your rulings: liability, no liability, or exclusion. Touch one to read it on the sign; press rule; watch the neighbours act on it.

mechanism kind: ported, with a toy on top

The two arithmetical examples are ported with Coase's numbers: crop loss of 1, 3, 6 and 10 tons for herds of one to four at $1 a ton; a $9 fence; a tract worth $12 that costs $10 to grow; one train worth $150 at a cost of $50, two worth $250 at $100; $60 of crops burnt a train, doubling to $120 under liability as the strip by the line is planted; $160 of crops on the abandoned land against $150 of released factors. Around them the game adds what the paper leaves out and tags as its own in the config block of the source: the meat each steer brings ($5, $4, $1, and a fourth that loses $1, so that the unconstrained herd is three, as Coase says); the corn's worth in the first quarrel ($8); the lawyer's fee ($3); turnips ($6); the chimney's figures ($20 to run, $15 of washing, a $5 filter); the orchard's ($300 standing, $200 of sparks a train); and an even split of the surplus from any bargain, where Coase says the split would depend on the shrewdness of the bargainers.

The game demonstrates that under Coase's arithmetic, with free bargaining, every ruling but the ban ends in the same herd and the same value, with the money running the other way; that a cost of bargaining makes the ruling matter; and that with his railway figures, liability stops the trains and lowers the total, while with other figures it is right. The game assumes the numbers it added, that neighbours who can bargain reach the best arrangement and split the gain evenly, that a hundred households or a hundred farms cannot bargain at all, and that the valley's worth is the value of what gets produced. The paper establishes the argument and the two examples as arithmetic, not as facts about any valley: it does not measure a lawyer's fee or a filter's price, and it says itself that the choice between arrangements should be made in broader terms than the value of production.

Where the game's discoveries come from: the tie between rulings in the first two quarrels follows from the paper's mechanism and from no number we chose (the calibration check reproduces it for three hundred random meat and corn values). The railway's reversal follows from Coase's own figures and holds for any standing crop on the far fields and for a strip worth up to $29; at $30 or more the ruling flips, which is Coase's "by altering the figures" remark and is played as the sixth quarrel. The lawyer's quarrel splits for any fee above the $2 a deal would gain. The score, the value of the valley against the most any ruling could get, is the paper's own criterion; the fate in words is ours.

what is cut

The confectioner and the doctor, and the case law of section VI; the firm as an arrangement; a Pigovian tax as a fourth ruling; Pigou's rabbits and Boulston's case (the temptation to draw a rabbit was resisted); the strategic preliminaries, which Coase says do not affect the long-run position; and the comparison of arrangements in anything but dollars, which Coase says is the real one.

the look

A picture book in the manner of Dick Bruna: a white page, one heavy black line, flat colour, everything reduced to the shapes a four-year-old recognises, all in lowercase. In the manner of, not the character. The paper's subject is a child's first farm, and the paper's argument is about where a line is drawn; the signpost is where the law stands.
