The game in one line

Eleven measurements made with nothing but your mind, each one a step in Augustine's argument about where a length of time is held: a moment of your own life that no clock ever measured; a voice held after it is gone; last night against yesterday, which only a clock measured, as a relation of two sounds; nothing, listened for three times and then guessed at; a voice in the middle of two others, twice, and then half of one and double another; a silence you plan and then recognise among voices; the sun's standing still, measured by a sound; three things from yesterday, which you made longest and by how much; a crowd; and at the end your night and day and your moment of joy, recognised again or not. Nothing is drawn while it lasts; what is over drops into memory.

What the book says

In Book XI of the Confessions Augustine asks what time is. "If no one asks me, I know: if I wish to explain it to one that asketh, I know not." The past is no longer, the future is not yet, and the present has no length. Yet we measure times and compare them, and say this one was double that. Where, then, is a length held? He answers by elimination. Not in the thing: a voice cannot be measured before it sounds, nor while it sounds, nor after, when it is gone. Not in the motion of bodies: a learned man told him time is the motion of the sun, moon and stars, but a potter's wheel running now slower, now quicker has rounds of different lengths, and when the sun once stood still over a battle "time went on"; we measure a body's motion, and its standing still, by time, so time is not its motion. Not in the future or the past as such, which are not. "It is not then themselves, which now are not, that I measure, but something in my memory, which there remains fixed. It is in thee, my mind, that I measure times." The man who plans a long sound "hath in silence already gone through a space of time". We measure silence by stretching our thought to a voice "as if it sounded". A psalm said by heart drains from expectation, through attention, into memory; so does a life. Time is a stretching of the mind (distentio), and in chapter XXIX the same word turns: a mind stretched among many things is torn among them, and he asks to be gathered. Book X supplies the store: the "fields and spacious palaces" of memory, where images stay when the things are gone.

How the game plays it

Every station is one of the book's measurements, done by the player. A moment of joy held for as long as it lasted: a span nothing outside the player ever measured (X.viii). The low one heard and held: the measure is in you, since the voice is gone (XI.xxvii). Last night and yesterday as two sounds in a relation: the night is the one span of a life not attended to at all, known only by a clock outside; what can be given is the relation, not the lengths (XI.xx). Nothing: the player is told something will sound and listens for it three times; nothing does; then they are asked to guess its length with a hold, and the guess is the trap: the instant of a touch has no length, and a length can be laid against a length, never against a point; the point is the present, which has no space (XI.xv). The middle: a short voice and a long one, and the player holds the voice right in the middle, twice with two pairs; then half of the last long one, which is a middle with the present as its other end, and double the last short one. On a clock a half and a double undo each other; the game lays the player's two together and says whether they do, which is the test of a ratio scale that holds from the inside. Equal-difference judgments across pairs are what an interval scale is made of; a zero makes it a ratio scale, and durations have one, the instant. People asked for the point halfway between a short and a long duration tend to put it near the geometric mean rather than the arithmetic one, as if the mind's scale were logarithmic, on which zero lies infinitely far off and there is no halfway between nothing and something; the game reads each middle as nearer the plain middle or the middle by proportion, and says what the two together imply for the half (XI.xvi, xxi). Three things from yesterday, held as the player has them, then which was made longest: an order and nothing more, measurement at its oldest; then about how many times longer, which is a ratio claimed about the player's own holds and checked against them (XI.xxvi). A silence held with nothing sounding, then two voices, one of them that long: the planned span had gone through a space of time without sounding (XI.xxvii). The sun crossing a sky of a different width at a different pace each evening, standing still on the way, and its standing still measured by a sound: a body's rest is measured by time, so time is not the body's motion (XI.xxiii–xxiv). A crowd of voices, the low one to be held until it drops out, then how many there were: a mind stretched among several things holds each less (XI.xxix). And the two inner spans offered back among decoys, at the end: the only measures checked against the player rather than the world.

What the words mean

After a hold the game says in words where it landed against its target: the same, nine tenths, half, double. No decimal ever reaches the screen. Where there is no target (the moment, the night and day, the planned silence, the three things) the game says so, and later offers the player's own span back among decoys half again as long or two thirds of it, or pairs at three sizes with the player's relation in one, or asks which of their own three holds was longest and about how many times longer; a right pick is a right pick and nothing more is read into it. The half and the double are read as a product: on a clock a half times a double is one whole, and a product within about a seventh of that either way is "they undo each other, near enough". A middle is read as nearer the arithmetic mean ("the plain middle") or the geometric mean ("the middle by proportion") in proportion; two middles that disagree are read as none, and the game says that two is the least from which a scale can be read. With ordinary scatter in holding, a player who bisects consistently is read rightly about three evenings in four. In the crowd, a release within a few tenths of the low one's end is "on the moment"; holding until every voice has stopped is "to the end". The headline on the last page counts the three inner spans known again; the pictures show them beside what was offered, and the world-checked measures with the thing above and yours below.

What the book establishes

An argument, not a measurement: that the present has no length, that past and future are in the mind as memory and expectation, that time is not the motion of any body, and that we measure times in the mind, as a stretching that is also a scattering. How close a player's holds come is no evidence for any of this, or against it. Augustine starts from the fact that we do measure; the game lets you do it in each of the places he looks, and lets you feel where the measure was. The referee of the world-checked holds is the phone's clock, which Augustine would call one more motion measured by time; the game uses it and nothing on screen calls it time. The inner spans have no referee at all, which is the point.

What is left out

The opening prayers and the account of creation by the Word (XI.i–ix). The question what God was doing before he made heaven and earth (XI.x–xiii, xxx): one line on the last page. God's knowledge, which has no expectation and no memory (XI.xxxi). Foretelling the sunrise from the daybreak (XI.xviii). The potter's wheel (XI.xxiii), which earlier builds played and this one leaves to the sun. The hymn "Deus Creator omnium" and its syllables (XI.xxvii), which earlier builds played and this one does not. The half of a voice never heard before, which has no right answer: a paragraph on the last page. The names of the scales: the last page describes them without the words ordinal, interval and ratio. From Book X, everything but memory's store. And all of Augustine's life.

The note from the builder

The last page ends with a short note on the scales, in the terms economists use for them. Order alone: Vilfredo Pareto, Manuale di economia politica (1906), who showed that choice needs only an ordering of bundles. Equal differences: Oskar Lange, "The Determinateness of the Utility Function", Review of Economic Studies 1 (1934), and Franz Alt, "Über die Messbarkeit des Nutzens", Zeitschrift für Nationalökonomie 7 (1936), on what it takes to say that one gain is as large as another. Ratios from gambles: John von Neumann and Oskar Morgenstern, Theory of Games and Economic Behavior (1944), whose utility is fixed up to the choice of a zero and a unit, like temperature, and which no economist has ever supplied with a true zero. The names of the scales: S. S. Stevens, "On the Theory of Scales of Measurement", Science 103 (1946). The middle by proportion: the temporal bisection studies that followed John Gibbon, "Scalar Expectancy Theory and Weber's Law in Animal Timing", Psychological Review 84 (1977), where the point judged halfway between two durations falls near their geometric mean.
