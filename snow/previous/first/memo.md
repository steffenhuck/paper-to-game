# Snow's Cloudy Glass — design memo

## 0. Given

Mine; author title; no ideas. Every choice below is the engine's.

## 1. Paper

John Snow (1856), *Cholera and the Water Supply in the South Districts of London in 1854*, reprinted from the *Journal of Public Health*, October 1856; London: T. Richards. No DOI. Read from the Project Gutenberg transcription (eBook 66507, https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/66507), typeset text with six tables, so no numbers were read off a figure or a scan. The paper is Snow's own write-up of what is now called the grand experiment: the house-by-house inquiry behind the water-company chapter of *On the Mode of Communication of Cholera* (2nd ed., 1855), extended through the whole epidemic with the registrars' returns and compared, subdistrict by subdistrict, with what the water mix predicts.

## 2. What the paper hands over

**Primary: an estimate.** Counts. For twenty-one subdistricts where the two companies' pipes are intermixed (Table I, Snow's personal inquiry, first seven weeks) and eleven supplied by one company (Table II, Mr Whiting's): houses and people supplied by each company, deaths in houses on each supply, mortality per 10,000 supplied. For the first four weeks: 286 deaths in Southwark and Vauxhall houses against 14 in Lambeth houses, 107 against 8 per 100,000, "between thirteen and fourteen times as fatal". For the whole epidemic (Table V): 160 against 27 per 10,000, six to one. Table VI: for thirty-one subdistricts, the actual 1854 mortality beside the mortality calculated from the subdistrict's water mix at 160 and 27, close all down the column.

**Secondary: an argument about method.** The pipes go down the same streets and into the same courts; rich and poor alike; 300,000 people "divided into two groups by no other circumstance than the difference of water supply". Snow did not take the resident's word: he asked for the water-rate receipt, and he carried a chemical test, because the dry summer had let the sea run far up the Thames and Southwark and Vauxhall water held nearly forty grains of salt a gallon against Lambeth's one. And a worked example of what sloppy measurement does: the Board of Health, matching company lists to death lists, found 125 against 37 per 10,000, three and a half to one, because with deaths six times as common in one company's houses, five mistakes in six move a death from the guilty company to the innocent one.

**Tertiary: two readings.** The ratio falls as the epidemic goes on (14, then 8, then 5) as other ways of catching it join in. And it is not impurity in general: thousands drank from foul cisterns unharmed, and Broad Street's pump was drunk from for weeks before the one case that mattered.

## 3. The discovery, and its kind

**Identification.** Farr had already shown in 1853 that districts with more Lambeth water died less; but districts differ in everything. Snow's discovery is that the comparison can be made inside one street, house against the house next door, where nothing differs but the pipe, and that when it is, the difference is enormous and the parish stops mattering: the crowded, dirty, very poor riverside of Lambeth Church had low mortality because its water was mostly Lambeth's, and the thinly peopled, comparatively genteel Clapham and Battersea had high mortality because theirs was Southwark and Vauxhall's. With a counterintuitive edge for 1854 (airy and well-to-do died; crowded and poor lived) and a method lesson inside it: you need both the dead houses and the living ones, and you need to know, not be told, which water each drinks.

## 4. The core narrative

In London in 1854 two companies sold water through pipes under the same streets, so that one house drank from one and the house next door from the other. One company took its water from the river in the middle of town, where the sewers run in; the other had just moved its works upriver, above all that. Cholera came that summer. A doctor walked the streets where people had died, knocked on each door, and found out which water the house drank; when nobody knew, he took a glass of it and added a few drops that turn salt water milky, because the town water was salt with the tide and the upriver water was not. Behind the doors with crape, the glass nearly always went milky. Behind the living doors, it went milky about half the time. Rich street or poor, high or low, it was the water.

## 5. Who lives in the paper

- **Snow**, with his death list, his shoe leather and his bottle of silver nitrate. Controls which doors he knocks on and what he accepts as proof. Could be asked to anticipate what the glass will do.
- **Mr Whiting**, who did the eleven single-company subdistricts; **Dr Farr and the registrars**, who continued the inquiry from the twenty-seventh of August by asking at the door, and left a quarter of the deaths unascertained.
- **The Board of Health's clerks**, matching two lists in an office. Control the method; find three and a half to one.
- **The two companies.** Lambeth moved its intake to Thames Ditton between the epidemics; Southwark and Vauxhall stayed at Battersea Fields. One choice each, already made.
- **The residents**, who often do not know whose water they drink, and die at the rates in the tables.
- **The morbid matter** itself, which Snow says travels in what leaves a patient, by hand or by river, and which the game could cast as an agent.
- **The subdistricts**, each with its crowding, its riverside or its hill, and its water mix: the units in which the parish-versus-pipe argument is made.

## 7. The aim and the verdict

**Aim** (the sentence on the start screen, the top of the play screen, and the first line of How to play): *Find out whether the water makes the difference between the doors with crape and the doors without.*

**Yardstick during play**, in the aim's units: the four-pane window. Top row, doors with crape; bottom row, doors without; left pane brown (Battersea water), right pane blue (Thames Ditton water). Each knock puts one house in one pane. A line under it reads the rows: "Crape doors tried: 4, of them 3 brown. Living doors tried: 2, of them 1 brown." It describes; it does not judge.

**Verdict**, the lines the end screen leads with:
1. *What Snow found.* On the same streets, a house on Battersea water lost someone about seven times as often as a house on Thames Ditton water in these seven weeks, fourteen times in the first four; and the parish, crowded or genteel, low or high, made no difference once the water was known.
2. *What you found.* Behind the crape doors you tried, [nearly all / most / about half] drank Battersea water; behind the living doors, [about half / most / none tried]. By your knocks, a Battersea house wore crape about [N] times as often as a Thames Ditton house [or: more often than your knocks can count, since you found no crape behind a blue door / or: cannot be told, since you tried no living door].
3. *Where you part.* [If no living door: your list says only that the dead drank Battersea water; the Board could answer that so did most of the living, and you could not reply.] [If the number is near Snow's: your three streets agree with his twenty-one subdistricts.] [If far: the streets you knocked on were few; his were 658 deaths.] Plus the parish line: Battersea, airy and well-to-do, [n] crape doors; Lambeth Church, crowded and poor by the river, [m].
4. *Had yours stood.* If the question had been answered from lists instead of doors, the gap would have shrunk to three and a half to one, and the air would have kept the blame a while longer. [Where the player's own knocks were careless: and your streets would have been that evidence.]

Then the prediction street's result in words, the residents' word against the glass, the ratio falling as the epidemic went on, and the paper's line that it is not impurity but the specific matter.

## 8. Three concepts

**A. The Door (experimenter, discovery).** Three streets of eighteen houses, each a real subdistrict with its real water mix and its real death rates. Crape hangs on the doors where someone died: the record, shown before the player does anything. Seven knocks a street. Tap a door: the resident says what they think (often wrong, sometimes nothing); draw a glass; add the silver drops; the glass clouds (Battersea) or stays clear (Thames Ditton); the door is marked in that colour and a house goes into the window. *Thinking before pressing:* crape door or living door; after street one, whether the crape doors being brown means anything when the whole street might be brown. *After:* the glass, the mark, the window filling. *Moment:* the first living door that comes up blue when every crape door has come up brown; then Battersea, genteel and all brown, with crape on seven doors, after which Lambeth Church, crowded and poor and nearly all blue, with crape on one. *Fun:* anticipation (the drops fall), discovery (the window's two rows diverge), mastery (street two is knocked on with a plan). *Second playthrough:* different streets' draws, a different split of knocks, and the prediction street to beat.

**B. Farr's Return (controller, challenge).** The player is the Registrar-General in July 1854 with a budget of clerks and weeks, and decides the unit of the inquiry (district, subdistrict, house) and the method (company lists, registrars at the door, Snow in person with the test). The game returns the ratio that method finds, with the paper's numbers at the endpoints (Farr's districts, Simon's three and a half to one, Snow's fourteen), and the confounds each unit leaves standing. *Thinking:* cost against cleanliness. *Moment:* the house-level inquiry done from lists gives half the answer of the same inquiry done at the door, for a reason the screen shows (the mistakes run one way). *Fun:* discovery and agency. *Second playthrough:* another budget. Walkthrough: pressing "by house, from lists" and reading a number is a demonstration; the player never meets a street.

**C. The Morbid Matter (agent, strategic) — the reckless one.** The player is what leaves a cholera patient, and chooses where to go: into the bed linen and the hands of the house, into a cesspool three feet from a pump, or into the Thames at the sewer's mouth. A play screen with no words: the town as a map, the pipes as veins, the tide as a breathing line. The outcome is the shape of the outbreak the paper describes: sudden, violent and limited from a pump; gradual and extended from a river; slow and local by hand. *Thinking:* which route reaches most. *Moment:* the river route cannot enter the Thames Ditton pipe, which starts above the tide; the map shows the blue pipes staying dark. *Fun:* agency, discovery. *Second playthrough:* the other routes. Walkthrough: a strong opening image and then no second decision; the player learns a geography, not a comparison; and a toy whose numbers the paper does not supply (how far hands carry it) would be ours all the way down.

## 10. The pick, and why

**A, The Door.** The player is inside the paper at the exact place Snow was, with his instrument, doing his comparison by hand, and the paper's two lessons both land inside play: the living doors (the second row of the window is the denominator, and the stranger has to decide to fill it), and the parish against the pipe (two streets whose look says one thing and whose crape says the other). The sentence afterwards has no "guess": *I knocked on the doors with crape and the doors without, tested the water, and the crape doors were nearly all on the town's water while the street was half and half.* Originality: no game in the table has had the player build a two-by-two table by hand, and none has put a chemical test in their hands.

**Author test.** Everyone who teaches identification knows the grand experiment. What they have not done is knock: feel the pull toward the crape doors, notice the empty second row, and watch Battersea's railings come up brown seven times. And the Board of Health's three and a half to one, with Snow's own explanation of why the mistakes run one way, is a result most of them have not read.

**Runner-up: B, Farr's Return.** Switch if the author wants the method lesson (unit and measurement) as the spine rather than as the second row and a verdict line; its endpoints would then be built from the same tables.

**What the reckless concept would have given.** The image of a pipe the poison cannot enter because it starts above the tide, and the paper's three outbreak shapes. Kept from it: the line on the start screen that says where each company draws its water, and the paper's shapes on the About screen. Given up: the wordless map, real time, and the player as the poison.

## 11. The shape

Three knock streets of eighteen houses, seven knocks each; a fourth street with the company's books open, where the player marks five doors and a month passes. No presets beyond the streets, which are the paper's own subdistricts and ordered for the player: a mixed street first (Trinity, Newington: 55 per cent Battersea water, 51 against 7 per 10,000), then the genteel brown one (Battersea: 96 per cent Battersea water, 67 per 10,000), then the crowded blue one (Lambeth Church, part 1: 16 per cent Battersea water, 19 against 6), then a mixed street for the prediction (Borough Road: 57 per cent, 68 against 10). Score: none during play; the verdict is in words, with the prediction street's hits said in words. The seed is in the URL and on the verdict screen; another game draws new crape.

## 12. Controls, mechanism, feedback, moment

- **Control:** tap a door (preview: the door opens, the resident speaks, a glass is drawn; nothing is spent yet); *Add the silver drops* commits one knock. On the fourth street, tap doors to chalk a cross on five; *Come back in a month* commits.

- **Moment:** the first living door on street one (the bottom row starts); Battersea's seventh crape door on a street with railings; the verdict's number next to Snow's.

## 15. Divergences

- **A street stands for a subdistrict.** Eighteen houses carry the subdistrict's share of each company's houses (Tables I and II) and its per-house death rate for each water. Snow's units were deaths and houses supplied over whole subdistricts.
- **The clock and the thickness.** Deaths are drawn ten times as thick as they fell in the seven weeks, so that one street of eighteen shows what Snow needed thirty-two subdistricts to show; the ratio between the two waters on each street is the table's. Said on the first street and on the About screen.
- **The denominator by knocking.** Snow had the number of houses supplied by each company from the return made to Parliament; the player gets it by knocking at living doors. The comparison is the same (deaths by water over houses by water); the way of getting the second number is ours, and it is the deliberation.
- **Seven knocks a street** is ours, so that crape doors and living doors compete for the same shoe leather.
- **The resident's word** is wrong one time in seven and absent one time in four; the paper says residents often did not know and Snow did not rest content with their reply, and gives no rate. The Board's list-matching error, backed out of 160/27 against 125/37, is about one death in fourteen; we doubled it so that a player meets a wrong answer in a game.
- **Floors and caps on crape.** Street one has between two and five crape doors, Battersea at least four, Lambeth Church between one and three, so that the first street leaves knocks for living doors and the parish comparison cannot fail to appear; each is within a few per cent of what the unconstrained draw gives.
- **The prediction street** and the five marks are ours; the month's deaths are drawn at Borough Road's rates.
- **Battersea's one Lambeth house** dies at the Lambeth rate for all twenty-one mixed subdistricts (Table I's 6.8 per 10,000), since Table II reports no deaths among its 270 Lambeth-supplied people.
- **The window** is ours; Snow's tables are the same four numbers in rows.

## 16. Mechanism kind and claim boundaries

**Reconstructed.** Each street is a data-generating process built from one row of Table I or II: the row's share of houses on each company's water sets the street's eighteen houses; the row's deaths over houses supplied for each water, times ten, is each house's chance of crape in the seven weeks, drawn independently. The ratio between the two waters on each street is the row's. Everything else is ours and declared: the knock budget, the residents' words, the floors and caps on crape, the prediction street and its five marks, the four-pane window, and the verdict's estimate (deaths by water over houses by water, with the share of each water among crape doors and among living doors taken from the doors tested; a street with no tested door of one kind borrows the share pooled over the other streets).

- **Demonstrates:** how the two rows of a two-by-two table are gathered at the door; what the table looks like when the water is the cause (nearly all brown on top, the street's mix below); that a genteel street on the town's water wears more crape than a crowded one on the upriver water.
- **Assumes:** independence of deaths across houses (Snow's own view is that the sick infect their households, which would cluster deaths; the per-house rate is unchanged); the compression; the residents' error rates; that three streets of eighteen stand for twenty-one subdistricts.
- **The paper establishes:** the counts and rates in its tables; the identification argument (nothing but the water distinguished the two populations in the mixed streets); the Board of Health's attenuated ratio and why the mistakes run one way. It does not establish anything about any particular house, nor a rate at which residents misreport their supply.

**Where the discoveries come from.** The top row being brown and the bottom row mixed follows from the tables (the ratio of rates is the row's), not from any rule of ours: the calibration check reruns the sensible player at compression 5 and 20 and without floors, and at five or nine knocks, and the water is found guilty in nine games in ten either way. The parish line (Battersea's crape against Lambeth Church's) holds in 99 per cent of unconstrained draws; the floors only remove the last one per cent. The empty second row, the trap, is a design choice (the budget) and is presented as one: the verdict says it is what Parliament's return gave Snow.

## 17. Calibration plan and result

`calibration.js`, 67 checks, all passing (`calibration-result.txt`): every street row's rate per 10,000 recomputed from its deaths and population (within 0.15); the supplied population is houses times one constant for both companies on each street, so deaths over houses carries the table's ratio; the aggregates the verdict quotes (47.2 and 6.8; 107 and 8, "between thirteen and fourteen"; 916 against 180; 160 against 27; 125 against 37, "three and a half to one"); the list error that turns six to one into three and a half (about one death in fourteen, with nine mistakes in ten moving a death off the Southwark company) and the residents' rate as its double; forty grains of salt against one; each street's house split within six points of the row's share and its per-house odds ratio equal to the row's; compression leaving the expected pooled ratio (8.0) unchanged; over 3,000 unconstrained seeds the realised ratio per street within 10 per cent of the row's (20 per cent for Battersea, which has one blue house); crape within floor and cap over 3,000 seeds, with the bound binding in 22, 14, 35 and 0 per cent of draws; Battersea above Lambeth Church in 99 per cent of unconstrained draws; residents one in four unsure and one in seven wrong; determinism; the rules of a knock, a mark and the month; the strategies' bands; the prediction's hits (2.6 against 0.4); robustness at compression 5 and 20, no floors, five and nine knocks; no decimal, NaN or undefined in 10,800 verdict lines.
