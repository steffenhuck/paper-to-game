The paper. John Snow (1856), Cholera and the Water Supply in the South Districts of London in 1854, reprinted from the Journal of Public Health, October 1856 (London: T. Richards). No DOI; the text is on Project Gutenberg as eBook 66507. Game built from this paper; the paper is the authority where the two disagree.

What the paper found

Between the epidemics of 1849 and 1854 the Lambeth Company moved its intake from the middle of London to Thames Ditton, above the tide and the sewers; the Southwark and Vauxhall Company stayed at Battersea Fields. Their pipes ran down the same streets, into the same courts, often to neighbouring houses, and supplied rich and poor alike: three hundred thousand people divided by nothing but their water. Snow went to the house of every cholera death in the mixed subdistricts for seven weeks, asked for the water-rate receipt, and where that failed tested the water for salt (the tide had made Southwark and Vauxhall water brackish, forty grains a gallon against one). In the first four weeks the Southwark and Vauxhall houses died at 107 per 100,000 against 8: thirteen to fourteen times. Over his seven weeks, 47 against 7 per 10,000, in every subdistrict alike; over the whole epidemic, 160 against 27. Crowded, poor riverside subdistricts on Lambeth water died little; airy, genteel Clapham and Battersea on Southwark water died much. The Board of Health, matching company lists to death lists in an office, found only three and a half to one, because with deaths six times as common in one company’s houses, five mistakes in six move a death from the guilty company to the innocent one.

How the game is built

Mechanism kind: reconstructed. Each street is a small data-generating process built from one row of Snow’s Table I or II. Trinity (Newington), Battersea, Lambeth Church part 1 and Borough Road each have eighteen houses, split between the two waters in the row’s proportion of houses supplied, and each house wears crape with the row’s probability for its water (deaths over houses supplied in the seven weeks) multiplied by ten, so that one street shows what Snow needed thirty-two subdistricts to show. The ratio between the two waters on each street is the row’s. Battersea’s one Lambeth house dies at the Lambeth rate for all twenty-one mixed subdistricts, since the row reports no deaths among its 270 Lambeth-supplied people.

What the game demonstrates: how the two rows of a two-by-two table are gathered at the door, and what the table looks like when the water is the cause: nearly all brown in the top row, the street’s mix in the bottom. What it assumes: everything a street of eighteen has to invent: which house is on which pipe, which house dies (drawn independently at the row’s rate), the residents’ words (wrong one time in seven, absent one in four: our numbers), seven knocks a street, floors and caps on the number of crape doors so that the first street leaves knocks for living doors and the parish comparison cannot fail to appear, and the prediction street with its five marks. The verdict’s “times as often” is deaths by water over houses by water, as Snow divided them, with the share of each water among crape doors and among living doors estimated from the doors you tested. What the paper establishes: the counts and rates in its tables, and the identification argument: that within the mixed streets nothing but the water distinguished the two populations. It does not establish, and the game does not claim, anything about any particular house, nor about the residents’ knowledge of their supply beyond Snow’s remark that he did not rest content with their reply.

Divergences

  • Snow had the number of houses on each water from the return made to Parliament; the player gets it by knocking at living doors. The comparison is the same; the way of getting its second row is the game’s, and it is the thing to think about.
  • Deaths are drawn ten times as thick as they fell, and the clock is the first seven weeks of the epidemic; the later weeks, when the gap narrowed, are read on the verdict screen.
  • “Battersea water” and “Thames Ditton water” are the game’s names for the Southwark and Vauxhall Company’s and the Lambeth Company’s supply, by where each was drawn. “The silver drops” are silver nitrate, which throws down white silver chloride in salt water.
  • The deaths from pumps, ditches and the river itself, the water-rate receipt as a separate act, Table VI’s thirty-one-subdistrict comparison, the Exeter and Hull comparison, the 1849 outbreaks and Broad Street are left out or reduced to a line.
