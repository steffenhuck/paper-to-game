London · July 1854

Snow’sCloudy Glass

Two water companies pipe the same streets of south London, so that one house drinks from one and the house next door from the other. The Southwark and Vauxhall Company pumps from the Thames at Battersea, inside the town, where the sewers run in and the tide brings salt up from the sea. The Lambeth Company moved its works upriver to Thames Ditton in 1852, above both.

Cholera came in July. Where someone has died, black cloth hangs on the door: crape. The parish doctor blames the air. Dr John Snow wants two houses that are different in one thing only.

You have a morning’s knocks. At a door you can ask whose water it is, which is cheap and sometimes wrong; or draw a glass and add a few drops of silver, which turn salt water milky. The town’s water is salt with the tide; the upriver water is not. The glass is never wrong.

What your hands will do

Pick a crape door.
Set a door against it.
Ask, or draw a glass.

How to play

Find out whether it is the water: set a crape door beside a door without, and know what each drinks.

The two streets

Twelve houses each. Where someone has died of cholera, black cloth hangs over the door: crape. Battersea is thinly peopled and genteel, and Battersea Fields are marsh. Waterloo Road is crowded, poor and by the river. Every house is on one of two pipes, and you cannot see which. Battersea water is the Southwark and Vauxhall Company’s, pumped from the river inside the town. Thames Ditton water is the Lambeth Company’s, from above the town.

A pair

  1. Tap a door with crape. Then tap a door without crape to set against it: the door next to it, or any door on either street.
  2. Under the two doors a card says what is different between them. Two doors on different streets differ in the street (the air, the people) as well as the water, so whatever happens, you cannot say which did it. Two doors on the same street differ in the water alone, if their water differs; or in nothing.
  3. Choose how to know their water. Ask at both doors costs one knock: the people say which company they think they are on, and one time in seven they are wrong, and one time in four they cannot say. Draw a glass at each costs two knocks: salt water turns milky with the silver drops, so milky means Battersea water and clear means Thames Ditton water, and the glass is never wrong.
  4. Each door is painted the colour of its water: solid where the glass said it, an outline where only the word did. Tap an outlined door to check its word with the glass, for one knock.

The window

Four panes, built from pairs made in the same street. The top row holds the crape doors, the bottom row the doors set against them; brown on the left, blue on the right; outlined where known only by word. The line under it says what the pairs say so far. Pairs made across the two streets are counted on their own line, because they cannot choose between the street and the water.

The parish doctor

He offers pairs for nothing: a crape door in Battersea against a living door in Waterloo Road, by what the people told him. You may take them or not.

The book and the Board

You have eight knocks. Once two pairs are in the book, or when the knocks are gone, close it and send the Board one sentence: the marsh air, the town’s water, or that these doors cannot say. The verdict reads your sentence against your own book and against Snow.
