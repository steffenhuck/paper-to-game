The paper. John Snow (1856), Cholera and the Water Supply in the South Districts of London in 1854, reprinted from the Journal of Public Health, October 1856 (London: T. Richards). No DOI; the text is on Project Gutenberg as eBook 66507. Game built from this paper; the paper is the authority where the two disagree.

What the paper found

Between the epidemics of 1849 and 1854 the Lambeth Company moved its intake from the middle of London to Thames Ditton, above the tide and the sewers; the Southwark and Vauxhall Company stayed at Battersea Fields. Their pipes ran down the same streets, into the same courts, often to neighbouring houses, and supplied rich and poor alike: three hundred thousand people divided by nothing but their water. Snow went to the house of every cholera death in the mixed subdistricts for seven weeks, asked for the water-rate receipt, and where that failed tested the water for salt (the tide had made Southwark and Vauxhall water brackish, forty grains a gallon against one). In the first four weeks the Southwark and Vauxhall houses died at 107 per 100,000 against 8: thirteen to fourteen times. Over his seven weeks, 507 deaths in 16,038 houses against 98 in 20,554, about seven to one, and “nearly the same throughout” the subdistricts. The crowded, dirty, very poor riverside subdistricts of Lambeth Church and Waterloo Road, mostly on Lambeth water, died little; the thinly peopled, comparatively genteel Clapham and Battersea, on Southwark water, died much. The parish and the air had nothing to do with it once the water was known.

The idea the game teaches

A cause that varies from house to house can be told apart from everything that varies from district to district, if you compare neighbours. Between the inland side of town and the riverside, a hundred things differ, and the water is one of them; any difference in deaths can be read as any of the hundred. Between two houses on the same street, one on each pipe, only the water differs. That is why Snow walked the mixed streets and not the all-Southwark or all-Lambeth districts, and why the Board of Health’s comparison of districts could be answered and his could not.

How the game is built

Mechanism kind: reconstructed. The map’s six streets are six of Snow’s subdistricts, ordered from the river inland: Waterloo Road (first part), Lambeth Church (first part), Trinity, Borough Road, Kennington (first part) and Clapham. Each has six houses, with the subdistrict’s real share of houses on the Southwark and Vauxhall Company’s water (23, 16, 55, 57, 85 and 98 per cent, Tables I and II), placed at random within the street so that a house is often on the other pipe from its neighbour, as the paper says. Each house wears crape with the paper’s probability for its water, deaths over houses supplied in the first seven weeks over the twenty-one mixed subdistricts (Table I totals), multiplied by ten so that a map of thirty-six shows it. The ratio between the two waters is the paper’s, about seven.

What the game demonstrates: that tests placed on one side of town cannot tell the water from the side, and tests placed in the same street can; and what the map looks like when the water is the cause and the pipes are sorted by district: crape inland, where the town’s water is, and in the middle streets at the brown doors only. What it assumes: everything a map of thirty-six has to invent: which house is on which pipe, which house dies (drawn independently at the paper’s rate), the same rate for every street (the paper reports it as nearly the same throughout), the floors on the draw (five to ten crape doors; more inland than by the river, which the tables’ shares give in nine maps in ten unaided; at least one street with a brown crape house and a blue house without), eight tests, the parish doctor and his inland air, and the sentence. The line under the map is deaths over tested houses by colour, as Snow divided deaths by houses supplied, once over all tests and once over the streets where both waters were tested. What the paper establishes: the counts and rates in its tables, and the identification argument. It does not establish anything about any particular house or street beyond the shares and rates it reports.

Divergences

  • Snow had the number of houses on each water from the return made to Parliament; the player gets a street’s mix by testing houses on it. The comparison is the same; the sampling is the game’s, and where to sample is the thing to think about.
  • Deaths are drawn ten times as thick as they fell, and the clock is the first seven weeks; the later weeks are read on the verdict screen.
  • “Battersea water” and “Thames Ditton water” are the game’s names for the two companies’ supply, by where each was drawn. “The silver drops” are silver nitrate, which throws down white silver chloride in salt water. The doctor and his inland air are the game’s example of the kind of reading a comparison between districts allows; the paper quotes no doctor. The order of the streets from the river inland is schematic; Kennington and the middle streets are not described in the paper.
  • Deaths from pumps, ditches and the river itself, the water-rate receipt, the Board of Health’s list-matching error, Table VI, Exeter and Hull, the 1849 outbreaks and Broad Street are left out or reduced to a line.
