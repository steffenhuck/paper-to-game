The paper. John Snow (1856), Cholera and the Water Supply in the South Districts of London in 1854, reprinted from the Journal of Public Health, October 1856 (London: T. Richards). No DOI; the text is on Project Gutenberg as eBook 66507. Game built from this paper; the paper is the authority where the two disagree.

What the paper found

Between the epidemics of 1849 and 1854 the Lambeth Company moved its intake from the middle of London to Thames Ditton, above the tide and the sewers; the Southwark and Vauxhall Company stayed at Battersea Fields. Their pipes ran down the same streets, into the same courts, often to neighbouring houses, and supplied rich and poor alike: three hundred thousand people divided by nothing but their water. Snow went to the house of every cholera death in the mixed subdistricts for seven weeks, asked for the water-rate receipt, and where that failed tested the water for salt (the tide had made Southwark and Vauxhall water brackish, forty grains a gallon against one). In the first four weeks the Southwark and Vauxhall houses died at 107 per 100,000 against 8: thirteen to fourteen times. Over his seven weeks, 47 against 7 per 10,000, in every subdistrict alike; over the whole epidemic, 160 against 27. Crowded, poor riverside subdistricts on Lambeth water died little; airy, genteel Clapham and Battersea on Southwark water died much. The Board of Health, matching company lists to death lists in an office, found only three and a half to one, because with deaths six times as common in one company’s houses, five mistakes in six move a death from the guilty company to the innocent one.

How the game is built

Mechanism kind: reconstructed. Each street is a small data-generating process built from one row of Snow’s Table I or II. Battersea and Waterloo Road (first part) each have twelve houses, split between the two waters in the row’s proportion of houses supplied and placed at random, so that a house is often on the other pipe from its neighbours, as the paper says; and each house wears crape with the row’s probability for its water (deaths over houses supplied in the seven weeks) multiplied by ten, so that a street of twelve shows it. The ratio between the two waters on each street is the row’s. Battersea’s one Lambeth house dies at the Lambeth rate for all twenty-one mixed subdistricts, since the row reports no deaths among its 270 Lambeth-supplied people.

What the game demonstrates: that two doors can only answer a question about the one thing that differs between them (a pair across the streets differs in the street as well as the water; a pair in the same street in the water alone, or in nothing); and that a count built from what people say at the door drifts toward the Board of Health’s number for the paper’s reason: nearly every crape door is on the town’s water, so nearly every wrong word moves a death from the guilty water to the innocent one. What it assumes: everything a street of twelve has to invent: which house is on which pipe, which house dies (drawn independently at the row’s rate), what the residents say (wrong one time in seven, unable to say one time in four, with a floor of one wrong word at a Battersea crape door so that the drift can be met: our numbers), the parish doctor and his reading of the marsh, eight knocks, the cost of a word against a glass, the floors on crape (three to seven doors in Battersea, one or two in Waterloo Road, one of them brown with a blue door beside it), and the sentence for the Board. The line under the window is deaths over doors by colour from the pairs you made in the same street, as Snow divided deaths by houses supplied. What the paper establishes: the counts and rates in its tables; the identification argument, that within the mixed streets nothing but the water distinguished the two populations; and the Board’s attenuated ratio and why its mistakes ran one way. It does not establish anything about any particular house, nor a rate at which residents misreport their supply beyond Snow’s remark that he did not rest content with their reply.

Divergences

  • Snow had the number of houses on each water from the return made to Parliament; the player gets the second row of the window from the doors set against the crape doors. The comparison is the same; the pair is the game’s way of making it, and what to set against what is the thing to think about.
  • Deaths are drawn ten times as thick as they fell, and the clock is the first seven weeks; the later weeks, when the gap narrowed, are read on the verdict screen.
  • “Battersea water” and “Thames Ditton water” are the game’s names for the Southwark and Vauxhall Company’s and the Lambeth Company’s supply, by where each was drawn. “The silver drops” are silver nitrate, which throws down white silver chloride in salt water. The marsh is the game’s example of the kind of reading a comparison between parishes allows; the paper does not quote a doctor.
  • Deaths from pumps, ditches and the river itself, the water-rate receipt as a separate act, Table VI’s thirty-one-subdistrict comparison, Exeter and Hull, the 1849 outbreaks and Broad Street are left out or reduced to a line.
