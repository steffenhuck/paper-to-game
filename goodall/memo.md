## 2. What the paper hands over

**Primary: an argument**, built from a review. Tool-using is "the use of an external object as a functional extension of mouth or beak, hand or claw, in the attainment of an immediate goal" (p. 195); nest-building and string-pulling are manipulation of materials, not tool-use. Tool-use is found in species far apart on the phylogenetic scale, so tool-use as such shows nothing about intelligence (the ant-lion that flings sand is no more "gifted" than the dragonfly larva with a hinged jaw, p. 244). It becomes interesting "when the motor patterns available to a given animal for the manipulation of objects can be adapted to new situations" (p. 244). Each animal's tool-use grows out of a movement it already has: the Egyptian vulture throws stones at ostrich eggs "using exactly the same movements as those seen when it throws a smaller egg at the ground" (p. 203); the banded mongoose, unable to hurl a dummy ostrich egg between its legs, hurls stones at it instead (p. 205). In the chimpanzee, tool-use develops in each individual from manipulative play, trial and error, observation of others and occasional insight (pp. 240–243); a pattern practised out of context "is readily available and may be adapted to new purposes" (p. 241). Only the chimpanzee, apart from man, adapts tool-use to "a rather wide variety of problems"; only man uses a tool to make a tool (pp. 240, 244–245). Gifted individuals and observational learning may make cultures (p. 245).

**Secondary: estimates**, from Goodall's own Gombe observations, on the development of termite-fishing (pp. 228–231):
- Adults push five to eight inches of the tool into the hole; withdraw it "after a slight pause"; pick the termites off with their lips. Tools are prepared (leaves stripped, strips pulled from grass blades, a bent end bitten off); several are picked at once; tools are often selected when the nest is as much as 100 yards away and out of sight (Fig. 16: about 60 yards); no chimpanzee was seen to move out of sight of a nest to fetch a new tool; a tool may be poked in two or three times and then discarded "as though it is the tool that is at fault".
- Infants under two: no tool-use for termites; from nine months they watch their mothers intently.
- Two to three years: tools used in the right context, but "inappropriate material (too short, too thick, etc.) and clumsy technique (the tool was often pulled from a hole with a jerking movement which would have dislodged any insect that had bitten on)". In 22 bouts (a few seconds to five minutes), two termites caught.
- Three to four years: tools sometimes longer but often too flexible (Fig. 17: "too long, too flexible"); pushed in "only one or two inches".
- Four years: "a more adult technique, although their tools were usually shorter"; bouts up to 15 minutes against under five for three-year-olds.
- Infants picked up and used tools that adults discarded (p. 243).

**Also handed over: the sponge** (pp. 232–233, 242). Adults at Gombe drink from hollows by stripping leaves, chewing them briefly ("thus increasing their absorbancy") and pushing the mass in. A three-and-a-half-year-old, after first dipping his hand, poked in a piece of dry grass "using movements similar to those observed during termite feeding. Each time he put it to his mouth, he chewed the end: eventually, therefore, the stem was a tiny crumpled mass — a minute 'sponge'" (Fig. 18). Goodall's gloss (p. 242): "the repetition of such a pattern during infant play may, on occasion, lead to a new tool-using technique".

Formalised here as an explicit toy (section 12): a passage with bends, termites that bite on and let go, a stem with length, thickness, stiffness, leaves and a tip that wears; water that a plain stem barely lifts and a crumpled one soaks up.

## 3. The discovery, and its kind

**A mechanism made intelligible**, with a counterintuitive edge. The intuitive view (in 1970 and now) is that a tool-using animal is a clever animal, and that a tool is a special thing. The paper: a tool is an ordinary movement given a new object, and any animal can have one by evolution; what marks the chimpanzee is that its movements are learned slowly, by playing, trying and watching, and then carried to new problems. The game's discovery is played in two steps: the player learns termite-fishing the slow way, making exactly the mistakes Goodall recorded at each age; then the movement they learned turns out to drink water.

## 4. The core narrative

There is food inside the termite mound, but you can't get your hand in. Your mother pokes a grass stem down the hole, waits, and pulls it out covered in termites. You try. Your stem is too floppy and folds up; the next one is too fat to go in; when one finally goes in, you yank it out so fast that every termite falls off. It takes young chimpanzees years to get this right, and they learn it by trying and by watching their mothers. Then one day there is water in a hole in a tree, too deep for your lips, and you poke a stem in, the same way you fish for termites, and it comes out wet.

## 5. Who lives in the paper

- **The young chimpanzee** (two to four years old): chooses a stem, prepares it or not, pushes it in, waits, pulls it out; watches its mother or not; picks up her discarded tool. Could be asked to anticipate: will this stem go round the bend; how slowly is slow enough? The player, in the pick.
- **The mother** (an adult): selects tools far from the nest, prepares them, fishes at an adult's depth. Kept as the one the player can watch, and as the measure of an adult's catch.
- **Goodall, the observer**: binoculars and a notebook; she records each bout and what age it looks like. Kept as the voice of the notebook (red pencil), not as the player.
- **The termites** (*Macrotermes bellicosus*): bite on to whatever comes into their passage. Kept as the mechanism.
- **The Egyptian vulture, the banded mongoose, the seriemas, the bald eagle, the sea otter**: each has one or two movements that became a tool. Candidate players for concept C; kept on the last page.
- **The zoo experimenters** (Dekker in Amsterdam with the seriemas, McGinnis with the mongooses, the van Lawicks with plaster eggs at Ngorongoro): set an animal a problem and watch. Candidate experimenter, concept B.
- **The low-ranking male with the paraffin cans** (p. 210): rose to the top by banging cans in his charging display. Considered (section 14), cut.

**What this game does differently.**

- **Position: the subject.** Not a party at a table, not the experimenter, not a builder: the player is the young animal the paper studies, with the skill being learned in their own hand, while the paper's observer writes them up. *I picked my stems sixty yards away, stripped the leaves, pushed one in six inches and drew it out slowly.*
- **Format: a challenge of mastery against a clock, then one new problem.** One afternoon, twelve dips' worth of daylight, then a hollow with five. Nobody opposes the player, nothing is surveyed, there is no par: the player gets better, visibly, for reasons they can name, and then the skill meets a problem it was not learned for.
- **Device: the stem itself, drawn in section, coming out of the mound beaded with termites or bare; and Goodall's age ruler**, on which a red-pencil tick lands after every dip (two years, three, four, adult). No circles, no spot of light, no album of panels, no loom.
- **Chassis: graphite on a squared field notebook** (Drawn, by the Given). Cream and graphite where Curie had white and flat colour; a typewriter and a pencil hand where Curie had a comic title face and comic lettering; pencil-boxed paper slips where Curie had ink caption boxes; nothing flat, everything shaded with a pencil.

## 7. The aim and the verdict

**The aim** (start screen; top of the mound screen; first line of How to play): *Fish termites out of the mound with a stem of grass, before your mother moves on.*

The yardstick during play is in the aim's units: a tally of termites eaten, in pencil strokes of five, which grows the moment a stem comes out with termites on it; and the afternoon, twelve small suns across the page, one shaded per dip. Every stem that comes out is visibly a success or a failure at once: termites on it, or none.

**The second aim** (the hollow, a surprise: not announced on the start screen): *Drink the rainwater in the hollow. Your lips can't reach it.* Yardstick: sips, drawn as a mouth-level gauge that fills toward "enough".

**The verdict** (the notebook's last page), three lines composed from what the player did, then the age ruler with every dip ticked on it:

1. *What you did:* "You caught 26 termites this afternoon; your mother, about 70. Your first dips looked like a two-year-old's (a stick too thick to go in); your last, like an adult's (a stiff stem, stripped, six inches in, out slowly). You learned it by trying, and by watching your mother once. Then you pushed your stem into the hollow, the way you fish, chewed the end, and drank."
2. *What the paper says:* "Goodall watched young chimpanzees take two years over what you did in an afternoon. Between two and three: the wrong stems, and a jerk that shakes the termites off; two termites in twenty-two tries. At three, stems too long and too floppy, in an inch or two. At four, an adult's way, with a shorter tool. A three-and-a-half-year-old made the first sponge she saw from a stem of dry grass, by chewing it each time he sucked it."
3. *What it means:* "A tool on its own is nothing clever: a vulture smashes ostrich eggs with stones, but it throws them with the throw it uses on small eggs, and that is all it does. What Goodall found remarkable is a movement learned slowly, by playing, trying and watching, then carried to a new problem. No animal but us does that for as many problems as the chimpanzee."

No score number; the tally is a count of what was eaten, and the verdict says what it means in words.

## 8. Three concepts

**A. Goodall's Long Grass** (subject: a young chimpanzee; a challenge of mastery against a clock, then a new problem).
- *Controls:* in the long grass, tap a stem to hold it (it is drawn held out over a ruler, drooping as much as it really does), TAKE IT (three at most), STRIP THE LEAVES. At the mound: one gesture, a vertical drag on the section drawing (down pushes the stem in, letting go leaves it there, up draws it out); BITE OFF THE END; WATCH YOUR MOTHER; PICK ANOTHER STEM NEARBY. At the hollow: the same drag, CHEW THE END, PICK SOME LEAVES.
- *Deliberation:* which stems to carry when the hole cannot be seen (long or short, thin or thick, floppy or stiff, leafy or bare); how deep, how long to wait, how slowly to pull; whether to spend a dip watching her or trying; bite the bent end off or switch; and at the hollow, what on earth to do.
- *After:* the stem moving in the passage, folding at a bend or not, stopping or going on; termites arriving and clinging; termites falling off during a fast pull; the count; the typed line; the red-pencil tick on Goodall's ruler.
- *Moment:* the first slow pull of a stiff stem six inches in, after a fast one that shook them all off: the stem comes up beaded with termites. Then the hollow, where nothing has been explained, and the termite stem comes out wet.
- *Fun:* agency (the drag is the technique; a slow hand is rewarded visibly), mastery (dip 8 is better than dip 2 for reasons the player can name: firm, deep, slow), discovery (the notebook names the age each mistake belongs to; the stem drinks), anticipation (will it go round the bend?).
- *Second play:* the other way of learning (watch first and copy; or never watch); another bunch of grass with different stems; a sponge from leaves instead of the stem, or the stem instead of leaves.

**B. The Plaster Egg** (experimenter: the zoo tester; discovery).
- *Controls:* five animals in turn (Egyptian vulture, seriema, banded mongoose, bald eagle, chimpanzee), one plaster ostrich egg; the player chooses what to put beside it (a stone, an anvil rock, a stick, a small egg, nothing) and runs three tests per animal, predicting before each whether the animal will use a tool.
- *Deliberation:* which object will this animal use, given what it does with food already?
- *Moment:* the mongoose tries to hurl the giant egg backwards between its legs (Fig. 4), fails, and then hurls a stone at it the same way (Fig. 5).
- *Fun:* discovery, anticipation. *Second play:* little; the animals answer the same way.
- *Why it loses:* the player watches more than acts, and the core loop is a prediction; it is Curie's position (experimenter) and close to Curie's format (a survey under a budget); the paper's experiments are tiny (two seriemas, two mongooses, four tests), so most outcomes would be ours.

**C. The Throw** (agent, of a strange kind: the player is a movement; **the reckless one**).
- *Controls:* the player is the head-flick an Egyptian vulture uses to smash a small egg on the ground, drawn as a pencil arc. It cannot change its shape. Drag an object into it and release toward a target. It passes from body to body: the vulture, the mongoose's backward hurl, the bald eagle's beak-throw (Fig. 3), the chimpanzee's threat gesture (Fig. 13).
- *Deliberation:* in each body, the problem the movement cannot solve with its usual object, and what else it could carry.
- *Moment:* the vulture faces an ostrich egg too big to lift and the throw has nothing to throw, until the player drags a stone into the arc. Then the chimpanzee, threatening a baboon with a banana in hand: let go mid-gesture and the banana flies (p. 222).
- *Fun:* discovery, a little agency. *Second play:* none to speak of.
- *Why it is reckless:* the protagonist is an abstraction with no body and no wants; almost nothing on screen can be explained in words without giving the answer; and after the first stone every body is the same trick.

Also considered and dropped: **The Cans** (agent: the low-ranking male who bangs paraffin cans to the top, p. 210). A strategic display game against higher-ranking males. One anecdote, a payoff entirely ours, and a story about status, not tools.

## 9. The first five minutes

**A (the pick).** *0:00.* A cream page ruled in faint squares. A binocular view, two overlapping circles, and inside it a pencil drawing of an old female chimpanzee at a termite mound with a grass stem between finger and thumb. Typed: GOODALL'S LONG GRASS. "Gombe Stream, Tanzania. The termite season." The aim on its own line: *Fish termites out of the mound with a stem of grass, before your mother moves on.* INTO THE LONG GRASS.

*0:15.* The long grass, drawn in pencil, swaying. Eight stems stand out darker. Typed: "Sixty yards from the mound: you can't see the hole from here. Take up to three stems." I touch the longest: it is lifted into a box marked *in your hand*, held out over a ruler: thirteen inches, thin, and it droops at the far end like a wet noodle. I touch a thick stick: eleven inches, stiff, fat. A leafy twig: ten inches, thin, stiff, leaves on it. A short twig, four inches. I think: long reaches further. I take the long grass blade, the thick stick and the leafy twig. WALK TO THE MOUND.

*0:50.* The mound, drawn as a section on the squared page: the hole at the top, a passage curving down past two bends to a chamber where termites wait, a ruler of inches beside it. My mother is a small figure at her own hole on the right. Twelve suns across the top; my tally, empty. "Drag down to push your stem in. Let go to leave it in. Drag up to pull it out. Termites bite on to whatever comes into their passage." I drag: the grass blade goes in, reaches the first bend at an inch and a half, and folds up above the hole. I drag harder; it folds more. I let go, wait, pull. "In 1½ inches: it folded at the first bend. No termites." Red pencil, in Goodall's hand, a tick on her ruler at *3 yrs*: "too long, too flexible: in only an inch or two (Fig. 17)". Hm.

*1:30.* The thick stick: it stops at the rim. "Too thick for the hole. No termites." Tick at *2 yrs*: "the wrong material". I frown. *1:45.* The leafy twig: the leaves catch on the rim. STRIP THE LEAVES (the box shows it bare before I press). It slides past the first bend, the second, down into the chamber: six and a half inches. I let go; small grey termites gather and cling to it. I pull fast. They fly off as it comes up; two are left. "Out fast: five shaken off. 2 termites." Tick at *2 yrs*: "a jerk, which would dislodge any insect that had bitten on". I see what I did.

*2:20.* Same twig, slowly, my thumb crawling up the glass. It comes out beaded. "In 6½ inches, a pause, out slowly. 7 termites." Tick at *adult*: "five to eight inches; out smoothly". I grin. *2:40.* Next dip, it folds at the first bend. "The end is bent." BITE OFF THE END (the box shows it an inch shorter). It goes in again. *3:00.* WATCH YOUR MOTHER: the binocular view, her stem coming out slowly; her section: seven inches, nine termites. "She picked hers in the long grass, before she came." She drops it beside her, bent at the end. PICK UP HER STEM. *3:20 to 4:00.* Five more dips, mostly adult ticks, one jerk when I hurry. The last sun is shaded: "Your mother gets up and moves off." 31 termites; hers, about 70.

*4:05.* A tree trunk in section, a hollow with rainwater three inches down. New aim: *Drink the rainwater in the hollow. Your lips can't reach it.* Five suns; a gauge for sips. Nothing tells me what to do. I try my hand: it won't go in past the knuckles. I take my twig and do what my thumb now does without thinking: push, wait, pull. It comes out wet: one sip. Again: two sips, the end a little chewed. CHEW THE END: the box shows a crumpled tip. Four sips. *4:50.* PICK SOME LEAVES, CHEW THEM: a wad; nine sips. Enough. Red pencil: "A 3½-year-old did this with a stem of dry grass: a minute sponge (Fig. 18)." *5:10.* The last page.

**C (the runner-up, and the reckless one).** *0:00.* A pencil arc on cream, and a line: "You are a throw: the flick of the head an Egyptian vulture uses to smash an egg on the ground. You can't change your shape; you can change what you hold." *0:15.* A small egg; I drag it into the arc and let go; it smashes. *0:30.* An ostrich egg, too big for the arc. I drag it: it won't lift. Pebbles lie about. I drag one into the arc and let go at the egg; on the fourth stone it cracks. Fig. 2, in pencil. A grin, once. *1:20.* The arc turns into a mongoose's backward hurl between its legs. The giant egg won't go between them (Fig. 4); a stone will. The same grin, smaller. *2:00.* The bald eagle's beak, a cricket two feet away: drag a stone, let go. *2:40.* A chimpanzee threatening a baboon, a banana in hand: the arm swings; I let go and the banana flies at the baboon. Is that the point? *3:30.* I have done one trick five times. "And I don't know what I'm supposed to feel" from the eagle onward: the paper's thesis is there in one gesture, but after the first stone the player has nothing to weigh.

## 10. The pick, and why

**A, Goodall's Long Grass.** The player is inside the paper at the place Goodall's own data are richest: the termite mound, where she recorded what a young chimpanzee gets wrong at each age. Every mistake the paper lists (too short, too thick, too flexible, in only an inch or two, pulled out with a jerk) is a move a stranger will make on their own, because each looks sensible (long reaches further; fat is strong; fast is efficient), and the notebook names the age each one belongs to. The deliberation is real twice over: what to carry when the hole cannot be seen, and how to move the thumb. The discovery lands inside three minutes (the slow pull), and the thesis inside five (the termite movement drinks). The sentence afterwards has no "guess": *I picked three stems sixty yards away, stripped one, pushed it in six inches and drew it out slowly; then I chewed it into a sponge.* Originality: no game in the table has made the player an animal learning a skill in its body, and none has scored a move on the paper's own developmental scale.

**Author test.** The colleague who sent the paper knows that chimpanzees fish for termites; he does not know, until his thumb tells him, how easy it is to pull too fast, or that a long grass blade is a three-year-old's mistake. A primatologist would recognise Fig. 16 (the tool picked sixty yards away), Fig. 17 (the floppy stem), Fig. 18 (the sponge), the bitten-off end, the discarded tool an infant picks up, and the five-to-eight inches. Goodall's notebook as the voice that ticks the player's age is the kind of joke the author would enjoy.

**Runner-up: C, The Throw.** Switch if the author wants the paper's breadth (birds, mongooses, otters, apes) played rather than read, in a ninety-second toy, and accepts that the player's choice is thin after the first stone.

**What the reckless concept would have given.** The paper's thesis felt as one movement finding new objects, across species, without a word. Kept from it: the hollow, where the movement the player has just learned is carried, unchanged, to a new problem; and the vulture, the mongoose and the eagle on the last page, each with the one throw that became its tool. Given up: playing the other species.

## 11. The shape (as rebuilt in revision 3)

One game in two places, about five minutes, and one drawing for each. At the mound: six suns (twelve until revision 4); each dip costs one. The whole afternoon is one pencil plate: the long grass on the left with five stems standing in it, the mound cut open in the middle with your hole, your mother at her own hole on the right, fishing every sun in the picture. You hold one stem at a time: touch a stem in the grass and it comes into your hand, drawn as itself; whatever you held goes on the heap at the foot of the mound, and the grass grows back, so the five kinds (a blade, a stem, a leafy twig, a stick, and one more) are always there to choose from. Then the hollow (five suns), the last page, How to play, About. No presets: the paper's regimes are ages, and the player passes through them by their own hand. Ordered for the player: the mound first, because the movement has to be learned before it can be carried; the hollow unannounced. Score: none; the tally of termites, the sips, the heap and the ticks on Goodall's ruler are the yardstick. The game ends once; ANOTHER AFTERNOON begins a new one with new grass.

## 12. Controls, mechanism, feedback, moment

- **Controls.** *Mound:* touch a stem standing in the grass to take it (one in hand at a time; the other goes on the heap); one gesture on the plate: press and hold, and the stem goes into the hole by itself at a grown-up's push and waits there while the termites climb on; let go, and it comes out, as a snatch if you let go within half a second of its arriving and gently otherwise (revision 6); if a stem is in the hole and another is touched, the game draws the first out at a slow pull and switches; two pencil tags on the plate, present only when they apply, are touches: *leaves on it: touch to strip them*, *bent end: touch to bite it off*; your mother's thrown-down stem lies between the holes with *her stem: touch to take*. Watching her costs nothing: she fishes in the picture after every sun. *Hollow:* press and what you hold goes in, let go and it comes out (speed is nothing to water); slips to hold the stem, your hand or the leaves; CHEW THE END; PICK SOME LEAVES. *Ants' nest (revision 6):* the same press and hold: the stick goes in, the ants run up it, let go before they reach your fingers; the clump is there to take a longer stick.
- **Mechanism (formalised from the argument; numbers in CONFIG).** The passage: entrance a quarter inch wide (a thick stick stops at the rim; unstripped leaves catch there), a bend at 1½ inches, a second at 4 inches, termites from 3 inches down to the end at 8. A stem has a length, a thickness, a stiffness (floppy, springy, stiff), leaves or none, and a tip that wears. It goes in as far as the finger pushes, unless stopped: a floppy stem (or a bent tip) folds at the first bend, a springy one at the second; the grip uses 1½ inches, so a four-inch twig reaches 2½. Termites bite on to the part of the stem below 3 inches at a rate per inch, gathering over a second or two after the stem stops moving. Each termite has a grip; a termite lets go when the stem moves faster than its grip, so a slow pull keeps them all and a flick keeps none. Every dip past the first bend wears the tip; three dips and it is bent. The mother fishes an adult's dip every sun. *Hollow:* the water stands 3 inches down; a hand reaches 1½ inches; a plain stem lifts a sip, a crumpled end more, a chewed wad of leaves most; each suck crumples a grass end a little more, as the paper says; twelve sips is enough. Each dip is classed on Goodall's scale by what happened: the wrong material (too thick, leaves on, too short) or a jerk: two years; a floppy stem in an inch or two: three; smooth and in three to five inches, or a short tool: four; smooth and in five to eight, with a full-length stem: adult.
- **Feedback.** The stem drawn in the passage moving with the thumb, folding where it folds; termites appearing along it and clinging; termites dropping as it comes up too fast; the eat (termites picked off the stem one by one into the tally); the result in the game's voice (typed); then Goodall's line in red pencil with a tick on her age ruler. At the hollow: the stem coming out dark with water, the drops, the gauge.
- **Moment.** The slow pull after the fast one. Then the hollow, with nothing explained.

## 13. The look

**Register: the paper's own figures** (Drawn family: a hand-drawn notebook and the naturalist's "drawing from photo"). It comes from the Given and from the paper itself, whose figures are either pencil-and-ink drawings traced from photographs (Figs. 1, 4, 13: "Drawing from photo by…") or grainy photographs of chimpanzees in long grass at Gombe (Figs. 7–20). The game draws everything as such a tracing: soft graphite contour, short hatch strokes for fur and shade, no fills, no flat colour. The notebook is the page everything sits on: squared, cream, with typed entries and a pencil hand in the margin, the way field notes were kept and typed up.

- **Palette:** notebook cream `#F1EBDC` (the page); graphite `#2E2F31` (every line, the text, the primary slip), with two lighter pressures of the same pencil, `#6F6E6B` (secondary lines, captions) and `#B3AEA3` (hatching, the grass behind); squared-rule blue `#CAD5D9` (the faint grid only); red pencil `#A9452F` (Goodall's hand only).
- **Type:** Courier Prime (Alan Dague-Greene, SIL OFL), the typewriter in which field notes were typed up, for the game's voice, labels, buttons and the title, regular and bold; Kalam (Indian Type Foundry, SIL OFL), a light pencil hand, for Goodall's red-pencil lines and the ruler's age marks. Both embedded as data.
- **Buttons:** paper slips: a typed label in capitals inside a pencil-ruled box (1.5 px graphite, corners barely rounded, the line roughened by an SVG pencil filter where the browser allows it, clean where it does not), cream on cream; the primary action is a slip shaded solid with graphite and typed in cream. No shadows, no colour.

## 15. Divergences

- **The clock.** An afternoon of six dips (twelve until revision 4) stands for the two years (about two to four) over which Goodall's infants learned; termite seasons last three or four months a year. Reason: the player has to pass through the ages by their own hand in a few minutes.
- **The passage, the termites and the stem are ours.** Bends, widths, depths of the chamber, the rate at which termites bite on, their grip, the wear of the tip, the length the fingers take up: chosen so that the paper's mistakes fail for the paper's reasons and the adult technique (five to eight inches, a slight pause, a smooth withdrawal) succeeds. The paper gives the depths, the mistakes and the preparations, not the mechanics.
- **The section view.** A chimpanzee feels the passage; it does not see it. The player sees in pencil what the hand would feel. Declared on About.
- **The mother's catch per dip and the termites per inch are ours.** The paper gives no adult rates; the infants' two in twenty-two bouts is the one count, and the toy reproduces it (section 17).
- **The age a dip is given** is our mapping of Goodall's descriptions to what happened in the dip; the ages themselves and their descriptions are hers.
- **The hollow.** Two of the paper's drinking techniques, the adult's chewed leaves and the infant's chewed stem, with sips, absorbencies and the threshold for "enough" ours. The infant who invented the stem sponge was three and a half; the game does not say how old the player is.
- **One mound, one hole, one hollow.** The paper's male who carried one tool half a mile past nests that were none of them ready is cut.
- **The grass beside the mound, one stem at a time, and the mother in the picture** (revision 3). The paper's adults choose tools as much as a hundred yards away, out of sight of the nest, and carry several; the game puts the grass beside the mound, offers one of each kind every round, and lets you hold one stem at a time, so that the choice of stem is the whole of each round. Watching the mother costs nothing: she fishes at her own hole in the picture after every sun, and infants, the paper says, watch intently. Declared on About.

## 16. Mechanism kind and claim boundaries

**Kind: a toy, calibrated to the paper's few numbers.** The paper hands over an argument and field descriptions, not a model. The game formalises the termite-fishing descriptions as a passage with two bends and a chamber, termites that bite on over a second or two and let go above a grip speed, and stems with a length, thickness, stiffness, leaves and a tip that wears; and the sponge as sips per dip by what is pushed in. It is calibrated to: the adults' five to eight inches; the three-to-four-year-olds' one or two inches with too-flexible tools; the two termites in twenty-two bouts of the two-to-three-year-olds; the order of the ages; and the paper's account of a chewed end soaking up more.

- **Demonstrates:** under its own assumptions, that the infants' mistakes Goodall lists fail for her reasons, and the adult technique succeeds; that the movement learned at the mound drinks at the hollow once the end is crumpled.
- **Assumes:** the inside of the mound, the termites' bite and grip, the wear of a tip, the stems' lengths, the clock (twelve dips for two years), our mapping of a dip to an age, the sips. The player sees the passage; a chimpanzee feels it.
- **The paper establishes:** the observed ages, mistakes, depths, the two in twenty-two, the preparations, the grass and leaf sponges, at one site in the 1960s; its developmental account (play, trial and error, observation, insight) and the cultural point are reasoned from observation, not tested.

**Which discoveries come from the paper and which from the rules.** (1) *The infants' mistakes catch nothing and the adult way catches several:* from the paper (each failure is one Goodall names, with her depths); the size of the catch is ours. (2) *A slow pull keeps the termites, a jerk loses them:* from the paper ("a jerking movement which would have dislodged any insect that had bitten on"); the grip speeds are ours. (3) *Choosing out of sight, carrying spares, biting a bent end, picking up her stem:* the behaviours are the paper's; that they pay (in termites per afternoon) follows from our clock and wear, and the game presents them as what chimpanzees do, not as findings. (4) *Watching once pays; watching four times costs more:* a design consequence of the clock, not a claim of the paper, which says only that infants watch and that observational learning "might also play a role". The verdict reports how the player learned, and does not say which way is better. (5) *The termite movement drinks:* the paper's infant did exactly this; the sips are ours.
