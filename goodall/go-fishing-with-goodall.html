What it means

How to play

You are a young chimpanzee. Fish termites out of the mound with a stem of grass, before your mother moves on.

The picture

The big drawing is the whole afternoon. On the left is the long grass, with five stems standing in it. In the middle is the termite mound, cut open so you can see inside: the hole at the top, the tunnel going down, and the termites waiting at the bottom. On the right, your mother is fishing at her own hole. Watch her as much as you like.

Picking a stem

Touch a stem in the grass, and it is in your hand. Each stem is different. A grass blade is wide and floppy. A grass stem is thin and stiff, with a seed head on top. A twig has leaves on it. A stick is thick. The line under the picture tells you about the stem you are holding. If you pick a new stem, the old one is dropped on the heap by the mound, and the grass grows back, so you can always try another.

Fishing

Press on the mound and keep your finger down. Your stem goes into the hole by itself, as far as it can. Then it waits. The termites bite on to it, and you can watch them climb on. Let go, and the stem comes out. Whatever is on it, you eat.

Two things to know. If you let go too soon, you yank the stem out, and the termites fall off. If you wait a little, you pull it out gently, and they stay on. And some stems do not work: a stick is too thick for the hole, a floppy blade folds up, a twig with leaves on gets stuck at the top. When that happens, a little note on the picture tells you what went wrong.

Every go costs one sun. When the suns are gone, the afternoon is over. If your stem gets a bent end, a note by your hand says touch to bite it off. If your twig has leaves, a note says touch to strip them. If your mother throws her stem away, it lies between the holes, and you can touch it to take it.

After every go, the game tells you what happened. Then Jane Goodall's notes, in red, tell you which little chimpanzees fished the way you just did: the two-year-olds, the three-year-olds, the four-year-olds, or the grown-ups. The red ruler keeps count.

Afternoons

Every afternoon is different. The mound is a different shape inside. The grass has different stems in it. Sometimes your mother has gone ahead, and you are on your own. And on the way home there is either water or ants.

The hollow tree

There is rainwater in a hollow tree, but your lips cannot reach it. Choose what to use: your hand, your stem, or some leaves from the branch. Press on the tree, and let go. Whatever comes up wet, you suck. Chew the end of a grass stem and it holds more water. Pick some leaves and chew them, and they hold a lot.

The ants' nest

Driver ants are good to eat, but they bite. Press on the nest and keep your finger down. Your stick goes in, and the ants run up it. Let go before they reach your fingers, and you eat the ants off the stick. Wait too long, and they bite you. A long stick gives you more time.

About this game

The game in one line

You are a young chimpanzee at Gombe in the termite season. Your mother fishes at her own hole beside you. You take a stem from the long grass, one at a time, push it into a termite hole and draw it out, and Jane Goodall's notes say which age of young chimpanzee fishes the way you just did. Then, on the way home, there is water in a hollow tree, or a nest of driver ants. Every afternoon is dealt differently: another mound, other grass, your mother there or gone ahead.

What the paper reports

Jane van Lawick-Goodall's chapter reviews every case of tool-use then known in birds and mammals, and her own observations of wild chimpanzees at the Gombe Stream. She defines a tool as an outside object used as an extension of the mouth, beak, hand or claw to reach an immediate goal, so that building a nest or pulling on a string does not count. Tool-use turns up in animals far apart in evolution, and on its own says nothing about intelligence: an ant-lion larva that flings sand at its prey is no cleverer than a dragonfly larva born with a jaw that does the same job. Each animal's tool grows out of a movement it already has. An Egyptian vulture smashes ostrich eggs with stones, thrown with the very flick of the head it uses to smash small eggs on the ground; banded mongooses that hurl eggs backwards between their legs, given an egg too big to lift, hurled stones at it instead.

What she finds remarkable is the chimpanzee: an animal that learns its tool-use slowly, by playing, trying and watching, and carries it to new problems. At Gombe, infants under two do not fish for termites; they watch their mothers. From two to three they use the wrong material (too short, too thick) and pull the tool out with a jerk that shakes off anything that bit on: in twenty-two bouts she saw two termites caught. From three to four their tools are longer but often too flexible, and go in only an inch or two. At four they fish almost like adults, with shorter tools. Adults push five to eight inches in, pause, and draw the tool out smoothly; they strip the leaves, bite off a bent end, pick several tools at once, often far from the mound and out of sight of it, and throw a tool away when it stops working. Infants pick up the tools adults throw away. To drink from a hollow, adults chew a handful of leaves into a sponge; one infant of three and a half, pushing a stem of dry grass into water with his termite-fishing movements and chewing the end each time he sucked it, made a sponge of the stem. The chapter ends on cultures: techniques that one gifted animal finds and others copy.

Mechanism: a toy, built to illustrate the paper's argument

This game demonstrates, under its own assumptions, how the paper's account of learning to fish can work: the mistakes Goodall lists for each age fail for the reasons she gives (a stick too thick for the hole, a floppy stem that folds at a bend and goes in an inch or two, a jerk that shakes the termites off), and the adult's way succeeds (a stiff, stripped stem, five to eight inches in, a pause, a smooth pull). It also shows the movement you learned at the mound carried, unchanged, to the water in the hollow. Played the way her two-to-three-year-olds fished, the toy catches about two termites in twenty-two dips, as she saw.

It assumes (all ours): the inside of the mound (where the passage bends, where the termites wait, how wide the hole is); how fast termites bite on, how hard they hold on, and how many fit on an inch of stem; how quickly a tip bends; the lengths of the stems in the grass, and that the grass beside the mound always offers one of each kind (she describes tools chosen as much as a hundred yards away, out of sight of the mound; the game puts the grass beside it); an afternoon of six dips for what took the infants two years; one of Goodall's ages given to each dip by what happened in it; the sips a hand, a stem, a chewed stem and a wad of leaves lift from the hollow, and how many are enough. The game draws the mound cut open, so you can see what a chimpanzee's fingers would only feel. Every number sits in the config block at the top of the source, with its source or the reason for it.

Afternoons and ants. The paper describes one site with many mounds, tools of several kinds, and infants sometimes with their mothers and sometimes not; the game deals each afternoon from those (the mound's bends and depth, the five stems in the clump, whether your mother is there), all within the paper's descriptions, and the numbers are ours. The driver ants are in the paper by a line: Goodall names sticks used at the nests of driver ants among the Gombe chimpanzees' tools. The detail of ant-dipping played here (the stick pushed in, the ants swarming up it, the sweep into the mouth before they reach the hand) is from later work at Gombe, and the ants' speed, the stick's reach and what counts as a meal are ours.

The paper establishes what Goodall observed: the ages, the mistakes, the depths, the two termites in twenty-two bouts, the preparations, the infant's grass sponge and the adults' leaf sponge, from field notes at one site in the 1960s. Her claims about how tool-use develops (play, trial and error, watching, insight) and about cultures are offered as the reasonable reading of those observations, not as experiments. The termites you catch here are no evidence for any of it.

What is cut

Half the paper is about objects used in aggression and display: branches waved and dropped, stones thrown at baboons (one hit from nine throws in the first year of the feeding station, four from thirty-two the next), sticks used as clubs, and the low-ranking male who rose to the top of his community by banging empty paraffin cans in his charging display. Also cut: the birds and other mammals (except the vulture, on the last page); the experiments in captivity, from Köhler's chimpanzees to the one that would not use a hand axe to make a tool; sniffing the end of a stem to tell whether a hole is worth working; honey, ants, and leaves used to wipe the body; the only wild case of sudden insight she saw (a male who, afraid to take a banana from a human hand, shook grass at it, then broke off a stick and knocked it to the ground); and Kortlandt's idea that savanna chimpanzees lost their weapons to early man.
