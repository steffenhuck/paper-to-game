Why it matters, and why it might be

The study cites evidence that clear releases get picked up by journalists more often, that people remember clear messages better, and that people read complicated language as a sign of distance. It found the gap on topic after topic, migration and the pandemic included. Why the Commission writes this way is open: habit and identity, compromise between many departments, or deliberate caution on touchy subjects. The study leaves that for future research.

How to play

The setting. The European Commission proposes the EU's laws and runs much of its day-to-day business. Every day it issues press releases, written for journalists but meant to reach ordinary citizens. A 2023 study measured how easy 44,978 of them (1985–2020) are to read, and compared them with UK and Irish government press releases, political-science abstracts, and political news in broadsheet and tabloid papers.

Reading ease. A standard score (Flesch, 1948) worked out from two things only: how many words a sentence has on average, and how many syllables a word has. Long sentences and long words lower it. Higher is easier: everyday English scores 60 or more; text for university graduates scores under 30. It is the study's main measure.

Guess 1. Tap the line where you think the Commission's releases sit, then lock it in. No points; it just makes you commit.

Guess 2. Say whether the releases got easier, harder or stayed the same between 1985 and 2020. The study's yearly averages are then drawn in.

The edit. A press release we wrote in the Commission's usual style (it is not a real one). Tap a paragraph: you get two rewrites that keep every fact. "Shorter sentences" cuts it into pieces but keeps the long words. "Everyday words" swaps long official words for short common ones. "Say who does what" turns nouns like "implementation" back into people doing things. You have two edits. The score above the release is the reading ease of the whole release, worked out live with the same formula the study used.

The yardstick. The UK government's average, 40. Reach it and you've done what was asked.

What it shows. Where the Commission's language sits, that it has not moved in 35 years, and that plain versions of the same message are within reach.

About the paper

Rauh, C. (2023). Clear messages to the European public? The language of European Commission press releases 1985–2020. Journal of European Integration 45(4): 683–701. doi:10.1080/07036337.2022.2134860

Fidelity: two parts, labelled

The two guesses are the study's data. Every dot and the yearly line are averages the paper reports in its Figure 2, read off the figure (to about ±1 reading-ease point; the paper gives no table). The edit is a working model of the study's measure. The score is the Flesch reading-ease formula, exactly as the study uses it: 206.835 − 1.015 × (words per sentence) − 84.6 × (syllables per word). The press release and its rewrites are ours, written in the Commission's usual style. It is not a real release.

It demonstrates where Commission press releases sit against national governments, academics and newspapers; that they did not get easier from 1985 to 2020; and how the study's main measure responds to shorter sentences, shorter words and verbs. It assumes our invented release, our rewrites, our two-edit budget and our syllable counter (checked by hand for every word in the game). The paper establishes the averages across 44,978 Commission releases, 92,070 UK and Irish government releases, 2,332 political-science abstracts and about 80,000 newspaper paragraphs, and that the gap holds within topics (56 of 60 topic-by-measure comparisons, Figure 3) and after matching on topics. It does not test rewrites of any release, and it does not claim that clearer releases would change what Europeans think.

The three measures

Reading ease (Flesch 1948): sentence length and word length. Familiar words: how often the words appear in the Google Books corpus for that decade, relative to "the". Verbs, not nouns: the share of verbs among all verbs and nouns; noun-heavy "nominal style" hides who does what. The game computes only the first live; the other two appear as the study's values, because we cannot reproduce the study's Google Books data or its part-of-speech tagging on the same scale.

The discovery

The Commission's political power and public controversy both grew over these 35 years, and it repeatedly promised better communication. Its press releases stayed about as hard to read as academic prose, harder than two national governments and far harder than newspapers — and the yearly average did not move. Only word choice became slightly more familiar.

What is cut

Figure 1 (the number of releases per month: about 100 a month by the 1990s, around 150 under Prodi and Barroso, slightly more than 50 under Juncker, as social media took over); the topic-by-topic detail of Figure 3; the topic-matching check (Appendix B); the study's discussion of the Commission's history of public communication.

Divergences

Benchmark values are read off a figure. Our release starts at 21, a little below the Commission's 26.7 average and well inside the spread of individual releases. The UK government line as the aim and the two-edit budget are ours: with two edits, about one plan in four reaches the line. The lesson the end screen draws (long words weigh heavily) holds on the study's formula; on a related formula that weighs sentence length more (Flesch–Kincaid grade), splitting sentences counts for more, though the single best edit — rewriting the quote — is the same.
