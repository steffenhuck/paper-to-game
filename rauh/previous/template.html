Why it matters, and why it might be

The study cites evidence that clear releases get picked up by journalists more often, that people remember clear messages better, and that people read complicated language as a sign of distance. It found the gap on topic after topic, migration and the pandemic included. Why the Commission writes this way is open: habit and identity, compromise between many departments, or deliberate caution on touchy subjects. The study leaves that for future research.

How to play

The setting. The European Commission proposes the EU's laws and runs much of its day-to-day business. Every day it issues press releases, written for journalists but meant to reach ordinary citizens. A 2023 study measured how easy 44,978 of them (1985–2020) are to read, and compared them with UK and Irish government press releases, political-science abstracts, and political news in broadsheet and tabloid papers.

Reading ease. A standard score (Flesch, 1948) worked out from two things only: how many words a sentence has on average, and how many syllables a word has. Long sentences and long words lower it. Higher is easier: everyday English scores 60 or more; text for university graduates scores under 30. It is the study's main measure.

Guess 1. Tap the line where you think the Commission's releases sit, then lock it in. No points; it just makes you commit.

Guess 2. Say whether the releases got easier, harder or stayed the same between 1985 and 2020. The study's yearly averages are then drawn in.

The edit. A press release we wrote in the Commission's usual style (it is not a real one). Tap a paragraph: you get two rewrites that keep every fact. "Shorter sentences" cuts it into pieces but keeps the long words. "Everyday words" swaps long official words for short common ones. "Say who does what" turns nouns like "implementation" back into people doing things. You have two edits. The score above the release is the reading ease of the whole release, worked out live with the same formula the study used.

The yardstick. The UK government's average, 40. Reach it and you've done what was asked.

What it shows. Where the Commission's language sits, that it has not moved in 35 years, and that plain versions of the same message are within reach.
