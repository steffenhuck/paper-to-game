# Say It Plainly — design memo

## 1. Paper
Rauh, C. (2023). Clear messages to the European public? The language of European Commission press releases 1985–2020. *Journal of European Integration* 45(4): 683–701. doi:10.1080/07036337.2022.2134860. Open access.
Available: the PDF only (20 pages, figures included). The replication corpus (Harvard Dataverse, doi:10.7910/DVN/UGGXUF) was not used; every number in the game is read off Figure 2 at 400 dpi and marked as such.

## 2. What the paper hands over
**Primary: estimates.** Mean values of three clarity indicators across 44,978 Commission press releases (1985–2020) and five benchmark corpora: UK and Irish government press releases, political-science abstracts, broadsheet and tabloid politics reporting (Fig. 2, upper panel); the annual Commission series (Fig. 2, lower panel); topic-matched Commission-vs-national differences (Fig. 3).
**Secondary: a measurement.** The three indicators themselves, defined exactly: Flesch reading ease (sentence length and word length), average Google Books frequency of the words used, and the share of verbs among verbs and nouns (note 5).

## 3. The discovery, and its kind
**A counterintuitive result, plus a mechanism made intelligible.** The EU's executive gained power and became publicly contested over 35 years, and repeatedly said it would communicate better. Its press releases did not get easier to read: they sit near academic abstracts, below national governments and far below newspapers, on every topic, and the line is flat from 1985 to 2020. The second half — what makes a text read like that — is the measurement: long sentences and long, noun-heavy words. The paper insists clarity is not "dumbing down" (pp. 686–7); the game lets the player check that claim by hand.

## 4. The core narrative
The European Union has an office in Brussels that writes news announcements meant for ordinary people. Over thirty-five years that office got more powerful, people started arguing about it, and it kept promising to explain itself better. A researcher measured how hard its announcements are to read, and compared them with governments, newspapers and professors. They read like the professors — and they never got easier. So: here is one of those announcements. Can you make it as easy to read as a government's, without leaving anything out?

## 5. Fidelity level and claim boundaries
Mixed, labelled by part.
- **The bets (statistical, in the data).** The dots and the line are the paper's reported averages, read off Fig. 2. *Demonstrates*: where Commission releases sit and that they did not move. *Assumes*: nothing beyond reading accuracy (about ±1 reading-ease point). *Paper establishes*: these averages over full corpora, and that they hold within topics (Fig. 3) and after topic matching (Appendix B).
- **The editor (structural for the score, illustrative for the text).** The score is the Flesch formula exactly as the paper uses it. The press release and its rewrites are ours, written in the Commission's register; it is not a real release. *Demonstrates*: that the formula rewards short sentences and short words, and that one Commission-style text can reach national-government level without losing content. *Assumes*: our text, our rewrites, our two-edit budget, our syllable counter (hand-checked). *Paper establishes*: nothing about this text; it does not test rewrites, and it does not claim that clearer releases would change opinion (it cites evidence that clarity helps recall and media pickup, pp. 686).
- No causal lever is played: the game never says that politicisation caused or failed to cause anything.

## 6. The aim and the verdict
**Aim (start screen, play screen, How to play):** "Make a Commission press release as easy to read as a UK government one — with just two edits."
**Verdict lines:** (you) "Your release scores 45 — easier than the average UK government release." / "…still harder than a UK government release." (paper) "The Commission's real releases average 27: closer to academic abstracts than to any government, on every topic, and flat from 1985 to 2020." (means) "Every version you could pick said the same thing. Plain was possible; the paper asks why the Commission doesn't write it."

## 7. Concepts
1. **The Midday Briefing (picked).** Position: agent (the press officer). Format: challenge, opened by two forecaster bets. Controls: which paragraph to rewrite, and how (shorter sentences / everyday words / say who does what), under a two-edit budget. Thinking before pressing: "which of these paragraphs is worst, and which rewrite fixes most?" Seen after: the score marker slides along a ruler carrying the paper's benchmarks. Discovery: the bets deliver the paper's result (the Commission sits with the academics; flat for 35 years); the editor delivers the mechanism (word length and nominal style weigh as much as sentence length; the quote is the worst offender; it can be fixed without losing content). Fun: anticipation (bets), agency and mastery (edits; a second try with what you learned). Replay: a different pair of edits; the best plan is not the obvious "split the longest sentence".
2. **Who wrote this? (runner-up).** Position: experimenter. Format: discovery. The player reads short snippets and sorts them: Commission, government, newspaper, academic, and guesses the year. Discovery: you cannot date a Commission release; you mistake it for an academic abstract. Problem: needs real snippets from the corpus (not available offline; invented snippets would make the "can't tell" result ours, not the paper's).
3. **The Spokesperson's Dial.** Position: controller. Format: strategic. Rising politicisation each year; the player sets a clarity level against an invented cost (member-state anger). Rejected: the cost is not in the paper — its three explanations (identity, internal bargaining, strategic caution) are offered as questions for future research, so any payoff would be our invention presented as a mechanism.

## 8. The first five minutes (pick)
0:00 Start screen: a press release headline, "Say It Plainly", the aim in a box. I press Start.
0:15 A ruler 0–80, "reading ease: higher is easier". Five labelled dots: academics 14, Irish government 33, UK government 40, broadsheets 48, tabloids 65. "Where do the EU Commission's releases sit? Tap the ruler." I think: they're government press releases, so near the UK… or maybe worse, it's Brussels. I tap 35.
0:40 The blue dot drops at 27. "You put it at 35 — too clear. The Commission sits between the academics and the governments." Two small rulers below: same picture for familiar words (Commission lowest of all) and verbs. I grin: worse than professors on vocabulary.
1:10 "Maastricht, the Santer resignation, the euro crisis, Brexit: Europe got contested. Did the Commission's releases get easier to read?" I guess Easier — surely they learnt. The line draws itself year by year past each event: flat. 27.6 → 25.6. I frown; that's the paper.
1:40 "Your turn." A five-paragraph release, gauge at 21, two edit tokens. I read: the quote is awful ("a significant contribution to the empowerment of consumers…"). I tap it; two rewrites appear. "Shorter sentences" barely changes words; "Say who does what" is plain. I pick it: the marker jumps 21 → 35. One left.
2:30 Which next? The first paragraph is the longest sentence; I split it: 35 → 40.5? No — 39.5. Missed by half a point. "Still harder than a UK release." I see the list: what each edit did, and "best possible with two edits: 47". 
3:00 Play again: quote with verbs plus the last paragraph in everyday words: 47. Easier than broadsheet? Not quite, but past the UK. I understand: long words cost as much as long sentences.
3:30 End screen: verdict; the paper's line; why it might be (three reasons, as open questions).

## 9. The pick, and why
The Midday Briefing: the bets put the paper's finding in the first ninety seconds, and the editor gives a real decision with a budget — and the thing learned (what the score punishes) is the paper's own measurement. Runner-up: Who wrote this?, switch if the author supplies real snippets from the replication corpus — it would then carry the "unchanged over time" finding as an experience rather than a chart.

## 10. The shape
Screens: start → bet 1 (place the dot) → bet 2 (the trend) → editor → end; How to play and About reachable throughout. One round each; the editor is replayable ("Try the edit again" skips the bets). Score: none for bets (graded in words); editor yardstick is the reading-ease score itself, in the aim's units, against the UK line. Presets: none; the benchmarks are the paper's corpora. Order: the paper's result first (it's the surprise), mechanism second (it explains the surprise).

## 11. Controls, mechanism, feedback, moment
Controls: tap a ruler (bet 1); three buttons (bet 2); tap a paragraph, tap one of its two rewrites (editor). Mechanism: `mechanism.js` — Flesch over the whole draft. Feedback: the drop (bet 1), the trajectory (bet 2), the counter-on-a-ruler with a delta per edit (editor). Moment: the line drawn flat past Brexit; then the quote rewrite jumping the marker by 14 points.

## 12. What is cut
Fig. 1 (number of releases per month) except as a line in About; Fig. 3 (topic-by-topic) except as one sentence ("on 56 of 60 topic comparisons"); the topic-matching robustness check; the familiar-words and verb measures in the editor (we cannot compute the Google Books measure, and the paper's verb share depends on spaCy tagging conventions we cannot reproduce on its scale — both appear only in the bet reveal, as the paper's values).

## 13. Divergences
- Values read off Fig. 2, not taken from tables (the paper gives none): ±1 reading-ease point.
- The text is invented, in the Commission's register; it starts at 21, a little below the Commission's 26.7 average, well within the spread of individual releases shown in Fig. 2.
- Syllables by a heuristic with a hand-checked exception list (every word in the game checked).
- Two-edit budget and UK target: ours, to create a decision. The editor's lesson (both halves of the formula matter) is the formula's, not the budget's: see fidelity check.
- Trend "about the same" threshold (5 points first-to-last): ours; the paper says "hardly changed" and "stagnate".

## 14. Visual world
From the paper: Fig. 2's dot-and-ruler plot — the Commission blue dot among yellow (governments) and green (academics and newspapers). Rules: one horizontal ruler is the only chart type; Commission blue, governments yellow, others green, as in the paper; the release is set like a press release (headline, dateline, serif text); the player's marker is a big ink triangle. Legibility: on the editor the player looks at one ruler (full width, 5 labelled ticks) and five paragraph cards; nothing else moves.

## 15. Fidelity plan
1. Flesch formula reproduces the known values: "The Australian platypus is seemingly a hybrid of a mammal and reptilian creature." = 37.5; "The cat sat on the mat." = 116.1.
2. syllables() matches a hand count for every word the release can contain.
3. Benchmarks reproduce the paper's verbal claims: Commission below both governments on all three indicators (p. 692); closer to academics than to broadsheets on reading ease and verbs; below academics on familiar words (p. 692); UK government reading ease ≈ "high school", Commission ≈ "university graduates" (Flesch bands: 30–50 college, 0–30 graduate) (p. 692); annual series flat (p. 693).
4. The original text scores within the Commission corpus spread and below the Irish government average; no rewrite drops a paragraph's content (manual).
5. Game balance: with two edits at least one plan reaches 40 and none reaches the broadsheet line by accident; random two-edit plans succeed less than half the time.
6. Robustness of the editor's lesson: (a) under the plain heuristic counter without exceptions and (b) under the Flesch–Kincaid grade formula, the best two-edit plan is the same or nearly as good, and the ranking of plans correlates strongly. (c) Lesson check: rewrites that only shorten sentences gain less on average than rewrites that shorten words — under both counters.

Robustness: under a plain syllable heuristic the ranking of all 40 two-edit plans is nearly identical (Spearman 0.98). Under the Flesch–Kincaid grade formula, which weighs sentence length more, the ranking shifts (Spearman 0.58). There, splitting sentences is worth about as much as the other rewrites, but the single best edit (the quote, with verbs) is the same, and that formula's best plan also reaches the UK line in the game. So the end screen's lesson is worded "on this score", and About states the caveat.
