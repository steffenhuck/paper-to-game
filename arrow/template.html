VotingwithArrow

After Kenneth J. Arrow, A Difficulty in the Concept of Social Welfare, 1950

Voting is easy. Counting is the hard part.

A small Swiss village. Two voters, Anna and Bruno. Three plans for the empty square: a fountain, a tower, a tree. Next Sunday they vote. Each writes the three plans on a ballot, favourite on top.

You count. If the two agree, counting is easy. If they disagree, it is one vote each, a tie, and you break it.

But fair means you say now, before the vote, how you will break every tie that can come up. You write it in a book. Then nobody can say the count was fixed afterwards.

AimWrite a book that never contradicts itself, without making one voter the boss.

Arrow proved in 1950 that nobody can do both. Try anyway, and see what gets in the way.

Aim
Never contradict yourself, and nobody the boss.

Your book

Anna can write her ballot six ways, and so can Bruno. So 36 different votes can come in next Sunday, and you will write a book that can be consulted for every one.

Where Anna and Bruno fill in the ballot the same way, it is easy: that is the answer. Where they differ, your book must choose.

You might be tempted to count points: 3 for a first place, 2 for a second, 1 for a third, add them up, highest wins. But that assumes something you do not know: that the step from first to second matters as much as the step from second to third, and as much to Anna as to Bruno. A ballot only says what is above what. So no points.

What is left is this. You will see three what-ifs, three of the 36 votes, and make two decisions for each: what goes on the square, and what if that falls through, for whatever reason. For each decision you pick one plan, or call it a draw and the village tosses a coin. Sometimes the second decision is already made by the first, and the game says so. And these six decisions fill all six lines of your book.

Sounds like magic? Almost. Here is the thing. Take the plans two at a time. Fountain or tower? If both put the same one higher, that settles it. If not, it is a tie, and your book says who gets their way, or calls it a draw. The same for the other two pairs. Three pairs, two ways to disagree about each: six ties, six lines.

The three what-ifs are chosen so that between them you break every one of the six. Then your book can be consulted on any vote: fountain or tower, tower or tree, tree or fountain, three results.

Two rules for your book.

1. Never contradict yourself. If the fountain is above the tower, and the tower above the tree, then the fountain is above the tree. Sounds obvious, but it is tricky: your book answers the three pairs separately, and three separate answers can clash. The village will still decide (the plan nothing is above goes on the square, or a coin), but Arrow goes through all 36 votes and counts every clash.

2. Nobody is the boss. A voter who wins every tie is the boss, and then the other one might as well stay at home.

Your score is how many of the 36 votes your book counts fairly: a vote with a clash does not count, and with a boss none of them count. The best any book can do is 34, and that scores 100. Arrow proved in 1950 that no book keeps both rules, so 100 already has two clashes in it. See how close you get.

          Why no book can do it. Read it like a comic strip.

          3Why no points

          You were told at the start: no counting points. Here is what goes wrong if you do.

          So a counting rule has three doors.

          Let a third plan decide between two, as points do. Let one voter decide: a boss. Or accept that some votes get no answer. Arrow proved there is no fourth door. Every way of counting anyone has ever proposed goes through one of the three; the argument is only about which. Arrow himself accepted the first rule (no third plan) as a matter of fairness, and said so.

          4The brute-force check

          Is a boss just bad luck?

          No. The answer may be exactly Anna’s ballot on one vote, and it has to be when the two agree on everything. A boss is the voter whose ballot is the answer on every one of the 36 votes, whatever the other one writes. Arrow’s word is dictator.

          Why not toss a coin for each tie?

          Then the same two ballots could get one answer today and another tomorrow. And a coin can land in a circle too: Fountain above Tower, Tower above Tree, Tree above Fountain, and nobody is first.

          Would more voters fix it?

          Why must the same tie get the same answer?

          So that fountain against tower is decided by what people think of the fountain and the tower, and not of the tree. Arrow’s example: an election counted in points. One plan wins. Then a losing plan drops out, nobody changes their mind, and the count is suddenly a tie.

          So is voting broken?

          No. Look how many squares had an answer. Arrow showed that no way of counting is safe on every possible vote: each one gives something up. Which one a country uses is a choice, and now that you can vote, it is partly yours.

          How to play

          Aim: write a book that never contradicts itself, without making one voter the boss. Arrow proved that nobody can do both; the score says how close you got.

          Your book

          36 votes can come in, but a tie is always about two plans: Anna puts one higher, Bruno the other. Three pairs of plans, two ways round: six ties, six lines in the book. No counting points: a ballot says only what is above what, not by how much. And a ranking must hang together: if the fountain is above the tower and the tower above the tree, the fountain is above the tree. Three separate answers can break that.

          The what-ifs

          The vote is next Sunday. Before it, the counting rule has to be written down. Arrow shows you a pair of ballots, Anna’s in red and Bruno’s in black, favourite on top, and asks: if they write this, what goes on the square?

          Settled and tied

          Take the plans two at a time. If both put the same one higher, that is settled, 2–0. If each puts a different one higher, it is 1–1: a tie, and your answer breaks it, for Anna, for Bruno, or as a draw (the village likes both the same and will toss a coin).

          Answering

          Say what goes on the square. Then, if more than one answer is still possible, what comes next if that falls through; plans do fall through, and the village should not have to vote again. When the second decision is already made by the first, the game says so instead of asking. Then press Write it in the book.

          Your book

          Six different ties can happen. Once you break one, the book keeps your answer, and that tie gets the same answer whenever it comes up again. Three what-ifs fill the book.

          Arrow checks the rest

          Arrow tries all 36 ways the vote can go. For each, the book gives three results, one per pair of plans. A square with a ranking means the three fit together. A red hatched square means they contradict each other (fountain above tower, tower above tree, but tower above fountain); the village still decides, by the plan nothing is above or by a coin, but the vote does not count as fair, and the box under the sheet says why. A red or black corner means the ranking is exactly that voter’s ballot. Touch any square to read it.

          Sunday

          The real vote, drawn by chance from the 36. Your book counts it, line by line, and something goes on the square.

          The debrief

          Your score: votes counted fairly, out of 36, as a share of the best possible (34 is 100; a boss is 0). Then why the best is 34, Arrow’s proof as a strip of pictures, why points go wrong, and every possible book checked.
