VotingwithArrow

After Kenneth J. Arrow, A Difficulty in the Concept of Social Welfare, 1950

A Swiss village with two voters, Anna and Beat. Three plans for the village square: a fountain, a tower, a tree.

Every voting Sunday each of them hands in a ballot, best plan on top. You are the counting rule. You turn the two ballots into one list for the village.

You read two ballots. You touch a list. You post it. What you rule when the two disagree, you must rule again next time.

AimGive the village a list on every Sunday that could ever come, without making one voter the boss.

Arrow proved in 1950 that it cannot be done. Five Sundays, then he checks your rule. See how close you get.

      Is a boss just bad luck?

      No. A list may be the same as Anna’s ballot on one Sunday, and it must be when the two agree. A boss is the voter whose ballot it is on every one of the 36 Sundays, whatever the other one hands in. Arrow’s word is dictator.

      Why must a ruling be kept?

      So that a quarrel about fountain and tower is settled by what people think of fountain and tower, not of the tree. Arrow’s example is an election by points. One plan wins. Then a losing plan is struck off the ballots, nobody changes their mind, and the count is suddenly a tie.

      How to play

      Aim: give the village a list on every Sunday that could ever come, without making one voter the boss.

      The ballots

      Anna’s ballot is red, Beat’s is black. Each ranks the three plans: a fountain (circle), a tower (square), a tree (triangle). Best on top.

      The three pairs

      Three plans make three pairs. For each pair the screen shows which plan each voter puts higher. Where both put the same plan higher, the village’s list must do the same. Where they disagree, you rule: Anna’s way, Beat’s way, or level (the village likes both the same).

      The lists you can post

      The village’s list is drawn in outline: the plan on the top step is first; plans on the same step are level. Only lists that fit the fixed pairs are shown. Touch one, and the open pairs show what it would rule. Then press Post the list.

      Your rule book

      There are six ways the two can disagree about a pair. Each has a box. Once you rule, the box keeps it, and the same disagreement gets the same answer on every later Sunday. So your choices shrink. When the fixed pairs leave only one list, you post that one, and it writes a ruling you did not choose.

      Arrow’s check

      After five Sundays the rule book is full. Arrow tries all 36 pairs of ballots the two could ever hand in. A square with a list is fine. A square struck through has no list: the pair results contradict each other, and the reading under the sheet says how. A red or black corner means the list is the same as that voter’s ballot. Touch any square to read it.

      The verdict

      The score is the number of the 36 Sundays that got a list. A boss is a voter whose ballot is the list on all 36.
