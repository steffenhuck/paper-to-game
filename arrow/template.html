VotingwithArrow

After Kenneth J. Arrow, A Difficulty in the Concept of Social Welfare, 1950

Voting is easy. Counting is the hard part.

A small Swiss village. Two voters, Anna and Beat. Three plans for the empty square: a fountain, a tower, a tree. Next Sunday they vote. Each writes the three plans on a ballot, favourite on top.

You count. Where both prefer the same plan, that is settled. Where they disagree it is one vote each, a tie, and you break it.

But the rule must be written down before the vote, so nobody can say the count was fixed afterwards. So Arrow brings you “what ifs”: what if Anna writes this and Beat writes that? You answer, and your answers go into a book. Three what-ifs fill it.

AimWrite a book that gives an answer whatever they vote, without making one voter the boss.

A boss is a voter who wins every tie. Arrow proved in 1950 that this aim cannot be reached. Try anyway, and see what gets in the way.

          Is a boss just bad luck?

          No. The answer may be exactly Anna’s ballot on one vote, and it has to be when the two agree on everything. A boss is the voter whose ballot is the answer on every one of the 36 votes, whatever the other one writes. Arrow’s word is dictator.

          Why not toss a coin for each tie?

          Then the same two ballots could get one answer today and another tomorrow. And a coin can land in a circle too: Fountain above Tower, Tower above Tree, Tree above Fountain, and nobody is first.

          Would more voters fix it?

          Why must the same tie get the same answer?

          So that fountain against tower is decided by what people think of the fountain and the tower, and not of the tree. Arrow’s example: an election counted in points. One plan wins. Then a losing plan drops out, nobody changes their mind, and the count is suddenly a tie.

          So is voting broken?

          No. Look how many squares had an answer. Arrow showed that no way of counting is safe on every possible vote: each one gives something up. Which one a country uses is a choice, and now that you can vote, it is partly yours.

          How to play

          Aim: write a book that gives an answer whatever they vote, without making one voter the boss.

          The what-ifs

          The vote is next Sunday. Before it, the counting rule has to be written down. Arrow shows you a pair of ballots, Anna’s in red and Beat’s in black, favourite on top, and asks: if they write this, what goes on the square?

          Settled and tied

          Take the plans two at a time. If both put the same one higher, that is settled, 2–0. If each puts a different one higher, it is 1–1: a tie, and your answer breaks it, for Anna, for Beat, or as a draw (the village likes both the same and will toss a coin).

          Answering

          Say what goes on the square. Then, if more than one answer is still possible, what comes next if that falls through; plans do fall through, and the village should not have to vote again. A question with only one possible answer is not asked. Then press Write it in the book.

          Your book

          Six different ties can happen. Once you break one, the book keeps your answer, and that tie gets the same answer whenever it comes up again. Three what-ifs fill the book.

          Arrow checks the rest

          Arrow tries all 36 ways the vote can go. A square with a ranking means the book has an answer. A square struck through means it has none: the book’s own answers contradict each other, and the box under the sheet says how. A red or black corner means the answer is exactly that voter’s ballot. Touch any square to read it.

          Sunday

          The real vote, drawn by chance from the 36. Your book counts it.

          The debrief

          Three lines on what happened, three other tie-breakers tried, Arrow’s proof as a strip of pictures, and every possible book checked.
