VotingwithArrow

After Kenneth J. Arrow, A Difficulty in the Concept of Social Welfare, 1950

Voting is the easy part. Counting is where it gets hard.

A small Swiss village has two voters, Anna and Beat, and three plans for the village square: a fountain, a tower or a tree. Every voting Sunday each of them hands in a ballot, favourite on top.

You count. Out of the two ballots you make one ranking for the village. Where the two agree, that is easy. Where they disagree it is one vote each, a tie, and you break it.

One rule keeps you fair: the same tie gets the same answer, every Sunday.

AimGive the village a ranking on every Sunday that could ever come, without making one voter the boss.

A boss is a voter who wins every tie. In 1950 Kenneth Arrow proved that this aim cannot be reached, by anyone, ever. Five Sundays, then he checks your work. See how close you get.

      Is a boss just bad luck?

      No. The ranking may be exactly Anna’s ballot on one Sunday, and it has to be when the two agree on everything. A boss is the voter whose ballot it is on every one of the 36 Sundays, whatever the other one writes. Arrow’s word is dictator.

      Why not toss a coin for each tie?

      Then the same two ballots could give one ranking this Sunday and another the next. And a coin can land in a circle too. On Sunday 1 both put the Fountain above the Tower; let the coin put the Tower above the Tree and the Tree above the Fountain, and there is no ranking.

      Would more voters fix it?

      Why must the same tie get the same answer?

      So that the choice between fountain and tower depends on what people think of fountain and tower, and not of the tree. Arrow’s example is an election counted in points. One plan wins. Then a losing plan drops out, nobody changes their mind, and suddenly the count is a tie.

      So is voting broken?

      No. Most Sundays, most ways of counting work fine: look how many squares had a ranking. What Arrow showed is that no way of counting is safe on every possible set of ballots. Each one gives something up. Which one a country uses is a choice, and now that you have the vote it is partly yours.

      How to play

      Aim: give the village a ranking on every Sunday that could ever come, without making one voter the boss.

      The ballots

      Anna’s ballot is red, Beat’s is black. Each ranks the three plans: a fountain (circle), a tower (square), a tree (triangle). Favourite on top.

      Choose what goes on the square

      The village’s ranking is drawn in outline. The top step is first place; two plans on one step are tied. Only rankings that fit what is already settled are shown. Touch one.

      What that decides, plan against plan

      Three plans can be compared two at a time in three ways. If both voters prefer the same plan of the two, it is 2–0 and settled. If each prefers a different one, it is 1–1: a tie. The ranking you touched breaks it: for Anna, for Beat, or as a draw (the village likes both plans the same). The table shows which. Then press Post the ranking.

      Your tie-break book

      Six different ties can happen. Once you break one, your book keeps the answer, and that tie gets the same answer on every later Sunday. So your choices shrink. When only one ranking fits, you post that one, and it breaks a tie in a way you did not choose.

      Arrow’s check

      After five Sundays the book is full. Arrow tries all 36 pairs of ballots the two could ever hand in. A square with a ranking is fine. A square struck through has none: the three results contradict each other, and the box under the sheet says how. A red or black corner means the ranking is exactly that voter’s ballot. Touch any square to read it.

      Hire a tie-breaker

      Then somebody else breaks the ties: Clara, a third voter who always votes the same way, or Anna, or Beat. Pick one and the 36 Sundays are checked again.

      The verdict

      Your score is how many of the 36 Sundays got a ranking from your own book. A boss is a voter whose ballot is the ranking on all 36.
