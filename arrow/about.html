About

Voting with Arrow is built from Kenneth J. Arrow, “A Difficulty in the Concept of Social Welfare”, Journal of Political Economy 58(4), August 1950, pages 328–346, doi:10.1086/256963. It can be read at JSTOR. Game built from this paper; the paper is the authority where the two disagree.

What the paper says

Arrow asks whether there is any procedure that turns the rankings of individuals into one ranking for society and meets a few conditions nobody would want to drop. The ranking must be a proper one, with no circles. If everybody raises a plan, society must not lower it. The choice between two plans must depend only on how people rank those two. No result may be fixed in advance against everybody’s wishes. And there must be no dictator: no single person whose preference wins whatever everyone else wants. His theorem: with three or more plans, no procedure meets all of these.

What the game is

The game runs Arrow’s own small case, the one his proof uses: two individuals and three alternatives. You are the procedure. The rule that the same tie gets the same answer is his third condition; the rule that agreement wins follows from his second and fourth. That leaves six ties with three answers each, 729 tie-break books in all, and the game checks every one of them on all 36 pairs of ballots. Exactly two never fail, and they are the two dictators.

The three what-ifs are the game’s: each opens two new ties, and together they fill the book. Arrow’s own proof is the strip of pictures on the debrief: one tie given to a voter, five what-ifs each with only one answer left, and that voter is the boss; so every tie must be a draw, and then one vote has no answer (his equations 1 to 8, pages 341 to 342).

Sunday, the real vote, is ours: one of the 36 pairs of ballots drawn by chance and counted by your book. So is what the village does when the book’s three results contradict each other: the plan that no other plan is above goes on the square, and where that leaves none or several, a coin. Arrow says only that such a result is not an ordering. And the score is ours: the votes on which the book does not contradict itself, none if one voter is the boss, as a share of 34, the best any book without a boss achieves. The two votes that even the best book loses are the paradox of voting that the paper opens with.

Mechanism kind: ported

  • The game demonstrates that, in this small case, every tie-break book either leaves some Sunday without a ranking or makes one voter win every tie.
  • The game assumes ballots without ties (Arrow also lets voters be indifferent); which ballots Arrow asks about; what the village does with a contradiction; the real Sunday; and the score. Counting Sundays out of 36, and so the number 34 and what is said about habit, is ours and not Arrow’s.
  • The paper establishes the theorem for any number of voters and three or more alternatives. It does not say that real elections often fail, or that any real voting rule is a dictatorship, and neither does the game.

The points method

The opening screen tells the player not to count points, and the debrief shows why: a plan nobody moved between can change the result between two others. That is Arrow’s own example against the rank-order method (pp. 337–338), and his third condition is the rule that forbids it. The calibration checks that the points ranking is never blocked during the three what-ifs (each opens only new ties) but disagrees with the finished book on about two thirds of the 36 votes.

What is left out

Voters who are indifferent between plans; more voters and more plans; and the second half of the paper, on what the result means for welfare economics, where Arrow shows that rules for dividing goods run into the same wall.

Names

Anna and Bruno are Arrow’s individuals 1 and 2. The fountain, the tower and the tree are his x, y and z. Sunday 1 is the pair of rankings from the last step of his proof.
