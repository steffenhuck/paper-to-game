About

Voting with Arrow is built from Kenneth J. Arrow, “A Difficulty in the Concept of Social Welfare”, Journal of Political Economy 58(4), August 1950, pages 328–346, doi:10.1086/256963. It can be read at JSTOR. Game built from this paper; the paper is the authority where the two disagree.

What the paper says

Arrow asks whether there is any procedure that turns the rankings of individuals into one ranking for society and meets a few conditions nobody would want to drop. The ranking must be a proper one, with no circles. If everybody raises a plan, society must not lower it. The choice between two plans must depend only on how people rank those two. No result may be fixed in advance against everybody’s wishes. And there must be no dictator: no single person whose preference wins whatever everyone else wants. His theorem: with three or more plans, no procedure meets all of these.

What the game is

The game runs Arrow’s own small case, the one his proof uses: two individuals and three alternatives. You are the procedure. The rule that a ruling is kept is his third condition; the rule that agreement wins follows from his second and fourth. That leaves six rulings with three answers each, 729 rule books in all, and the game checks every one of them on all 36 pairs of ballots. Exactly two never fail, and they are the two dictators.

On Sundays 4 and 5 you are living a step of Arrow’s proof: he shows that one ruling in a voter’s favour, together with agreement, forces the next.

Mechanism kind: ported

  • The game demonstrates that, in this small case, every rule book that settles each pair on its own and respects agreement either leaves some Sunday without a list or makes one voter win every disagreement.
  • The game assumes ballots without ties (Arrow also lets voters be indifferent); which ballots come on which Sunday; and the score. Counting Sundays out of 36, and so the number 34 and what is said about custom, is ours and not Arrow’s.
  • The paper establishes the theorem for any number of voters and three or more alternatives. It does not say that real elections often fail, or that any real voting rule is a dictatorship, and neither does the game.

What is left out

Voters who are indifferent between plans; more voters and more plans; and the second half of the paper, on what the result means for welfare economics, where Arrow shows that rules for dividing goods run into the same wall.

Names

Anna and Beat are Arrow’s individuals 1 and 2. The fountain, the tower and the tree are his x, y and z. Sunday 1 is the pair of rankings from the last step of his proof.
