About

The paper. Elinor Ostrom (2000), “Collective Action and the Evolution of Social Norms”, Journal of Economic Perspectives 14(3): 137–158. doi:10.1257/jep.14.3.137, open access at aeaweb.org. Game built from this paper; the paper is the authority where the two disagree.

What the paper argues. The old theory said self-interested people would not provide a public good. In the lab they do, about half, and it decays; talking face to face raises it and keeps it up; people pay to punish free riders. Ostrom reads this with three kinds of player: rational egoists, conditional cooperators who share while they expect others to and give up when disappointed, and willing punishers. Norms are a value people add to the plain payoffs, and they evolve: toward whoever earns more, up where sharing is reciprocated, and down where rules enforced from outside make a norm of one’s own unnecessary. A little outside policing is the worst of all worlds; a lot works while it lasts and leaves less behind than never having had it. The commons that lasted for generations were run by their own users, with monitors they chose and sanctions that start as a word.

What this game is. A toy built to illustrate that argument. Ten villagers of the three kinds (three, five and two, our reading of the paper’s 40 percent, 27 percent and the rest), a norm strength for each, a pond that breeds, and five arrangements that are the paper’s regimes: the lab baseline, face-to-face communication, local monitoring with graduated sanctions, weak external enforcement, strong external enforcement. The eleventh summer is the second phase of the Frohlich and Oppenheimer experiment the paper cites, in which the outside mechanism was removed. After five summers one villager moves away and a newcomer who has never fished here moves in: the paper’s in-migration threat.

What the meeting knows. The player is the village meeting and is told only what it could know. Everyone carries the catch home in a bucket, so the meeting always learns how many fish left the pond, and from that how many of the ten took two, but a name only when somebody at the pond saw: the village’s own watcher sees most villagers, one fish or two, and reports them all; the town’s policeman reports only whom he caught. With nobody at the pond, the row shows ten buckets. This is the paper’s own point about monitoring: it is information before it is punishment, and the lab’s communication treatments reveal totals, not names. The eleventh summer is then revealed in full, which the village never sees, together with who never cared about the rule.

What each card costs. Each villager needs one fish a summer, and anyone who ends a summer with none goes hungry: an empty plate on the shelf. Taking turns watching costs one of us the summer, the watcher. The occasional policeman costs one of us the summer, who fishes for his pay; the daily policeman costs three. The sizes are this game’s; the direction is the paper’s: users who monitor one another do it more cheaply and with better knowledge than an outside official, and government-run systems lose a great deal by comparison. The village’s own watcher sees eight villagers in ten; the daily policeman catches nine grabbers in ten, the occasional one fewer than two. The end screen sets the player’s village beside how villages usually end up under each card, from 400 simulated villages each.

What the game demonstrates. That a village of the paper’s three kinds, with norms that move as the paper describes, produces the paper’s pattern: about half share with nothing in place and it slides; a talk raises it and holds it; watching with graduated sanctions brings grabbers into line and leaves a village that shares unwatched; a policeman now and then is no better than nothing and wears the norm down; a policeman every day enforces sharing, costs, and leaves less norm than never having had him.

What the game assumes. Everything in its rules: how a villager weighs the second fish against reciprocity, shame, assurance and the chance of being caught; the sanctions in kind (a word, then both fish back, then both fish back and a summer out; both fish to the policeman) and the fees; the rates at which norms grow, are imitated, and are crowded out; the pond’s rule (each fish left can have one baby, food for twelve babies a winter, room for fifty fish); the newcomer. None of these numbers is in the paper. The daily policeman’s bad eleventh summer depends on crowding out being fast enough: under this game’s rate, and at three-quarters of it, the policed village ends below the one left alone; at half the rate it does not. Under a policeman, imitation of the villagers who ate best builds no norm, because what they show is compliance; it still erodes one when grabbers eat best.

What the paper establishes. Seven lab regularities, reported from the literature; field findings that user-made, user-enforced rules with graduated sanctions characterise long-surviving regimes; the crowding-out experiments; and an argument, not a theorem, that an evolutionary account with several types explains them. The paper says a mild degree of external monitoring “may be” the worst of all worlds; it does not measure it.

What is left out. The announced last round; group size and heterogeneity; boundary rules, cost-sharing, conflict arenas, the right to organise, nested layers; rewards; the trust game and the information regimes of Güth and Kliemt in their own right; the leader.

Numbers. Every number the game uses sits in a commented block at the top of its source, tagged with the page it came from or marked as ours. A village number replays exactly.
