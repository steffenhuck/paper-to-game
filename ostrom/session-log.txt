This is how the fishing and farming villages that lasted for hundreds of years did it: they watched the pond themselves, and the first punishment was just a word. The telling-off tells everyone that someone is watching; the trust carries on; and in the end hardly any watching is needed. In this game, the grabbers learnt the rule by being caught by their own village, and one of us going hungry each summer, by turns, was the whole price. / Elinor Ostrom (2000), Collective Action and the Evolution of Social Norms, Journal of Economic Perspectives 14(3): 137–158. While the policeman was there every day, everyone shared, and nobody needed a reason of their own. In an experiment Ostrom writes about, the group with an outside enforcer did well while it lasted and, when it was taken away, shared less than a group that never had one. In this game, every policed summer wore away the village’s own rule; that is what the eleventh summer shows, and it depends on how fast people forget a rule they don’t need. He was also dear: in this game three of us went hungry every summer to pay him, where a watcher of our own costs one. / Elinor Ostrom (2000), Collective Action and the Evolution of Social Norms, Journal of Economic Perspectives 14(3): 137–158.
