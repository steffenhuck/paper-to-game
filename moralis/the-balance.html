how to play

How to play

Your aim: find the balance no newcomer can beat.

The setting

People in a village are paired up to deal with each other — sharing a chore, splitting a resource, doing business. Each villager carries a temperament, a balance between two goals: their own gain, and the right thing, meaning what would be best for them if the other person did exactly the same. A balance of 0 cares only about own gain (economists' homo oeconomicus); 1 cares only about the right thing (homo kantiensis, after Kant); anything between is homo moralis. Nobody can see anyone else's balance.

What each round asks

You set the balance for the whole village. Then a few newcomers arrive with a different balance — eleven kinds in turn, from 0 to 1 in steps of 0.1. Everyone acts on their own balance, and earns what the deal pays. A newcomer kind that earns more on average than your villagers spreads and takes over; one that earns less dies out. One that behaves exactly like your villagers blends in.

Each society gives you three tests. After each, look at the drawer: it shows which newcomers took over and where they sit. Move your balance and test again.

The societies

Five of them, which differ in who meets whom: strangers in a city, siblings, half-siblings, and classmates in business. In some, newcomers tend to be paired with their own kind (a newcomer's sibling is likely to share its upbringing); in others, never. Three different deals are played: a shared project (effort), a quarrel over a resource (grab or share), and a dilemma (cooperate or cheat).

The score

One point for every newcomer that fails to take over: up to 11 per test, 33 per society, 165 in all. If you find a balance nobody beats before your third test, the remaining tests are scored at that result.

What the game is trying to show

Which temperaments survive when people can't read each other's minds. Play it before reading the answer; the last screen and the About page explain it.

about this game

The Balance

What the paper hands over

A model. People are matched in pairs to play a symmetric game. What each person wants is private. Matching may be assortative: one number, the index of assortativity σ, is the chance that a rare newcomer meets one of its own kind (Section 2.1). Homo moralis wants a mix: (1 − κ) of her own payoff plus κ of the payoff she would get if the other person did what she does — Kant's "act as if your maxim were a universal law" (eq. 7). κ = 0 is homo oeconomicus; κ = 1 is homo kantiensis. Theorem 1: the type with κ = σ, homo hamiltonensis, cannot be invaded, and any type that behaves differently can be.

The two reversals

One. Believed: when preferences are private, evolution selects pure self-interest; or, if anything, it selects goodness toward kin, and more is better. Paper: it selects a precise blend, κ = σ. A village more moral than that falls just as surely as a selfish one. Two. Believed: how moral people should be depends on what is at stake. Paper: the surviving morality depends only on who meets whom; the game changes behaviour, not the balance.

Where the player sits

Evolution's hand. You set the resident population's κ and release eleven newcomer types, κ = 0, 0.1, …, 1. For each, the game computes the residents' equilibrium behaviour, the newcomers' equilibrium behaviour given they meet their own kind with probability σ, and both groups' average payoff (eqs. 3, 11, 12, with the newcomers vanishingly rare). A newcomer that earns strictly more takes over; one that earns less dies out; one that behaves exactly like the residents blends in. The runner-up concept — the player as a preference carrying a different morality into family, market and classroom (Section 5.3) — is kept for a second pass.

The societies

1. Strangers, σ = 0, public good (Section 2.1, Example 1). 2. Siblings, σ = ½ (Section 5.1), same game. 3. Siblings, hawk–dove (Section 4). 4. Half-siblings, σ = ¼ (footnote 24), hawk–dove. 5. Classmates in business partnerships, σ = (μ − 1)/(n − 1) · p* ≈ 0.20 with n = 100, μ = 40, p* = ½ (Section 5.2), prisoners' dilemma with (T, R, P, S) = (7, 5, 3, 2) (Fig. 1).

What the check found

The engine's resident behaviour matches the paper's closed forms (Fig. 1's x̂(κ), hawk–dove's (1 − κ)/(1 + κ) · v/c, Example 1's first-order condition) to 10⁻¹². In all five societies, a resident with κ = σ is beaten by none of 201 newcomer types. Every lever position that behaves differently from homo hamiltonensis is invaded by at least one of the eleven; the invaders always sit on σ's side. In the classmates' dilemma, every κ ≤ ¼ defects and survives, as the paper notes after Fig. 1.

What is cut

Asymmetric games and role assignment; helping others and ultimatum bargaining; repeated play; morality versus altruism; conditional morality by group identity; newcomer types that are not homo moralis (the paper's "rich" type space, which its proof uses); multiple equilibria.

Divergences

Newcomers come from an eleven-point grid of κ, not every possible preference. Invasion is judged in the paper's limit of vanishingly rare newcomers; the village then shows the newcomers spreading or dying out at a stylised speed, which is not a dynamic the paper studies. Public-good forms B(s) = 3s − s²/4 and C(x) = x², and hawk–dove values v = 4, c = 6, are our choices within the paper's conditions. The score is ours.
