Last question

Does a sixth of a point matter?

Only about 1 in 3 proposals passes the first round, so a nudge can tip a proposal over the line. Applicants from the five biggest countries — Germany, the UK, France, Italy, Spain — find a compatriot on their panel about 3 times as often as everybody else.

Across both rounds, about 14 in 100 proposals are funded. How much bigger is the big countries' predicted funding edge from compatriots than everybody else's?

How to play

The setting. The European Research Council gives grants of up to €2.5 million. Panels of judges from many countries mark each proposal from 1 to 5, in half points. In every case, the compatriot judges (same country as the applicant) give higher marks on average than other judges.

The four explanations. Better proposals: those applicants really sent better work. Generous judges: those judges mark everybody high. Knows them better: a compatriot can judge the work more precisely. Favours their own: the same work simply gets a higher mark from a compatriot.

The three tests.

  • Same proposal — compares a compatriot judge's mark with the other judges' marks on the very same proposal. The work is identical, so its quality cannot explain a gap that survives.
  • Same judge — compares how one judge marks compatriots with how that same judge marks everybody else. The judge is identical, so generosity cannot explain a gap that survives.
  • Whole spread — on the same proposals, how often compatriot and other judges give each mark. Bars above the line: compatriots give that mark more often.

Reading a test. The dot is the gap that remains; the bar around it is the range it could plausibly be in. Still there: the range stays above zero. Gone: the range reaches zero.

Winning. A case is solved when you accuse the true explanation. Using fewer tests is neat but not required. There are three practice panels (invented countries, exaggerated effects) and then the real one, the ERC itself.

What the game shows. How researchers can tell bias from innocent explanations without ever seeing how good a proposal really is, and what they found when they did it for 37,073 real proposals.

About

One of Us

Built from Nicola Fuchs-Schündeln and Felix Holub, Homophily in Grant Evaluations (manuscript, WZB Berlin Social Science Center and Goethe University Frankfurt). The data are all 37,073 applications to the European Research Council, 2019–2023. Code: doi:10.6084/m9.figshare.30789395.

The discovery

When a judge shares an applicant's country, the applicant's marks are higher. On its own that proves nothing. The study rules out the innocent explanations one by one. It compares the same proposal marked by matched and unmatched judges, so quality is held fixed. It compares the same judge marking matched and unmatched applicants, so generosity is held fixed. It looks at the whole spread: better knowledge would add marks at both ends, but the extra marks are top marks only. And it controls for shared taste: closeness of the judge's own papers to the proposal, coauthorship, and topic. What survives is homophily, a bonus for being one of us. It is +0.164 on Step 1 project scores when country of work and citizenship both match (95% CI 0.135–0.193), +0.075 for citizenship only, +0.056 for country only, and +0.024 for gender. At the panel level, applicants from the five biggest countries meet compatriots about three times as often. That goes with a predicted funding boost of 1.7 percentage points against 0.8 for everybody else, on a base of 14%. This last part is a correlation.

Fidelity: three parts, labelled

The three practice panels are illustrative. They are invented countries with invented, exaggerated effects (0.30–0.40 points instead of 0.164). Each is simulated from a toy model in which one explanation is true. They teach the method; the paper says nothing about them. In the "knows them better" panel, the judges are Bayesian: compatriots read a precise signal of quality, others a very noisy one, and both shrink towards the average. A practice panel whose test lands on the wrong side of the line by chance is re-drawn, about 1 time in 40.

The fourth case is statistical: a reconstruction calibrated to the paper, not the confidential ERC data. It has 37,073 proposals, about 4.2 judges each, a Step 1 mark mean of 3.29 and SD of 0.99, about 3% of marks from a judge who shares country and citizenship, and a bonus that gives 0.166 when the paper's own estimator is run on it (paper: 0.164). Every player sees the same reconstruction. It demonstrates what the paper's estimates look like through the three tests. It assumes normal-ish latent marks rounded to half points, matches unrelated to quality (so the headline gap equals the bonus), and an additive bonus. That bonus puts somewhat more weight on a 5.0 than the data do (+0.02 against the paper's +0.010). The paper establishes the estimates above on 155,000 real reviews, with standard errors clustered by proposal and reviewer. The ranges in the game are 99% intervals from our simulation, not the paper's.

The closing question is the paper's Table 2 and is correlational. Panel decisions are made once per proposal, so quality cannot be held fixed.

The paper calls the effect homophily. It does not say whether it is deliberate: "Favours their own" means only that the same work gets a higher mark from a compatriot.

What is cut

Step 2 reviews (similar sizes), PI scores, the split by grant scheme and by domain, the shared-taste test (shown only as a sentence), same-rank homophily between elite institutions (none found), the chair results, and the study's policy ideas: limiting matched assignments, more diverse panels, partial lotteries near the cutoff, blind project scoring.
