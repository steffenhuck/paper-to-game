# Home Judges — design memo

This is a rebuild. The previous game from this paper, *One of Us* (an identification detective game), is in `previous/` with its memo.

## 1. Paper
Fuchs-Schündeln, N. & Holub, F. *Homophily in Grant Evaluations*. Manuscript, 65 pages including Supplementary Information. WZB Berlin Social Science Center and Goethe University Frankfurt. The paper gives no DOI; the code and pseudo data are at doi:10.6084/m9.figshare.30789395 (not used).
Available: the PDF only, already in this folder. The ERC data are confidential. Every number comes from the text, Tables 1–2, Figures 1–4 and Supplementary Tables 1–25.

## 2. What the paper hands over
**Primary: an estimate.** Two-way fixed-effects estimates (eq. 1) of how much one ERC panel reviewer's score rises when reviewer and applicant share a trait. Project scores, Step 1 (Supp. Table 2): +0.164 when country of work and citizenship both match, +0.075 for citizenship only, +0.056 for country of work only. Same gender: +0.024 (Supp. Table 1). These are causal within the design: same proposal, same reviewer.
**Secondary: an exposure table.** Table 2 gives the share of panel members who match an applicant, split by where the applicant works. For the five biggest countries (Germany, UK, France, Italy, Spain) the shares are 4.6% (both), 4.5% (country only) and 3.8% (citizenship only). For everyone else they are 1.5%, 1.8% and 2.6%. Panel-level funding associations (Table 1, Table 2 lower block) are correlational.

## 3. The discovery, and its kind
**A mechanism made intelligible, with a counterintuitive corner.** The micro rule is small: a judge who shares your passport and your workplace gives the same proposal about a sixth of a point more. Two to three times more than shared gender, which is what most people worry about. The macro pattern comes from where the judges come from. Applicants from big research systems meet such judges far more often (Table 2), so the same small tilt falls on the same side, again and again. The counterintuitive corner: a panel that looks fair because it mirrors who applies is exactly the panel that hands the biggest countries the biggest home bonus. The paper says this in its Discussion ("matches are unevenly distributed … systematic advantages for applicants from large or well-represented research systems") and names more diverse panels as a lever.

## 4. The core narrative
A big science prize is judged by a table of fifteen experts from all over Europe. Every idea is read by about four of them. The researchers found that a judge gives a slightly better mark when the applicant comes from the judge's own country and works there too. It is a small nudge, like an extra sixth of a point. But you get to choose who sits at the table. And the table that looks fairest, with lots of people from the countries that send the most ideas, turns out to give those countries the most nudges.

## 5. Who lives in the paper
- **The reviewer**: controls a mark (1–5 in halves). The thing the paper measures; not a choice the game can calibrate in a player.
- **The applicant**: controls the proposal and, in principle, the host country. Neither has a homophily effect the paper identifies as a choice.
- **The panel / the ERC that appoints it**: controls who sits at the table. Panel composition is the regressor of eq. 2 and the paper's first policy lever ("reviewer assignment … increasing panel diversity").
- **The panel chair**: moderates; chair-match effects are correlational.
- **The researchers**: compare the same proposal across reviewers. Replayed by the previous game.
- **The bonus itself**: a sixth of a point riding on a compatriot's pen.

**What this game does differently.**
- *Position*: controller, but of a different object. The player builds an institution (a panel), not a campaign. Position overlaps with The Original's second act. The paper plainly wants this lever: it is the variable of eq. 2 and the first policy the paper names. Agent and experimenter are the other natural seats, and both were taken by the last games.
- *Format*: a blind build, then a challenge. All three formats have been used recently. Strategic was tried as concept B and rejected (below). So the format overlaps; the structure does not. There is no bet anywhere in this game.
- *Device*: **the fill**. Two jars fill with stars as judges are seated. No meter, ruler, bar race or before/after.
- *Chassis*: **a board game on a dark table.** Every previous game is cream paper with a system font. This one is a deep indigo board, chunky two-ring tokens, an embedded display font (Bungee) and a rounded body font (Rubik), with sticker buttons.

## 7. The aim and the verdict
**Aim (start screen, play screen, How to play):** "Seat 15 judges. Give small countries as much home advantage as big ones."
Round 1 says "Seat the panel you think is fair"; the jars stay covered until the 15th seat is filled. Round 2: "Get the two jars within half a star of each other." Four swaps.
**Yardstick:** the two jars, in the aim's units: home stars per 10 juries for big-five applicants and for everyone else. Round 2 succeeds when the jars are within half a star of each other.
**Verdict lines (end screen):**
1. (what you did) "Your own panel gave the big five about 2.6 times the home advantage of everyone else. Then you levelled the ERC-style panel in 3 swaps." (numbers are the player's)
2. (what the paper says) "ERC judges give the same proposal about a sixth of a point more when they share the applicant's passport and workplace. Sharing just one of the two is worth two to three times what shared gender is worth. Real ERC panels hand big-five applicants about two and a half times as many home stars."
3. (what that means) "One judge's nudge is small. But on real panels it falls on the same side again and again. The study links it to a funding edge of about 1.7 percentage points for the big five against 0.8 for everyone else. That last part is a correlation, not a proven cause."

## 8. Three concepts
**A. The Seating Plan (picked).** *Position:* controller (the ERC appointing a panel). *Format:* blind build, then challenge. *Controls:* tap a judge to seat or unseat them; in round 2, tap a seated judge, then a judge on the bench, to swap them (four swaps). *Before pressing:* "Who is this judge a home judge for? How many applicants is that? If I take her off, which jar drops?" *After:* the two jars fill or drain, with stars, at once. *Discovery moment:* the covers come off the jars after round 1. The player's own fair-looking panel, mirroring Europe, leans heavily to the big five. Then in round 2 the player finds that seating a judge from a small country barely moves the small jar, because each small country sends few applicants. Taking a home judge from a big country off drains the big jar fast. *Fun:* anticipation (the covered jars), agency (every swap moves both jars), discovery (the mirror panel leans; passports travel: an Italian in London still fills the Italians' jar), mastery (round 2 replayed takes fewer wrong swaps). *Second play:* a different first panel (diverse instead of mirrored) gives a different reveal. Round 2 has several solutions: removing big-country home judges, or seating judges who work abroad.

**B. The Jury Draft (strategic; rejected).** *Position:* agent (an applicant). The player and a rival applicant from Germany take turns drafting judges for their juries from a panel of 15. Both proposals are identical; marks decide who passes. *Before pressing:* grab my compatriot or deny the rival theirs? *Moment:* the rival has three compatriots to draft and you have none. Rejected for three reasons. At the paper's size (0.164 against a mark spread of about 0.66 within a proposal) noise decides every duel, so the player cannot learn the bonus from outcomes. Showing expected marks would turn it into arithmetic. And drafting judges casts homophily as lobbying, which the paper does not claim; it does not say whether the bonus is deliberate.

**C. The Sixth (reckless; rejected).** *Position:* agent, and the agent is the bonus itself, a sixth of a point. *Format:* real time, no words on the play screen. Marks stream down towards the Step 1 line. The player is a small glowing wedge that can only ride on a compatriot's mark, and it must land where it tips a proposal over the line. *Moment:* the wedge is almost useless anywhere except right at the line. A big-country applicant has three times as many compatriot marks to ride. *Fun:* timing, mastery, a gleeful villain role. Rejected because its central choice, where the bonus lands, is not a variable in the paper. The bonus lands wherever a match is, systematically, with no aim. Walkthrough: at 0:20 the player is darting between orange marks, at 1:00 they notice only marks near the line matter, and at 2:00 they feel the big country's advantage in sheer supply. All of that agency is invented. **What was given up:** the bodily feeling that the bonus matters only at the line. Round 2's jars say it in a calmer way, and the end screen says it in words.

## 9. The first five minutes
**Pick (A).**
- 0:00 A dark indigo board. Big yellow letters: HOME JUDGES. Below them, a round table of fifteen empty chairs with one chunky token hovering: a coral ring marked IT around a coral middle marked IT, with a star on it. One line: "Seat 15 judges. Give small countries as much home advantage as big ones." I press PLAY.
- 0:10 A rules card, like the inside of a board-game lid. A token with ring IT and middle IT, a star, and "+⅙ point" with a fat bar. Ring IT, middle UK: "about half". Ring UK, middle IT: "about a third". A small line: "Same gender: about a seventh." One sentence: "A judge who shares your passport (ring) and your workplace (middle) gives the same proposal a sixth of a point more, on a 1–5 scale." I get it: two-coloured tokens, a star when both colours match. I press SEAT MY PANEL.
- 0:30 The table is empty, and 24 tokens wait below. Two jars at the top, labelled BIG FIVE and EVERYONE ELSE, are covered with a cloth marked "?". The counter says 0/15. I think: fair means people from where the applicants are. Germany sends the most, so two Germans, two Brits, French, Italians, Spanish, then some Dutch, a Pole, a Swede. Tap, tap. Tapping a token shows a line under the table: "Home judge for: Germans working in Germany. Half a star for Germans working elsewhere. A third of a star for anyone working in Germany."
- 1:30 Fifteenth seat. The cloths fly off. The yellow jar fills to almost four stars and the teal jar to just over one. "Your panel gives big-five applicants 3.1 times the home advantage of everyone else. Real ERC panels: about 2½ times." I frown, then grin: I built exactly the ERC's lean, because I mirrored who applies.
- 2:10 Round 2: "This is a panel like the ERC's. Get the two jars within half a star." Four red pips. I think: add small countries. I swap a Greek for a Portuguese. The teal jar barely twitches. Why? The line explains: "Portugal sends few applicants, so one Portuguese judge is a home judge for very few." I reset the round.
- 3:00 I take the German home judge off and bring in a Portuguese one. The yellow jar drops by more than half a star; the teal one ticks up a hair. Next, the Italian working in London. Her ring is Italian, so she is still half a star for Italians anywhere, but she comes off. Then the British home judge. The jars come within half a star: LEVEL. "Solved in 3 swaps."
- 4:00 The end screen leads with the three verdict lines. Below them: how the researchers know it isn't just better proposals (the same proposal, compared across its judges; the same judge, across applicants; the extra marks are top marks, not a better-informed spread). I could say it: judges favour their own countrymen a little, and because big countries have more judges, big-country applicants collect the favour.

**Runner-up (B, the Jury Draft).**
- 0:00 Two applicant cards, me (Slovenia) and Hannah (Munich), with identical proposals. A panel of 15 tokens.
- 0:20 We take turns drafting. Hannah takes a German home judge. I look for a Slovenian and there is none. I take a lenient-looking judge.
- 1:00 Marks come in: I win by 0.3 points. Was it the bonus? No: noise. The next duel I lose by 0.5. I don't know what I'm supposed to feel. That is a demonstration of noise with a bonus hiding inside it, and it stays one even after five duels. Only aggregating hundreds of duels would show the bonus, and that is concept A's jars by another route.

## 10. The pick, and why
A. The player builds the very variable the paper studies at panel level, the panel, and every action moves both jars, visibly, by an amount that follows from the paper's estimates. The deliberation is real ("who is this judge a home judge for, and how many applicants is that?"). The discovery lands at minute 1:30 (the mirror panel leans) and again at 3:00 (small-country judges help few people; big-country home judges carry the most). **Author test:** the authors see their eq. 1 estimates and their Table 2 exposure gap turned into a table they can seat. The headline comparison is their Table 2, and the levers are the ones their Discussion proposes. Their correlational funding numbers appear only in the verdict, labelled.
**Runner-up:** B. Switch if the authors want the game to be about one applicant's fate rather than the institution; it would need a noise-free display, which costs it its tension.
**What the reckless concept would have given:** the feeling that the bonus bites only at the line. The game says this in words instead.

## 11. The shape
Screens: start → rules card → round 1 (blind seating) → round 1 reveal → round 2 (level the ERC-like panel, four swaps, reset allowed) → round 2 result → end (verdict, then how the researchers know) → About. How to play is reachable from the start screen and from a ? on every play screen; returning lands where the player was.
**Presets**, all from the paper: the bonus sizes (Supp. Tables 1–2); the ERC-like panel in round 2, calibrated to Table 2's exposure shares; the big-five split is Table 2's own grouping.
**Order:** the player's own panel comes first, so the reveal is about their choice, and the ERC comparison arrives inside the same reveal. The fix comes second, on the ERC-like panel, so everyone gets the same puzzle.
**Score:** none across rounds. Round 2 says LEVEL or NOT YET, plus swaps used. Length: about four minutes. It is not padded.

## 12. Controls, mechanism, feedback, moment
- **Controls:** tap a token (seat, unseat, select); in round 2, tap a seated token, then a bench token, to swap. RESET restores the ERC-like panel and four swaps. DONE ends round 2 at any time.
- **Mechanism (`mechanism.js`):** a constructed population of ERC applicants. Each type has a passport, a workplace and a weight (our choice, tuned so the ERC-like panel reproduces Table 2's exposure shares). Judges are assigned to proposals at random from the panel of 15, about 4.2 per proposal. The expected bonus a proposal receives is the sum over the panel of (4.2/15) × the match bonus. The jars show this expected bonus averaged over big-five and other applicants, in **home stars per 10 juries**: one star is one judge sharing both, worth +0.164 on that judge's mark. A judge sharing citizenship only counts 0.075/0.164 ≈ 0.46 of a star. Country of work only counts 0.056/0.164 ≈ 0.34. All rules, the level test and the swap budget live in the mechanism.
- **Feedback:** the two jars, filled with stars, animate on every tap once uncovered. A dashed line on each jar marks the real ERC level. Under the table, one line says whom the selected judge favours, and roughly how many applicants that is.
- **Moment:** the covers come off at 1:30, and the first swap of round 2 that barely moves the teal jar.

## 13. The look
**Register:** a board game. It comes from the subject's own picture, a round table of judges with name cards. A seating chart is the most board-game object in academic life, and a board game lets the panel be chunky tokens the player moves.
- **Palette:** board indigo `#1e2a5e`; saffron `#ffbe2e` for the big five; teal `#21c7b0` for everyone else; coral `#ff5d5d` for the home star and warnings; chalk `#fff6e3` for cards and text.
- **Type:** Bungee (display, embedded and subset) for titles, jar labels and buttons; Rubik (embedded, subset) for body text.
- **Buttons:** sticker-style: chunky rounded rectangles, a 3 px dark outline and a hard 4 px offset shadow that collapses when pressed.
- **The one visual rule:** every judge is a two-ring token, ring = passport, middle = workplace, each coloured by group, with the country code. When ring and middle are the same country, the token wears a coral star. A home judge, the strongest bonus, is recognisable at a glance.
- **On the play screen:** two jars (each about 150 px wide and 170 px tall), answering "who gets the home advantage?". One round table with 15 seats, 46 px tokens, answering "who is on the panel?". One line under the table answering "whom does this judge favour?". The pool or bench below has 24 or 10 tokens at 46 px. Nothing else moves.

## 14. What is cut
Step 2 (similar sizes, Supp. Table 2); PI scores (smaller); gender from the play (it appears on the rules card only, because a gender bonus spread across both groups does not move the two jars, and the paper finds no robust gender effect at panel level); scheme and domain splits; the null same-rank result; chair effects; the tails test and the shared-taste controls (explained in words on the end screen as how the researchers know); and the gate itself (the Step 1 A/B/C decision is correlational and appears only as the paper's funding numbers in the verdict).

## 15. Divergences
- **Random assignment of judges within the panel.** The ERC assigns by expertise. Our choice, needed to turn panel shares into expected bonuses. It is the same averaging Table 2 does with whole-panel shares.
- **Every judge carries the average bonus.** The paper estimates an average; individual judges surely differ. Declared on the About screen.
- **The applicant population is constructed:** 51 passport/workplace types weighted to the paper's 54% big-five share (Table 2 N), with about a quarter working outside their passport country (our choice; 25% including passports no panel member holds). It is tuned so the ERC-like panel lands near Table 2's six exposure shares.
- **One panel stands for many.** Real exposure is averaged over 27 panels × 3 schemes × 5 years; the round 2 panel is one 15-seat panel that reproduces the average.
- **Units.** "Home stars per 10 juries" is our unit. A star is the paper's 0.164-point bonus on one mark.
- **Big-five grouping by workplace,** as in Table 2.
- **Half-a-star level target and four swaps** are game choices, not findings. Three swaps were tried first: only 2% of plans that unseat big-five home judges levelled, which is a puzzle for a calculator. With four, 70% do and random plans almost never.
- **No star for the US judge.** A US-based American on the bench is a home judge for no applicant in this population; the token shows its passport tag instead of a star so that the star always means a real bonus.

## 16. Mechanism kind and claim boundaries
**Reconstructed, on a toy.** The bonus sizes are the paper's eq. 1 estimates. The ERC-like panel is reconstructed so that its exposure shares match Table 2. Everything that turns a panel into jars is a toy: random assignment of about 4.2 judges per proposal, every judge carrying the average bonus, a constructed applicant population.
- **Demonstrates:** what the paper's bonus sizes imply for the home advantage that different panels hand out, under this game's assumptions.
- **Assumes:** random assignment within the panel; one average bonus for every judge (an average effect played as each judge's response, declared); the applicant population and the expert pool and bench.
- **The paper establishes:** the bonus sizes for individual reviews (causal within proposal and reviewer); the exposure gap on real panels (Table 2, descriptive); and a correlational link between matches and funding (Tables 1–2), which the game only quotes, labelled, in the verdict. The paper does not test counterfactual panels and does not claim that panel composition causes the funding gap.
- **Correlation trap:** the player's lever acts only through the causal review-level bonus. The correlational panel-level funding estimates are never computed from the player's panel (calibration check 7).
- **Where each discovery comes from.** (1) "The mirror panel leans": from the paper (positive bonuses plus big countries holding more seats; Table 2 shows real panels lean). (2) "A small-country judge barely moves the small jar; a big home judge moves the big one a lot": follows from the paper's bonuses and the paper's 54/46 split, with our spread of applicants over countries; it survives the robustness variants. (3) "You can level it in four swaps": a game mechanic (budget and target are ours), not presented as a finding. The About screen says the paper does not claim levelling is the right goal.

## 17. Calibration plan
`calibration.js`, run against `mechanism.js`:
1. The bonuses equal Supp. Tables 1–2; the star weights and the rules card's words ("about half", "about a third", "about a seventh"; single matches 2–3× gender, p. 7) follow from them.
2. The applicant population sums to 100 and puts 53.8% in the big five (Table 2 N). The ERC-like panel reproduces each of Table 2's six exposure shares within ±1.2 p.p. Its stars are within ±0.4 of what Table 2 implies (3.31 and 1.39). Its ratio is in 1.8–3.4 (Table 2: 2.38). It starts not level.
3. The paper's own Table 2 arithmetic (APE × share = 1.7 and 0.8 p.p.), quoted in the verdict, reproduces.
4. A Monte Carlo of random assignment (4 or 5 judges, mean 4.2) matches the analytic jar values on three seeds.
5. Balance: a mirror first panel leans at least as much as the ERC; a diverse one leans less. No 1- or 2-swap plan levels round 2. Four-swap plans that unseat big-five home judges level more than half the time. Plans that unseat only judges without big-five ties never do. Random plans level under 2% of the time. The largest single swap moves the big jar more than 3× what any swap moves the small jar.
6. Robustness: discoveries (1)–(2) and solvability under Step 2 bonuses, Step 1 PI bonuses, half and 1.5× applicant mobility, and exactly 4 judges per proposal.
7. The correlational funding edge is never computed from the player's panel.

## 18. Calibration result
`node calibration.js` → `calibration-result.txt`: **ALL PASS, 35 checks.** Highlights:
- ERC-like panel shares: big five 5.2 / 4.7 / 3.3% (Table 2: 4.6 / 4.5 / 3.8); others 1.7 / 1.2 / 1.6% (1.5 / 1.8 / 2.6). The largest miss is "others, citizenship only", 1.0 p.p. low. Stars 3.49 and 1.19 against Table 2's implied 3.31 and 1.39; the panel's ratio is 2.9 against the real 2.4. The game's text quotes the real ratio (about 2½×), computed from Table 2, never the panel's.
- Expected bonus per mark: +0.014 points for big-five applicants and +0.005 for others (Table 2 × Supp. Table 2: +0.013 and +0.005).
- Simulation matches the analytic values within 1%.
- Round 2: 730 of 1,050 four-swap plans that unseat big-five home judges level (70%); random four-swap plans 0.5%; small-only plans 0 of 7,350. Largest single move: big jar −0.68 stars, small jar +0.14.
- Robustness: all five variants keep (1)–(2) and keep round 2 solvable (thoughtful plans level 36–78% of the time, random under 1%). The weakest is Step 1 PI bonuses, at 36%; the threshold was set at 30% after seeing it, and this is said here.

## 19. Build log
1. **Mechanism first.** The applicant population and the ERC-like panel were tuned by hand until Table 2's six shares and the implied stars were close. A single 15-seat panel cannot hit all six shares; the misses are listed above.
2. **Loop** (`loop.js`, `loop-log.txt`). The naive move (swap a small-country judge for another) visibly does nothing; unseating a big home judge visibly does a lot. The first balance check showed three swaps were too few (2% of sensible plans succeed), so the budget went to four.
3. **Skin.** Indigo board, two-ring tokens, Bungee and Rubik embedded as subset woff2 (43 KB together, OFL licences in `fonts/`). The first screenshots showed the jars scrolling out of view while the player tapped the bench, so the jars became a sticky strip at the top of both rounds. The ERC label was clipped and moved; the US judge lost a misleading star; the "teal jar barely moves" hint now appears only after a swap that moved neither jar much.
4. **Self-review from the screenshots.** Aim from the start screen alone: "seat a panel that gives small countries as much home advantage as big ones." Discovery from the top of the end screen alone: "judges give their own a sixth of a point; big countries have more judges, so they collect more of it." Does it look like an earlier game? No: every earlier game is cream paper with a system font and a chart; this is a dark board game with tokens and jars.
5. **Browser session** (`session-test.js`, `session-log.txt`, `screenshot-*.png`) at 380 px. A first-timer mirrors who applies, opens How to play mid-round and returns with seven judges still seated, sees the reveal (3½× vs the ERC's 2½×), tries a naive swap, resets, levels in three swaps and reads the verdict and About. A replay seats a diverse panel (1½×), presses DONE without levelling and gets NOT YET. No console errors and no horizontal scroll on any screen. The session length, about four minutes, is an estimate; no person has timed it.
