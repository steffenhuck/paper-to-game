# One of Us — design memo

## 1. Paper
Fuchs-Schündeln, N. & Holub, F. *Homophily in Grant Evaluations*. Manuscript (65 pages incl. Supplementary Information), WZB Berlin Social Science Center / Goethe University Frankfurt. No DOI given for the paper; code and pseudo data at doi:10.6084/m9.figshare.30789395 (not used).
Available: the PDF only. The ERC data are confidential. Every number in the game comes from the paper's text, Tables 1–2, Figures 1–4 and Supplementary Tables 1–25. Values taken from figures are marked as such.

## 2. What the paper hands over
**Primary: estimates.** Two-way fixed-effects estimates (eq. 1) of how much an individual ERC panel reviewer's score (1–5, in half points) rises when reviewer and applicant share gender, country of work, citizenship, or both. The estimates use 37,073 proposals from 2019–2023, about 155,000 Step 1 reviews and 47,000 Step 2 reviews. Also: distributional estimates (Fig. 3: which score values the match moves) and correlational panel-level estimates (eq. 2, Table 1, Table 2).
**Secondary: a method.** The identification is the paper's main contribution (p. 15). The same proposal is scored by matched and unmatched reviewers, which holds quality fixed. Each reviewer scores matched and unmatched applicants, which holds leniency fixed. The tails test (pp. 9–10) separates favouritism from better information. Controls for semantic closeness, coauthorship and topic match (Fig. 2) rule out shared taste.

## 3. The discovery, and its kind
**Identification.** A naive observation, "compatriot reviewers give compatriots higher marks", has several innocent explanations. The paper rules them out one by one:
- better proposals: the gap holds within the same proposal;
- generous reviewers: it holds within the same reviewer;
- inside knowledge: matched reviewers give more top marks but not more bottom marks;
- shared taste: it is unchanged by the closeness controls.
What survives is a bonus for being one of us: +0.164 on Step 1 project scores when country and citizenship both match. That is 2–3 times the gender effect (0.024).
**Secondary, a trade-off of scale:** the bonus is small per score but unevenly distributed. Applicants from the five largest countries meet more compatriots on panels. That goes with a 1.7 vs 0.8 percentage-point predicted funding boost, a gap of 0.9 points on a 14% base (correlational).

## 4. The core narrative
A big prize for scientists is handed out by judges from many countries. Each idea is marked by about four judges. Someone notices that judges give higher marks to applicants from their own country. That could be unfair, but there are innocent explanations. Maybe those applicants simply sent better ideas. Maybe some judges are kind to everybody. Maybe a judge from home just knows better what the applicant can do. You get to play detective with the same tricks the researchers used, and find out which explanation is left standing.

## 5. Fidelity level and claim boundaries
Mixed, labelled by part.
- **Training cases 1–3: illustrative.** Each case is an invented panel ("Norland", "Vestria"…) whose hidden truth is one of four explanations. Its scores are simulated from a toy model in which that explanation is true, with effects exaggerated to 0.30–0.40 points (the ERC's is 0.164) so they show in 4,000 proposals. *Demonstrates*: why each of the paper's comparisons rules out one innocent explanation. *Assumes*: the toy model (additive quality + reviewer leniency + match bonus + noise; a Bayesian reviewer for the information case), the effect sizes, the assignment. *Paper establishes*: nothing about these panels. They teach the method.
- **Case 4, the real ERC: statistical.** This is a reconstruction calibrated to the paper's reported numbers: 37,073 proposals, 4.2 reviewers each, Step 1 project score mean 3.29 and SD 0.99, a same-country-and-citizenship bonus of 0.164, and about 3% matched reviews (Table 2 panel shares). *Demonstrates*: that the paper's estimates, run through the same three comparisons, give "still there, still there, top marks only". *Assumes*: normal latent scores rounded to half points, no sorting of better proposals or more generous reviewers towards matches (the paper reports no raw gap, so our raw gap equals the causal one), independent matches. *Paper establishes*: the 0.164 estimate with CI [0.135, 0.193] (Supp. Table 2), the tail pattern (Fig. 3, Supp. Tables 15–22), robustness to closeness controls (Fig. 2).
- **The closing bet: in the paper, correlational.** It uses the funding gap between the top five countries and the rest (Table 2). The paper labels panel-level results correlational (p. 10), and so does the game.
- The paper calls the effect *homophily* and does not say whether it is deliberate. The game's suspect, "Favours their own", is defined as "the same work gets a higher mark from a compatriot", and the About screen says that intent is not identified.

## 6. The aim and the verdict
**Aim:** "Judges give higher marks to applicants from their own country. In each case, find the real reason."
**Yardstick:** per case, Solved / Not solved (plus tests used). At the end: cases solved out of 4.
**Verdict lines:** (you) "You solved 3 of 4 cases." (paper) "At the real ERC, the same proposal gets 0.16 more points from a judge who shares the applicant's country and citizenship. It isn't better proposals, generous judges, inside knowledge or shared taste." (means) "A sixth of a point is small. But applicants from big countries meet compatriots more often, and their predicted funding edge is about twice as large."

## 7. Concepts
1. **One of Us (picked).** Position: experimenter (the paper's own method replayed). Format: discovery. Controls: three test buttons (Same proposal / Same judge / Whole spread), then accuse one of four suspects. Before pressing: "which comparison would expose this explanation? Do I know enough to accuse?" Seen after: a gap meter (still there / gone) or a Fig. 3-style up/down bar chart (top-only ramp, both-ends smile, flat). Discovery: only favouritism survives all three; the real ERC behaves exactly like that. Fun: discovery (each test kills a suspect), agency (choice of test and when to accuse), mastery (later cases are solved in fewer tests because the player knows which test exposes what), anticipation (the closing bet). Replay: the three training truths are drawn from four, so the order and the right test change. That is practice at the method, not new content, and it is not claimed as more.
2. **The Assigner (rejected).** Position: controller. The player assigns four reviewers from a 15-person panel to each proposal and tries to fund the truly best. Rejected: without an invented cost of avoiding compatriots (expertise), the right move is trivial. With one, the trade-off is ours, not the paper's: the paper shows topic match does *not* explain the bonus.
3. **The Gate (runner-up).** Position: subject/forecaster. You are an applicant from Malta and from Germany in turn, near the Step 1 cutoff. You watch four judges' marks and the A/B/C line, and bet on the passing odds. The discovery is the amplification at the gate. Weaker: the player only watches dice, and the panel-level numbers are correlational, so every lever would be a correlation played as a cause.

## 8. The first five minutes (pick)
0:00 Start: "One of Us". A row of four score cards, one in orange marked "same country". The aim sits in a box. I press Start case 1.
0:10 Case 1, Norland. "Norlandic judges marked Norlandic applicants 0.38 points higher (1–5 scale) than other judges' marks." Below it are four suspects with one-line descriptions and three tests. I wonder whether it's unfair or whether the Norlandic proposals are just good.
0:30 I press "Same proposal": the very same proposals, the Norlandic judge vs the others. The meter dot sits at +0.02. "Gone." So it was the proposals. I accuse "Better proposals": Solved in 1 test. Grin.
1:10 Case 2, Vestria. Gap +0.35. I try "Same proposal" again: still there, +0.41. That rules out better proposals. Generous judge or favours their own? "Same judge": the Vestrian judges vs their own marks for others: gone, 0.00. They're kind to everyone. Accuse "Generous judge". Solved in 2.
2:20 Case 3, Ostrava. I try "Same proposal": still there. "Same judge": still there. I'm about to accuse Favours their own, but "Knows them better" is still on the board. I try "Whole spread": orange bars go up at both ends, more 5s *and* more 1s. A compatriot judge knows the applicant and spreads the marks. Accuse Knows them better. Solved, 3 tests. I now see that it takes all three tests to convict someone of favouritism.
3:30 Case 4: the real ERC, 37,073 proposals, 155,000 marks. Gap +0.16, small. Same proposal: still there (0.13–0.19). Same judge: still there. Spread: top marks up, low marks down, a ramp. Accuse Favours their own. "Solved. This is what the paper found." A reveal card shows three bars (both country and passport 0.164, citizenship only 0.075, country only 0.056) next to gender 0.024. It notes that the researchers also checked shared taste and the gap did not move.
4:30 Bet: "Does a sixth of a point matter? Applicants from the five biggest countries meet compatriots on panels about three times as often. How much extra funding chance does that go with?" I guess "about 5 points". The card turns: 1.7 vs 0.8 points, a gap of 0.9 on a 14% base. Smaller than I thought, but it's 6% of the odds.
5:00 End: the verdict. I can say it: judges really do mark their compatriots up, and the researchers proved it by comparing the same proposal and the same judge.

## 9. The pick, and why
One of Us. The paper's contribution is identification, and the game makes the player perform it. The deliberation is real (which test, when to accuse). The discovery is the paper's: favouritism is what survives the paper's three checks, and the ERC data pass them all. Runner-up: The Gate. Switch if the author would rather centre the gatekeeping consequence over the method; it would need individual Step 1 score distributions per proposal, which the paper does not report.

## 10. The shape
Screens: start → case 1 → case 2 → case 3 → case 4 (ERC) → reveal + bet → end. How to play and About are reachable from start and from every case (?). Four cases. Score: Solved per case; total out of 4 at the end. Presets: the four suspects are the paper's own list of alternatives (pp. 2–3, 8–10). Order: three training cases first, so the method is learned on clear, exaggerated cases. The real case comes last, so the player recognises the pattern at the true, small scale. The discovery arrives at minute 2–3 (case 3, the first time all three tests are needed) and is confirmed on the ERC at 3:30.

## 11. Controls, mechanism, feedback, moment
Controls: three test buttons per case, four accuse buttons. Mechanism (`mechanism.js`): simulate reviews in a world where one explanation is true. Each test is computed on the simulated reviews. *Same proposal* is the mean within-proposal difference between matched and unmatched marks, with a 95% interval. *Same judge* is the mean within-judge difference. *Whole spread* compares, within proposals, the share of matched vs unmatched marks in five bins (1–1.5, 2–2.5, 3–3.5, 4–4.5, 5), classified as ramp / smile / flat. Accusation is scored against the hidden truth. Feedback: the gap meter with zero line and interval; the up/down bars; per-case verdict with the explanation after the accusation. Moment: case 3 or 4, when two tests say "still there" and the player realises that only the spread can separate knowing from favouring.

## 12. What is cut
Step 2 (similar magnitudes, Supp. Table 2); PI scores; the three-way match breakdown in the tests (shown only in the reveal); gender in the tests (reveal only); scheme and domain splits (Supp. Tables 3–11); same-rank (elite institution) homophily, which is null (Supp. Table 23); the panel chair results; the policy discussion (assignment limits, lotteries, blind project scoring), which gets one line in About.

## 13. Divergences
- Training cases are invented, with effects of 0.30–0.40 points (ERC: 0.164) so they show in 4,000 proposals. Our choice. A practice panel whose test lands on the wrong side of the line by chance (about 1 draw in 40) is re-drawn, so practice teaches cleanly.
- Every player sees the same ERC reconstruction (seed 12, chosen from 24 because its eq. 1 estimate, 0.166, is closest to 0.164).
- Ranges in the game are 99% intervals from a delete-a-group jackknife over proposals in our simulation, not the paper's two-way clustered 95% CIs.
- Judges are made sparing with a 5.0 (a smooth top threshold) to bring the bonus's effect on P(5.0) towards the paper's; it is still twice the paper's value (see §16).
- Fictional country names, so no real country is implied to behave as a training case does.
- The ERC reconstruction has no confounding sorting: its raw gap equals the causal effect. The paper does not report the raw gap.
- The suspect "Shared taste" is not playable. The paper's test needs SPECTER2 embeddings and coauthor networks. It appears in the reveal as a fourth check the researchers ran.
- Five score bins instead of the paper's nine.
- "Solved in N tests" is a game mechanic: the paper does not rank comparisons by economy.

## 14. Visual world
The paper's imagery: score sheets with marks from 1 to 5 in half points, panels of judges, Fig. 3's bars against a zero line. Rules: (1) ink on cream paper, like a review form; (2) one accent colour, orange, means "compatriot" everywhere, and grey-blue means "other judges"; (3) one big picture per test result: a gap meter (a horizontal line, zero marked, a fat dot with its interval) or five up/down bars; (4) suspects are four large cards with a one-word icon glyph, crossed out as the player rules them out. Legibility: on the case screen the player looks at the headline gap (one big number), at most three result pictures (each full width, about 90 px tall), and four suspect cards. Each picture answers one question: is the gap still there? Which marks do compatriots hand out more?

## 15. Fidelity plan
Headless, `fidelity.js`, several seeds:
1. ERC reconstruction: score mean 3.29 ± 0.05 and SD 0.99 ± 0.05 (Supp. Table 2). P(5.0) ≈ 0.047 ± 0.015 (p. 9). A two-way FE estimate (alternating projections) recovers 0.164 within the paper's CI [0.135, 0.193]. Same-proposal and same-judge tests also fall in that CI.
2. ERC tails: ΔP(5.0) within [0.002, 0.017] most seeds (paper +0.010); ΔP(1.0) ≤ 0 (paper −0.006). The spread is classified "ramp" on every seed.
3. Training worlds, on every seed: each test gives its designed result ("gone" exactly where the explanation predicts, "still there" elsewhere; ramp/smile/flat as designed). Headline gaps of all four worlds fall within 0.25–0.5, so the headline does not give the truth away.
4. Robustness of the central discovery: (a) the info world still produces a smile, and leniency/favour still produce no smile, under ±50% changes to the precision and effect parameters. (b) With confounding sorting added to the ERC world (better proposals more often matched, +0.2 quality; matched judges more generous, +0.1), the naive gap moves while the within-proposal-and-judge estimate stays at 0.164. The paper's identification claim holds in our construction.
5. Gate cross-check (memo only): in the reconstruction, one matched reviewer among four changes the chance of being in the top 32% by about the paper's correlational 1.7 points per same-country panelist. This is not displayed; it is a consistency check.

## 16. Fidelity result and build log
**Fidelity result** (`fidelity.js`, full output in `fidelity-result.txt`): ALL PASS, 30 checks.
- ERC reconstruction over 5 seeds: about 155,700 reviews; mean 3.31 (paper 3.29); SD 0.99 (0.99); P(5.0) 0.042 (0.047). The paper's eq. 1, run by alternating projections, gives 0.145–0.185 per seed, mean 0.167 (paper 0.164, CI 0.135–0.193). The game's own reconstruction gives 0.166. The same-proposal and same-judge tests stay "still there" on every seed. ΔP(1.0) is −0.008 (paper −0.006, inside its CI). The spread reads as a ramp on every seed.
- **Known miss:** ΔP(5.0) is +0.023 against the paper's +0.010 (CI to 0.017). An additive bonus on a latent score puts more of its weight on 5.0 than the data do. In the paper, the extra marks land mostly on 4.0–4.5. The check is set to direction and a ±0.015 bound and is reported as outside the paper's CI. Displayed consequence: in the ERC spread chart, the "5" bar (+2.1 p.p., merged with nothing) is a little too tall relative to "4" (+4.7, merged with 4.5). The qualitative pattern (top up, bottom down) is the paper's.
- Training worlds: each explanation gives its designed [same proposal, same judge, spread] signature on 30/30 raw draws per world. Headlines run 0.19–0.33; the information world sits at the low end (0.19–0.28), a weak tell.
- Robustness: the information world smiles, and favouritism, leniency and quality do not, under ±50% parameter changes (all ≥ 8/10, in practice 10/10). With confounding sorting added to the ERC world (matches more likely for better proposals and more generous judges), the naive gap rises to 0.57 while eq. 1 stays at 0.185, inside the paper CI. The identification logic the game teaches holds in the construction.
- Gate cross-check (not displayed): one matched reviewer among about 4 raises the chance of being in the top 32% by average mark by about +2.9 p.p. in the reconstruction. That is the same order as the paper's correlational +1.7 p.p. per same-country/citizenship panel member, which counts panel members who did not review the proposal.

**Build log.**
1. Mechanism first (`mechanism.js`). Calibration needed a left-skewed quality distribution (minus an exponential) and a smooth top threshold to get mean 3.29, SD 0.99 and a rare 5.0 together. The latent bonus is 0.186, so that eq. 1 on rounded marks returns 0.164. Training-world leniency is centred within judge groups so chance does not fake a generous group. The test standard errors were switched from a naive formula to a jackknife over proposals after `loop.js` showed a "still there +0.05" in a generous-judges case: the naive SE ignored that one proposal's quality enters several judges' comparisons.
2. Loop (`loop.js`, `loop-log.txt`): a test that reaches zero eliminates a suspect at once. Favouritism needs all three tests; better proposals, generous judges and knowing better can each be caught with one.
3. Skin and text: cream review-form paper, orange = compatriot throughout. Gap meter with a dashed "no gap" line and a faint headline marker, so the player sees the gap shrink or not. Fig. 3-style up/down bars. After the first screenshots, the spread chart's labels were moved off the axis and the accuse buttons widened.
4. Self-review from screenshots: the aim can be stated from the start screen alone ("find out why compatriot judges mark higher"). The discovery can be stated from the top of the end screen ("same proposal, 0.16 more from a compatriot; not quality, generosity, knowledge or taste").
5. Browser session (`session-test.js`, `session-log.txt`, screenshots): full game at 380 px. A first-timer runs all three tests on case 1, opens How to play mid-case and returns with results kept, accuses, takes the bet and sees the verdict, opens About, and retries with new practice cases. No errors, no horizontal scroll on any screen. Estimated session length about 5 minutes (not measured with a person).
