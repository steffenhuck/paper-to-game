HOW DO THEY KNOW IT'S NOT JUST BETTER PROPOSALS?

The researchers compared the same proposal as marked by its different judges, so quality is held fixed. They also compared each judge with their own marks for other applicants, so a generous judge is not mistaken for a biased one. The extra marks are top marks, not a wider spread, so it does not look like inside knowledge. And the bonus stays when they account for judges who work on the same topics or have written papers with the same people.

The study calls it homophily: favouring people like yourself. It does not say whether anyone means to.

HOW TO PLAY

The European Research Council gives out big grants. Each research field has a panel of about 15 judges, and each proposal is marked by about four of them.

Tokens. Every judge is a token. The ring is their passport. The middle is the country they work in. Yellow means one of the five biggest applicant countries (Germany, the UK, France, Italy, Spain). Teal means any other country. Lilac means outside Europe.

The home bonus. The study found that a judge gives the same proposal about a sixth of a point more (marks run 1–5) when the applicant shares the judge's passport and workplace. That is a home star. Sharing only the passport is worth about half a star. Sharing only the workplace is worth about a third.

The jars. BIG FIVE is for applicants who work in those five countries; EVERYONE ELSE is for the rest. Each jar shows how many home stars an average applicant in that group collects per 10 juries. The dashed line is the real ERC's level.

  • Round 1: tap judges in the pool to seat them; tap a seated judge to send them back. When the 15th seat is taken, the jars are uncovered.
  • Round 2: a panel built like the ERC's. Tap a judge at the table, then one on the bench, to swap them. You have four swaps. Get the jars within half a star of each other. RESET starts the round again.

Tap any judge to see whom they favour, and how many applicants that is.

ABOUT

The game in one line

You seat the panel; the jars show who collects the home bonus. The bonus sizes are the study's; the panel, the applicants and the way judges are drawn are this game's.

What the study found

Fuchs-Schündeln and Holub use all 37,073 ERC applications from 2019 to 2023, with about four panel reviewers per proposal. They compare the same proposal across its reviewers and each reviewer across proposals. When reviewer and applicant share both country of work and citizenship, the Step 1 project score is 0.164 points higher on the 1–5 scale (95% CI 0.135–0.193). Citizenship only: +0.075. Country of work only: +0.056. Same gender: +0.024. The extra marks are top marks, and the bonus survives controls for academic closeness, coauthorship and topic. Their Table 2 shows that applicants working in the five biggest countries face panels where 4.6% of members share both their country and citizenship, against 1.5% for everyone else. Panel-level outcomes are correlational: they link these matches to a funding edge of about 1.7 percentage points for the big five against 0.8 for everyone else.

Mechanism: reconstructed, on a toy

This game demonstrates what the study's bonus sizes imply for the home advantage different panels hand out, under this game's assumptions. It assumes that every judge carries the study's average bonus (real judges surely differ); that judges are drawn at random from the panel (the ERC assigns by expertise); and an applicant population of 51 passport-and-workplace types that we constructed so that the round 2 panel reproduces the study's Table 2 exposure shares, give or take about a percentage point. About a quarter of our applicants work outside their passport country; that is our guess. The study establishes the bonus sizes for individual reviews and the exposure gap on real panels. It does not test what other panels would do, and it does not show that panel composition causes the funding gap.

A "home star" is our unit: one judge sharing passport and workplace, worth +0.164 on that judge's mark. Passport only counts 0.46 of a star; workplace only, 0.34. The jars show expected stars per 10 juries for an average applicant in each group. The dashed line is what the study's Table 2 implies for real ERC panels. Round 2's panel is one 15-seat panel standing for the average over 27 panels, three grant types and five years. The four swaps and the half-star target are game choices. The study does not say that levelling is the right goal; it lists more diverse panels, limits on matched assignments, extra reviews near the cutoff, partial lotteries and blind project scoring as ideas to evaluate.

What is cut

Step 2 reviews (similar sizes), PI scores (smaller), gender in the jars, the split by grant scheme and by field, the null result for elite institutions, the panel chair, and the Step 1 cutoff itself.
