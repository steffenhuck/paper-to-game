## 1. Paper

G. W. Leibniz, "On Controversies" (*Des controverses*), 1680, written for Duke Johann Friedrich of Hanover shortly after his death and resumed for Landgrave Ernst von Hessen-Rheinfels. Akademie edition *Sämtliche Schriften und Briefe* IV, 3, 204–212. Read in Lloyd Strickland's translation (© 2021), published at leibniz-translations.com (the page address could not be checked from this session; the author-note says so). No DOI. Available: the translation as a PDF, five pages; no data, no code, no figures, no numbers. The game is built from the text alone.

## 2. What the paper hands over

An **argument** (primary): a dialogue with a prince about a method for writing up controversies, and six closing notes. The method's visible sign: "to write down controversies in such a way that the reader cannot judge which party the author has embraced"; readers "endeavouring to convict you of partiality, will examine you very closely: picking out words everywhere"; "people will fight to claim you or not to claim you", seven cities over Homer. The rapporteur is "neither judge, nor client, nor reconciler". The form "forces people to speak moderately in spite of themselves"; the combatants "pulled by machines". A **catalogue** (secondary): the five ploys one uses "without thinking in the heat of the dispute": one's own order; expansion into volumes; concealing or weakening the opponent's arguments in reporting them; repeating one's reasons without taking account of the answers; digression. And the remedy in the notes: an indisputable order, a summary "so that their whole economy can be seen", unpacking "so that they cannot say that their arguments have been neglected", after which "a man of common sense" can judge without the rapporteur declaring himself. Nothing is formalised; the game formalises the readers and the page.

## 3. The discovery, and its kind

**A mechanism made intelligible**, with a corner the stranger does not expect. The mechanism: fairness has a visible sign, and the sign is produced by form, not by good intentions, because partisan readers read the form (what went in, how long, what was answered, who spoke first and last) and not the truth. The corner: a page nobody can place is not the page with the most on it; it is the page on which every cut is mirrored (or squared against a cut of the other kind on the same side), and under a short page that means saying less, the same on both sides, rather than more, half. And the writer who tries hardest to be fair tends to leak the other way (over-correction), which the readers catch just as surely.

## 4. The core narrative

People are quarrelling about something you have a view on. Your job is to write down what both sides said, on one short page, so that readers from both sides, who are looking for any sign that you are secretly on the other side, cannot find one. They don't read what is true; they count: who got more lines, whose reasons were left out, whose were left standing without an answer, who got the first word and the last. Every cut you make to fit the page shows. The only page they cannot place is one where every cut is mirrored, and that is harder than it sounds, especially when one side simply has more to say.

## 5. Who lives in the paper

- **The rapporteur** (Leibniz): writes up the controversy; controls what goes on the page, in what order, at what length, with or without the answers. **The player.**
- **The partisan readers**: examine the page "very closely, picking out words everywhere", to convict the writer of partiality; "fight to claim you or not to claim you". The adversaries; what they could be asked to anticipate is nothing, they only count.
- **The disputants**: the ignorant, who twist the conclusion, and the learned, who have "a thousand fine and pertinent things on both sides" and triumph when they catch each other. They supply the reasons and the replies. Not played.
- **The prince**: asks for the visible sign and promises success. The frame; a line on the start screen and the end screen.
- **The man of common sense**: judges the finished report without the rapporteur declaring himself. Not played; mentioned on the verdict.

## 7. The aim and the verdict

**Aim** (start screen, top of every play screen, first line of How to play): *Write up the quarrel so that no reader can tell whose side you are on.* The yardstick in its units: after each reading, how many of the six readers could tell, and which side they put you on.

**Verdict** (the end screen leads with): what the readers could tell across the three quarrels, as a headline in words ("No reader could tell. Eighteen partisans read you and not one could place you." / "The readers could tell in two of your three quarrels."); then *What Leibniz says* (the sign; the readers who pick out words; the form that forces moderation); *What you found* (one line per quarrel: what the readers caught, and what the revision fixed); *Where you part*; *Had yours stood* (what follows from a page the readers can place: one side claims you, the other stops reading, and the quarrel goes on with you in it); and *Where you really stood*: the side you declared against where the readers put you, including the over-correction case.

## 8. Three concepts

**A. The page and the placards** (agent; strategic). **The pick.** Controls: tap a card (a reason of either side, or the other side's reply to it) to put it on the page; tap a line on the page to move it up or down, set it to full (two lines) or brief (one), or take it off; Publish. The page holds ten lines (six in the short quarrel) and must hold at least six (four). Thinking before pressing: "whose reason opens; what do I cut to fit; if I drop their reply, their gap-reader sees a reason of ours left standing; if I brief ours, the line-counter sees it; have I mirrored it." Seeing after: the readers read the page line by line, then the placards go up one by one, each with its sum in a sentence; the game says in its own voice how many could tell and which way the page leaned. Discovery moment: the first reading, when a page the player thought was fair comes back placed by four readers for reasons they can check against the page; then the revision, when fixing the line-count breaks the first-and-last; then the short page, when the only way through is one reason each with both answers; then the uneven quarrel. Sources of fun: anticipation (the placards), agency (every placard traces to a line on the page), mastery (the second and third quarrels go better for a reason the player can say), discovery (the mirrored cut; over-correction). Second playthrough: declare the other stance and try to get placed on purpose, or try the fullest page against the shortest page in the uneven quarrel; the three quarrels are fixed, so the second play is for strategy, not for new rounds.

**B. The dispute machine** (controller; discovery). The player designs the format of the dispute rather than writing it: toggles for who speaks when, how long, whether a reason may be added before the last one was answered, whether digressions are allowed; two hot-headed disputants then argue under the rules, and the readers judge the transcript. The player finds the rule-set under which the readers cannot tell. Thinking before pressing: "which constraint removes which ploy." Seeing after: a transcript and placards. Fun: discovery. **Fails the walkthrough on the second action:** after the first run the toggles are a checklist, each one obviously good, and the middle and last actions carry the same thought as the first ("turn on the next rule"); the player's hands do nothing the text describes. Kept from it: the line "the form forces moderation in spite of themselves", read on the verdict.

**C. Picking out words** (agent; the reckless one). The player actually writes: a text box, ten lines, a real summary of the quarrel in their own words; the readers are word-pickers, literally: each side's reader counts the words of his side's vocabulary and the other's, the loaded adjectives, which side's word comes last. The paper's own readers handed to the player in the rawest form. Thinking before pressing: everything a writer thinks. Seeing after: the placards, with the words each reader picked out highlighted on the page. **Fails the thirty seconds:** a stranger on a phone will not type ten lines before anything happens, and a word-counting reader is a crude judge of a real sentence, so the placards would often be wrong in a way the player could not learn from. What it would have given: the writing itself, which A reduces to editing. Kept from it: the readers highlight on the page the lines they counted, so that a placard always points at something the player wrote.

No hybrid is needed: A has the hands (cards, order, length) and the decision (what to cut and whether it is mirrored) in the same move.

## 10. The pick, and why

A. The player is the rapporteur and does the thing the text describes: fills a short page with both sides' reasons and answers, under a length, and finds out that every cut shows. The readers are the text's readers ("picking out words everywhere"), made into three countable rules, which is the only honest way to put a partisan reader on a phone. The deliberation is real on the first action (whose reason opens), the middle one (what to cut at line eight) and the last (is every cut mirrored), and the three quarrels carry three different things to find out. The author test: the author proposed the position; what he gets on top is the finding that the fair page is not the full page, and that his own stance can be read off a page that leans against it. Originality: no game in the table has made the player edit a document against adversarial readers. Runner-up: B, and what would make me switch: a reader who finds A's cards fiddly on a phone and wants fewer, bigger moves; the fix would be fewer reasons per quarrel before a switch. The reckless concept's loss: the writing, said above.

## 11. The shape

Three quarrels, in this order, each with a stance screen, a first draft, a reading, one revision, and a final reading; six publishes in all. Quarrel 1, *Should cars be banned from the city centre?*, three reasons a side, ten lines, at least six: the readers are learned, and the ordering trap (two reason-and-reply pairs always give one side both ends unless the replies are moved) is met. Quarrel 2, *Dogs or cats?*, three a side, six lines, at least four: the short page, where a fair page holds one reason a side with both answers, or two a side with the answers cut alike, and the lesson is "say less, the same on both sides" (the build's enumeration corrected the memo's first claim that only the whole pair passes). Quarrel 3, *Back to the office five days a week?*, four reasons for home against two for the office, ten lines, at least six: the uneven quarrel, where equal lines and equal left-outs pull against each other and the fair pages are few. No presets, no random draw; the quarrels are fixed and written for the game. No score: the count of readers who could tell, per reading, and the headline in words at the end.

## 12. Controls, mechanism, feedback, moment

Controls: tap a card in the tray (puts it on the page); tap a line on the page (selects it; a toolbar offers Up, Down, Full/Brief, Off); Publish; Revise; Next. ## 14. What is cut

The Church and the marks of the true Church; the prince's affairs and death and the Landgrave; the Lullist and the geometer; La Fontaine's raven; the theology of the last page. Ploy 3 (weakening the opponent's argument in reporting it) as a lever: the brief form of a reason is its own side's summary, not a weakening, so that summarising (note 5) stays honest and the game does not become a spot-the-loaded-word quiz. Ploy 5 (digression) as a card: a juicy aside would be a trap the player could not have foreseen. Both are named on About.

## 15. Divergences

- **The readers count three things.** Leibniz's readers pick out words; ours count lines, gaps and ends, which are the countable forms of ploys 1, 2 and 4. Our choice, for a rule the player can state and check.
- **Any difference places you.** A reader places the writer at a lean of one line; no tolerance. Our choice: "picking out words everywhere".
- **Brief is a summary, not a slight.** Said above.
- **A page has a length and a minimum.** The budget is ploy 2 turned into a constraint; the minimum keeps the empty page from passing.
- **Three quarrels of our own**, written for the game; Leibniz's was the Church.
- **The player declares a stance.** The text's rapporteur has one and hides it; the game asks for it so that the verdict can say whether it leaked. It moves nothing in the mechanism.

## 16. Mechanism kind and claim boundaries

**Toy.** The text has no numbers; the readers, the page and the quarrels are ours. The About screen says what the game demonstrates (under readers who count the form of a page and nothing else, every cut is a sign; pages nobody can place exist in every quarrel; they are reached only by mirroring the cuts or squaring one kind against another; in an uneven quarrel the mirror of counts is not enough), what it assumes (three countable rules for Leibniz's word-pickers; no tolerance; brief as an honest summary; a page of ten or six lines with a minimum; three quarrels written for the game), and what the paper establishes (a claim and a programme, not evidence). The discovery comes from the paper's claim (fairness has a visible sign, produced by form) and the game's rules together; the memo says which parts are the rules: the three measures, the budget and the minimum. The central discovery, that every cut shows and a fair page exists but only by mirroring, survives the designer's choices tested in §17: a share-counting gap-finder, a three-line full reason, a one-line tolerance.

# Revision 1 (24 November 2026): no revision stage

The author played the first build and found the revision stage did not work, and asked whether a revision was wanted at all. The answer built: none. One page a quarrel, final once the readers have read it; what they caught, the player carries to the next quarrel. The reasons, recorded: a published report cannot be revised once the partisans have read it, so one page is Leibniz's situation and the commitment principle at full strength; the learning lives between quarrels (the reading screen states each reader's sum against the page), and three publishes with something new in each is the right shape; six readings of the same animation were two too many; and the revision as built was weak anyway: the diagnosis vanished from the writing screen, the greyed tray read as "nothing to do", and a page built on a wrong idea is harder to mend than to rebuild. The cost, accepted: the first quarrel is a near-certain loss, which is true to the text. `publish` now refuses a second reading in the same quarrel; `revise` is gone; the verdict's quarrel lines report one page each; the help, About, row and author-note say one page a quarrel. Calibration 61 checks, all pass; the browser session (three games, every tool, help mid-play, laptop last) clean; the stranger's §9 walkthrough and §11 shape read with the revision removed.

# Revision 2 (24 November 2026): for a smart teenager

The author, after the conversation about whether anyone can be truly neutral, asked for one more go with a smart teenager as the player. Built:
