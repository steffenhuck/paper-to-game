The verdict

What Leibniz says

A fair write-up of an argument has one visible sign: no reader can tell whose side the writer is on. Readers who are on a side will comb every word to catch the writer out; some will try to claim him, others to disown him.

The writer who pulls it off is not a judge, not a lawyer for either side, not a peacemaker: just the one who reports. And the form does the work. You cannot hide your side without giving each side its points, with the answers, in an order that is nobody’s own.

What you found
Where you really stood
Where you part
Had your pages stood
What the readers cannot see

They count. They cannot weigh. A page can be even in every count and still unfair in what it chose to say: leave out one side’s weakest point and the other side’s best, and every placard stays grey. No reader here can check that part. It is yours.

How to play

Write up the quarrel so that no reader can tell whose side you are on.

The page

Nine quarrels, three a round; you play three. On the first screen you pick one for each round; tap another in the same round to swap it. Each quarrel is one page: ten ruled lines (six for the short round). Below the page is everything each side said: three or four points a side, each with the other side’s one-line reply.

  • Tap a card to put it on the page. A point takes two lines in full, one in short; it goes on in full if there is room. A reply takes one line and goes on only under its own point.
  • Tap a line on the page to select it. Then Up and Down move it, Short and Full change its length, Off takes it off (a point takes its reply off with it).
  • Every line is in the colour of the side that speaks it: the first side, the second. A reply is spoken by the side that answered.
  • Publish when the page has at least six lines (four on the short page).

The readers

Eight readers read the page, four from each side, and every one is on a side. They do not weigh what is true; they count, and any difference places you:

  • Lines: who speaks more lines.
  • Left out: whose points you left off the page.
  • Unanswered: whose points stand with no answer under them.
  • First & last: who gets the first word and the last. Both the same side, and that side is yours.

Each reader holds up a placard: the colour of the side he places you with, or a grey question mark when he can’t tell. His sentence under the bench is what he counted.

One page a quarrel

Once the readers have read a page it is final: no second go. What they caught, you carry to the next quarrel. Before each quarrel you say where you stand; that changes nothing on the page, and only the verdict will say whether the readers worked you out.

The verdict

How many readers could tell, quarrel by quarrel; what caught you; and where you really stood against where they put you.

What Leibniz says. He tells a prince he has a way of writing up controversies that nobody can object to, and that its visible sign is this: he will write them up “in such a way that the reader cannot judge which party the author has embraced”. Readers trying to convict him of partiality “will examine you very closely, picking out words everywhere”; people “will fight to claim you or not to claim you”, as seven cities fought over Homer. The writer is “neither judge, nor client, nor reconciler, but rapporteur”, the one who reports, and the form “forces people to speak moderately in spite of themselves”. On the way he lists five ploys disputants use without thinking: each chooses his own order; disputes swell into volumes; one conceals or weakens the opponent’s arguments in reporting them; one repeats one’s reasons without taking account of the answers; one digresses. His remedy: an indisputable order, a summary “so that their whole economy can be seen”, every argument unpacked “so that they cannot say that their arguments have been neglected”; then a man of common sense can judge without the rapporteur declaring himself.

The game. You are the one who reports. Nine present-day quarrels in three rounds (phones in school, cars in the city centre, school uniforms; dogs or cats, pineapple on pizza, summer or winter; homework, the office, votes at sixteen); you pick one a round on the first screen; each is one short page, built from what both sides said; eight readers, four a side and every one of them on a side, who count rather than weigh: who speaks more lines (the length, ploy 2), whose points were left out (note 5, the neglected arguments), whose points stand with no answer (ploy 4), and who gets the first word and the last (the order, ploy 1). Any difference places you. One page a quarrel, final once the readers have read it; what they caught, you carry to the next. The verdict says how many readers could tell, and sets where you said you stood against where they put you.

Mechanism: a toy. The text is an argument with no numbers; everything countable here is the game’s. The game demonstrates that, under readers who count the form of a page and nothing else, every cut a writer makes to fit a length is a sign; that pages nobody can place exist in every quarrel and are reached only by mirroring every cut, which on a short page means saying less, the same on both sides, rather than more, half; and that when one side has more to say, a page nobody can place gives it more of the page, because the readers count the points left out, not the share. It assumes four countable rules in place of Leibniz’s readers who pick out words; no tolerance (one line places you); that a point’s short form is its own side’s honest summary and not a weakening (ploy 3 is not a lever here); a page of ten or six lines with a minimum; and nine quarrels written for the game, with three or four points a side and one reply each, three a game, picked by the player. The paper establishes a claim and a programme, not evidence: that such writing is possible, that its sign is the reader’s inability to place the writer, and that the form forces moderation. Nothing in the game is evidence for any of that; the game lets you try to do it. And the readers count what they cannot weigh: two pages that mirror every cut are both fair to them whichever points were chosen, so a page can pass every reader and still be unfair in what it chose to say. The verdict says so.

What is cut. The Church and the marks of the true Church, which the method was to be tried on first; the prince and his death; the Lullist and the geometer; the raven and the nightingale; ploy 3 (weakening the opponent’s reasons in reporting them) as a control, and ploy 5 (digression) as a card. Leibniz feared the two sides would “condemn me by mutual consent, in order to keep themselves in business”; the readers here do not collude, but a page can be placed both ways at once, and the game says so when it happens.

The look. A 2020s protest-print poster: chalk, ink, violet and tangerine, acid for the budget and the reading. The one rule: every line on the page is in the colour of the side that speaks it, and a placard is a block of the same colour, because the readers see colour, not words.
