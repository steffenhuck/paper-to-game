Leibniz told a prince he could write up any argument so fairly that nobody could tell whose side he was on. Readers who were on a side would comb every word to catch him out, and fail.

Three quarrels people still have. You write them up on one short page, and eight readers who are on a side try to catch you.

What Leibniz says

A fair write-up of an argument has one visible sign: no reader can tell whose side the writer is on. Readers who are on a side will comb every word to catch the writer out; some will try to claim him, others to disown him.

The writer who pulls it off is not a judge, not a lawyer for either side, not a peacemaker: just the one who reports. And the form does the work. You cannot hide your side without giving each side its points, with the answers, in an order that is nobody’s own.

What the readers cannot see

They count. They cannot weigh. A page can be even in every count and still unfair in what it chose to say: leave out one side’s weakest point and the other side’s best, and every placard stays grey. No reader here can check that part. It is yours.

How to play

Write up the quarrel so that no reader can tell whose side you are on.

The page

Three quarrels a game, each drawn by chance from two (an even one, a short one, an uneven one), each on one page: ten ruled lines (six for the short one). Below the page is everything each side said: three or four points a side, each with the other side’s one-line reply.

  • Tap a card to put it on the page. A point takes two lines in full, one in short; it goes on in full if there is room. A reply takes one line and goes on only under its own point.
  • Tap a line on the page to select it. Then Up and Down move it, Short and Full change its length, Off takes it off (a point takes its reply off with it).
  • Every line is in the colour of the side that speaks it: the first side, the second. A reply is spoken by the side that answered.
  • Publish when the page has at least six lines (four on the short page).

The readers

Eight readers read the page, four from each side, and every one is on a side. They do not weigh what is true; they count, and any difference places you:

  • Lines: who speaks more lines.
  • Left out: whose points you left off the page.
  • Unanswered: whose points stand with no answer under them.
  • First & last: who gets the first word and the last. Both the same side, and that side is yours.

Each reader holds up a placard: the colour of the side he places you with, or a grey question mark when he can’t tell. His sentence under the bench is what he counted.

One page a quarrel

Once the readers have read a page it is final: no second go. What they caught, you carry to the next quarrel. Before each quarrel you say where you stand; that changes nothing on the page, and only the verdict will say whether the readers worked you out.

The verdict

How many readers could tell, quarrel by quarrel; what caught you; and where you really stood against where they put you.
