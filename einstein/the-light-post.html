How to play
Deliver the flowers on time and still fresh.

Every order is a star with a deadline on the home clock and flowers that keep for a while on their own clock.

Boosters

Each booster makes the rocket go half as fast as light, faster than it was already going. You choose 0 to 6 per rocket. 0 means you refuse the order.

The race

Every rocket races a beam of light launched at the same moment. Yellow is always light. Red is always your rocket.

The two clocks

When the rocket lands, two clocks stop. Home clock: the star's station clock, set to agree with home. Flowers' clock: a clock riding in the rocket with the flowers. The line at the bottom of each dial is the limit. Hand still in the white half: in time, or still fresh. In the grey half: late, or wilted. The yellow tick on the home clock is when the light arrived.

Rounds

  • Test range: one star, as many launches as you like. Each launch adds a row to your log.
  • Order book: four orders, one crate of boosters, all launched together. One order may be impossible: refusing it counts as right.
  • The long haul: one star, 100 light-years away.
About this game

The Light Post

The game in one line

You choose how many boosters each rocket fires. The speeds and the clocks are Einstein's 1905 equations, computed exactly. The orders, the crate and the booster size are this game's.

What the paper found

Einstein started from two rules. The laws of physics are the same for anyone moving steadily. And light always travels at the same speed, whoever sent it. From these alone he showed that clocks that agree for one observer disagree for another who is moving (§1–2). He showed that a moving clock runs slow by the factor √(1 − v²/c²) (§4), and that a clock sent on a journey and brought back lags behind one that stayed. He also showed that speeds combine as (v + w)/(1 + vw/c²) (§5), so two speeds below light's always make one below light's: half and half make four-fifths. Light's speed cannot be reached by adding speeds below it. In the second half of the paper (§10), the energy needed to reach light speed is infinite.

Mechanism: ported

This game demonstrates what the paper's equations say about rockets boosted again and again and about the clocks they carry. In this model, every booster adds half light speed in the rocket's own frame by the §5 rule. A rocket's arrival on the home clock is distance ÷ speed. The far station's clock is set to agree with home's by light signals, the rule of §1. The flowers' time is that times √(1 − v²/c²), from §4.

It assumes boosts that happen instantly, steady flight, no braking (the flowers are dropped in passing), and the booster size of half light speed. The flowers age by their own clock: that is the paper's moving clock applied to something alive. The paper speaks only of clocks, though it says its result holds for a balance-clock at the equator, and footnote 7 rules out only a pendulum clock, whose ticking depends on the Earth.

The paper establishes these relations as consequences of its two principles. That nature obeys them was a matter for later experiment, not for this paper, and certainly not for this game. The game's outcomes are the equations' outcomes, not evidence for them.

What comes from the paper and what from the game. "Two halves make four-fifths", "nothing catches light", and "each booster adds less speed but slows the clock more" all follow from the paper. They hold for any booster size (checked for 0.3c to 0.7c). The order book's puzzles (which order is impossible, and how 11 boosters split) are game mechanics built on those equations.

What is cut

The whole electrodynamical part (§§6–9: magnet and conductor, Doppler shift, aberration, light pressure). Length contraction, which would shorten the rocket but changes nothing you do. The relativity of simultaneity is not played; it is the idea everything else rests on. Acceleration, braking and coming home. The energy cost of speed (§10), which here would grow without limit; boosters are identical stages instead.
