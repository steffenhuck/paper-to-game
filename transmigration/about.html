About this game

The text. G. W. Leibniz, On Unities and Transmigration, c. 1702–1705, manuscript LBr 40 Bl. 22–23, Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz Bibliothek, Hanover. Read in Lloyd Strickland's translation from the French (© 2016), published at leibniz-translations.com; his analysis of the text is "Leibniz vs. transmigration: a previously unpublished text from the early 1700s", Quaestiones Disputatae 7(2), 139–159. No DOI. Game built from this text; the text is the authority where the two disagree.

What the text says

If there were no true unities there would be no multitude; a true unity has no parts, and unities alone are entirely real, since aggregates are formed by thought. Change in things is change in the unities; the variety inside a unity is perception, and these unities are what is called soul in animals, principle of life in living things, and entelechy in every organic body. No unity acts on another, and it is unreasonable to have God run between them, so each unity expresses, by its own nature and from its own point of view, everything that happens outside it; the union of a soul with its body is the spontaneous agreement of their phenomena. The body passes from state to state by mechanical laws and the soul, of itself, from perception to perception, from joy to pain, and Socrates was right that the path between them is short. Souls cannot perish, and neither can organic bodies, which are folded in themselves to infinity, so fire and every other outside force can only ever disturb the outside; the animal remains. We cannot always tell an organic body from a heap: Leibniz cannot decide about the sun, the Earth, or a diamond. Then the question: could some souls pass from body to body? He doubts it could be distinctly explained, so he doubts it is in keeping with order. To take a soul out of a body one would have to destroy the body, and no body can be destroyed. Supposing no bodies made or destroyed, the only transmigration left is an exchange of souls; and either the exchange shows in the bodies or it does not. If it does not, it is contrary to order, since the body must express everything. If it does, we would have to see how: what means are there of expressing the passage of a soul by the laws of mechanics? The exception is minds, souls that can do science, subjects of the City of God, kept by moral laws as well: the mechanical laws themselves could make reborn elsewhere a life that continues my life, an intelligence that claims as its own what has happened to me, so that morally it would be me. That seems possible; but it seems to him more in keeping with order that moral identity is always accompanied by a physical identity.

What the game does with it

A space-age transfer station. A pod is an organic body; its pilot is a unity; its porthole is its point of view, the whole fleet drawn from where the pod is; its log is what mechanics does to it, each line from the line before; the pilot's tape is the same seen from inside, with no wire between the two. The station's book is the rule that the body expresses everything: it writes what the pods show and nothing else. The second book is the moral law for minds. In the first shift you try the text's three ways of moving the dog: burn the pod (a pod under every layer; the fire scorches the outside), swap pilots quietly (every porthole and every log the same, so the book writes nothing, and a swap it cannot write is no swap), swap with a mark (the sign goes into pod B's log, and the log explains it from the line before). In the second shift you are the pilot: a stick attached to nothing, and then the beam, whose one sign that the second book records is a memory. It records the far pilot as, morally, you; and you are still in your pod. Then you book your passage, by beam or by pod, and the verdict says in words what became of you and what Leibniz would have done.

The kind of mechanism, and what the game may claim

Toy. A world built to illustrate the text's argument. Five rules are run as the screen states them: a pod and its pilot never touch and agree; a pod shows everything, and every line of its log follows from the line before; no pod is made or destroyed, and fire scorches the outside; one pilot per pod, where a pod can be told from a heap; a mind's passage is kept in a second book by what the mind claims. Everything else is ours and sits in the config block at the top of the game's source with its reason: the fleet, the dog and the cat, the diamond's missing porthole, the logs and their causes, the three signs, the burn counter's cap, the memory's words, the three ways of booking.

Demonstrates (under this game's assumptions): that with soul and body kept apart and only agreeing, no fire opens a body, no quiet exchange can be written, and any sign of an exchange is the body's own; that a mind continued elsewhere by memory is recorded as the same mind in the moral book while nothing has moved; and that the original is still there, which the text does not say and which follows from its rules (no body destroyed, nothing leaving a unity). The exchange invariant was checked on the configured fleet and on two hundred random fleets with random logs: a supposed swap leaves everything the station can see identical, because a porthole belongs to a position and a log to a pod.

Assumes: that a point of view can be drawn as a porthole and expression as a log; that "the body must express everything" can be run as a book that writes only what pods show; that the moral law can be run as a second book keyed to a memory claim; that an exchange of souls, if it happened, would leave positions and logs as they were (which is what makes it unwritable); the signs, their causes, the dog, the cat, the diamond, the counter, the three bookings, and that two pilots claiming one life is the right picture of a memory-only transfer.

The text establishes: an argument from Leibniz's own principles (unities, expression, pre-established harmony, the infinite fold), with a dilemma about exchange and a hedged exception for minds. No trial, no numbers, and no claim that the original persists when a life is "reborn elsewhere"; that is the game's reading. Nothing in the game is evidence for the text; the game shows what the argument does under our rules.
