# Beam Me Up, Leibniz — design memo

Built 25 November 2026 from Leibniz, "On Unities and Transmigration" (c. 1702–1705), in Lloyd Strickland's translation. The game: a space-age transfer station, chrome and rocket orange on black. Pods drift in the dark, each with a pilot, each with a porthole that shows the whole fleet from where that pod is; under the pad two tapes run in step, the pod's log and the pilot's log, with no wire between them. The aim is to get a pilot out of one pod and into another in a way the station's book will record. Burn the pod and there is a pod inside, all the way down; swap pilots quietly and nothing shows, so the book writes nothing; swap with a mark and the pod's own log explains the mark from the line before. Then you board, a mind, and the one beam that works is a memory: a life rebuilt in the far pod that claims yours. The book records it, and you are still here. Book your passage by beam or by pod.

## 2. What the paper hands over

An **argument** (primary), in two movements. First the metaphysics in eight steps: there are true unities with no parts, and they alone are real; change in things is change in the unities; the variety inside a unity is perception; these unities are what is called soul in animals, principle of life in living things, entelechy in every organic body; no unity acts on another and God does not run between them, so each unity expresses everything from its own point of view, and the union of a soul with its body is "the spontaneous agreement of their phenomena"; the body goes from state to state by mechanical laws and the soul from perception to perception of itself (joy to pain, and Socrates' short path between them); souls cannot perish, and neither can organic bodies, "nature's machines being folded in themselves to infinity", so "fire and other external forces can only ever disturb the outside"; and we cannot always tell an organic body from a heap ("I cannot decide anything about the sun, the globe of the Earth, or a diamond"). Second the question: could some souls pass from body to body? The answer is a dilemma. To take a soul out of a body you would have to destroy the body, and no body can be destroyed. Granting that no bodies are made or destroyed, the only transmigration left is an exchange of souls, and an exchange is either noticeable in the bodies or not: if not, it is contrary to order, "since the body must express everything"; if so, "what means are there of expressing the passage of a soul by the laws of mechanics?" Then the exception: minds, souls that can do science, are also subjects of moral laws, and "the mechanical laws themselves would make reborn elsewhere a life which continues my life, and an intelligence which claims for itself what has happened to me", with memory of the principal things, "so that morally this intelligence would be me". That "seems possible", but it seems to Leibniz "more in keeping with order that moral identity is always accompanied by a physical identity". Secondary: nothing; no model, no estimate. The game formalises the argument as a toy world with five rules (section 12) and lists where the formalisation chose what the text does not (section 15).

## 3. The discovery, and its kind

**A counterintuitive result.** Everybody believes, in 1702 and in 1966, that a soul can leave its body: the Pythagoreans, the East, and every transporter room on television. Leibniz shows that in a world where soul and body never touch and only agree, nothing can leave anything: the body cannot be opened (it has no bottom), a quiet exchange is by definition nothing (what no body shows did not happen), and a loud one is only the body doing what it was going to do. The twist at the end is the second discovery, and the one the stranger takes home: the only "transmigration" the text allows is a life somewhere else that remembers mine, which is morally me and has moved nothing at all; and even that Leibniz would rather came with the body. The beam that works is the one in which nobody travels.

## 4. The core narrative

Imagine every living thing is a little ship with a pilot inside, and the pilot's window shows the whole sky from where the ship is. The pilot and the ship never touch: there is no wire, no stick, nothing. They just agree, like two clocks set to the same time. People say that when a ship is wrecked the pilot flies off into another ship. Leibniz says: try it. Set fire to the ship and you find a smaller ship inside it, and a smaller one inside that, and it never ends, so the fire only ever scorches the paint. Try swapping two pilots on the quiet: every window still shows the same sky, every log still says the same things, so there is nothing to write down, and what nobody can write down did not happen. Try swapping them with a bang: the ship's log says the bang came from the ship's own engine, and it would have. Then he tries the one thing that might work: if a ship far away started to remember your life, that pilot would, as far as right and wrong go, be you. He thinks that is possible. He would still rather you and your ship went together.

## 5. Who lives in the paper

- **The unity, the soul, the pilot**: perceives everything from its point of view, passes of itself from one perception to the next; nothing comes into it from outside. Its "control" is nothing external, which is the reckless concept (section 8, B).
- **The organic body, the pod**: a natural machine folded to infinity, indestructible, expressing everything, including its soul's future by its own. Could be the player's object to build; cut (section 14).
- **Fire and other external forces**: the thing that tries to open a body. One of the player's three keys.
- **"We"**, who would have to see an indication of a soul's passage, and who ask "what means are there": the one who tries the exchange and reads the body for a sign. This is the player: the operator at the station.
- **Minds**, including Leibniz's "me": the exception. The player, in the second shift.
- **God** (the supreme intelligence, the monarch of the City of God): the keeper of moral law. The station's second book.
- **The sun, the Earth, a diamond**: masses that may or may not be organic. A pod in the fleet with no porthole to be found.

## 7. The aim and the verdict

**Aim**, as the player reads it: *Get a pilot out of one pod and into another, and get the station's book to record it.*

**Yardstick** during play, in the aim's units: after every try, the book's line for the pilot, in words: where the book says the pilot is, and what it wrote about the try ("Pod A: outside scorched. Pilot: the dog, as before."). No count.

**Verdict**, the lines the end screen leads with:
- What the paper found: no soul leaves its body, because there is no body to open (a pod is pods all the way down) and no exchange to record (a swap that shows nothing is nothing, and whatever a swap shows, the body's own log explains); a mind can be continued somewhere else by a life that remembers it, and morally that life is the mind, but it moves nothing; and it is more in keeping with order that a mind and its body go together.
- What you found: how deep you burnt and what was there; which pod you swapped with and what the book wrote; which marks you tried and what the log said caused them; what the memory did, and how many pilots then claimed your life; how you booked your passage.
- Where you part: by beam, you took the road Leibniz calls possible and would not take; by pod, you went as he would; by beam and burn, you tried to leave one of you behind and met the first rule again.
- What would have followed had yours stood: if the quiet swap had been written, the book would record what nothing shows, and could then record anything; if the lamp had counted as a pilot, every lamp on every pod would be a pilot arriving; if the beam is you, two of you now claim one life, and the station must decide whose promise a promise is.

## 8. Three concepts

**A. The transfer station (dilemma, discovery).** The player is the operator at a space-age transfer station: pods with pilots, each pod's porthole showing the fleet from there, twin tapes under the pad. Three keys, the paper's own cases: Burn (a depth dial), Swap quietly (a choice of pod to swap with, among a cat's pod, a fern's pod and a diamond), Swap with a mark (a choice of the sign the far pod shall show: a lamp, a roll, a change of course). Every try plays out on the pad one step at a time and ends with the book's line. Then the second shift: the player boards a pod as a mind, and the mark menu gains a fourth sign, a memory; the far pod's pilot claims the player's life, the station's second book records it, and the player's own pod still has the player in it. Last: book your passage by beam, by pod, or by beam and burning the one left behind. Deliberation: before the first key, which of three ways the universe would accept; at the dial, whether there is a bottom; at the mark, which sign a log could not explain; at the memory, whether a claim is a passage; at the booking, whether a life that remembers mine is me. After: the layers peel with the pilot in every one; the windows after the swap, unchanged; the arrow from the mark to its cause; the two pilots who both say "I was in pod A". Discovery moment: the beam works, the book writes it, and the player is still in pod A. Fun: anticipation (the fire counter, the swap's reveal), agency (every key answers with a different line of the book), discovery (the memory, and the duplicate), and a little mastery (the second shift is played knowing the three rules; the player goes straight to the memory). Second playthrough: the other booking, and the diamond; the verdict differs by what was tried.

**B. The windowless stick (subject, discovery). The reckless one.** The player is the pilot. One pod, one stick, no words. Push the stick and the pod turns; the window shows the fleet turning; a line of the tape says the thruster fired on its schedule. Then the player is told: the stick is attached to nothing. Pull the stick out (it comes out in the hand) and keep flying: the pod still turns when you want it to, because your wanting and its turning were set to agree. Then you are swapped into another pod on the quiet: the stick does nothing new, the window shows the same sky, your tape runs on; where are you? Where your window is. Walkthrough: the first minute is the best minute in any of the three (the stick comes out and the pod still answers). The second minute is the quiet swap from inside, which lands harder than A's version. Then there is nothing left to do: the pilot cannot burn, cannot mark, cannot book; the paper's dilemma is seen from the one position that cannot act in it. Hands for a minute, then no decision. It loses, and what it gives up: the wordless screen and the stick. The stick is kept: the second shift opens with it (the hybrid, below).

**C. The inspector (experimenter, discovery).** Claims of transmigration come to the station's inspector: "the pilot of pod 4 is now in pod 7". The player reads the pods' logs (every line with its cause), the windows and, for minds, the testimony, and rules on each claim: happened, did not, cannot say. The finding is that mechanics alone never certifies a passage and testimony certifies a moral one. Deliberation real (reading a log for a line without a cause), the paper's "what indication will we have" operated. Loses on the author test: the author would rather try the exchange than audit one, and the player never touches a key; the discovery is a reading, not a doing. Kept from it: the inspector's arrow, drawn on the tape from each mark to its cause.

**The hybrid** (A's decisions carried by B's hands): the second shift opens in the pilot's seat with the stick, thirty seconds of flying a pod that is attached to nothing, so that the player meets the harmony from inside before trying to leave it; then the console. Built.

## 10. The pick, and why

A. The keys are the paper's own cases, in the paper's own order; the porthole is the paper's own rule drawn (each unity expresses everything from its point of view), the tapes are the harmony drawn, and the book is the paper's own instrument ("the body must express everything") in the player's hands. The decisive deliberations have live branches the text names: how deep (the fold to infinity), which pod (the diamond), which mark (lamp, roll, course: "what means are there"), which sign for a mind (the memory: "an intelligence which claims for itself what has happened to me"), and how to go (possible, or in keeping with order). The author test: the author knows the answer and still gets to turn the dial, pick the mark, and meet the duplicate, which the text implies and never states. Originality: no game in the table has had every attempt fail by design and then let one succeed by moving nothing; none has drawn a perception as a porthole. Runner-up: B, and what would make me switch: a reader who finds the console a sequence of demonstrations, in which case the fix is to make the stick the whole first shift and the keys the second. What the reckless concept would have given: the wordless screen and the stick that comes out; the stick is kept.

## 11. The shape

Two shifts and a verdict; seven to nine actions, each with one new thing to find out: (1) a pod has no bottom (one burn, a second allowed); (2) a quiet swap writes nothing, and the dog's window does not move (one swap, a second pod allowed, the diamond its own line); (3) a mark is the pod's own (one mark, a second allowed, then the key dims with the rule); (4) the stick is attached to nothing (free, not counted); (5) a memory is recorded, in a second book, and the original is still here (one beam); (6) the booking (one). Presets are the text's cases in the text's order: destruction, unnoticed exchange, noticed exchange, minds; the dog before the mind because the text's exception is for minds only. No score; a verdict in words. Five minutes.

## 12. Controls, mechanism, feedback, moment

**Controls.** Shift 1: three keys. BURN with a depth dial (1, 10, 100, all the way); SWAP QUIETLY with a pod to pick (the cat's pod, the fern's pod, the diamond); SWAP WITH A MARK with a sign to pick (lamp, roll, new course). Each key opens its control, with a preview on the pad (the shells to be burnt; the pod to swap with lit; the sign drawn dotted on pod B), and a key that commits (FIRE, SWAP). Shift 2: the stick (drag; no commit), then BEAM with a sign to pick (lamp, roll, a memory), then BOOK PASSAGE with three ways.

**Mechanism** (a toy, section 16). A world of pods with positions; each pod's log is a schedule of mechanical lines (roll, warm, cool, lamp, course) each with a cause in an earlier line of the same log (timer, thruster, heater); each pilot's tape is its pod's log seen from inside (roll → the fleet turns the other way; warm → glad; cool → pain, Socrates' short path), with nothing from outside. Five rules, each a sentence on screen: (1) a pod and its pilot never touch; they agree; (2) a pod shows everything: its window is the fleet from here, its log is everything that happens to it, and each line follows from the line before; (3) no pod is ever made and none destroyed; a pod is pods all the way down; fire scorches the outside; (4) one pilot per pod, and every pod has one, where a pod can be told from a heap; (5) a mind's passage is kept in a second book, by what the mind claims and remembers. Burn(depth): peels depth shells; returns a pod with the same pilot and window; the counter is capped at a million and the station stops counting. Swap(target): permutes pilots; returns windows and logs identical to before (an invariant the check proves bit for bit), so the book writes nothing; the diamond returns "no porthole found". Mark(sign): inserts the sign in B's log with its cause; the book writes the sign and its cause. Beam(memory): inserts the claim in B's log with its cause (B's mechanism) and writes the moral book; A's pilot still claims the same life (count of claimants = 2). Book(way): beam → two claimants, both recorded; pod → one, the moral and the physical together; beam and burn → two, and the burn's result again.

**Feedback.** Every try plays as steps, one thing and one plain sentence each, with Next: the shells peeling with the counter; the silhouettes crossing; the windows before and after, side by side; the sign on pod B and the arrow on the tape to its cause; the far pilot's speech bubble and the second book sliding open; pod A's pilot speaking the same words. Then the game's voice says what the try was, what came of it, what the book wrote and what that means; then Leibniz's sentence, in Strickland's words, as colour.

**Moment.** The second book writes "morally, B's pilot is A's", and the pad pans to pod A, where you still are.

## 14. What is cut

Sleep (perceptions too faint for memory; "no eternal sleep"); the crystallisation of mixed salts; the dominant monad and the hierarchy of unities within a body; the deleted passages; the sun and the Earth (the diamond stands for all three); the City of God as anything but the keeper of the second book; the body expressing the future state of its soul (used only as the arrow on the tape: the log's next line is in its last); the question whether the same intelligence could pass "otherwise than by mechanical laws" (a deleted clause); building a pod; any pilot dying, since in the text nothing does.

## 15. Divergences

- **The station, the pods, the book.** Ours: a 1960s frame for a 1702 argument. A pod is an organic body, a pilot a unity, a porthole a point of view, the book the principle that the body expresses everything, the second book the moral law. The About screen says so.
- **The dog and the cat.** The text says "soul in animals". Ours, in the space-age manner (a dog went up in 1957, a cat in 1963).
- **The diamond as a pod with no porthole.** The text says it cannot decide whether a diamond is organic; the station's "cannot tell whether a pod or a heap" is that sentence.
- **The marks.** Lamp, roll, course: ours, as the mechanical means the text asks for and does not list. Their causes (timer, thruster, schedule) are ours.
- **The counter.** Layers are counted and capped at a million; the text says infinity. Ours.
- **The memory's content.** "The roll at 3:12 and the warmth at 3:13": ours, as "the principal things that have happened to me".
- **The duplicate.** The text does not say the original remains; it follows from its rules (no body destroyed; nothing leaves a unity) and the game shows it. Declared on About as our reading.
- **The booking.** Beam, pod, beam-and-burn: ours, as the text's "possible" against "more in keeping with order", with the third as the player's own likely wish, met by the first rule.
- **The clock.** Times on the tapes (3:12 and so on) are ours.

## 16. Mechanism kind and claim boundaries

**Toy.** A world built to illustrate the text's argument, with five rules run as the screen states them (section 12) and everything else chosen by us and listed in CONFIG. The About screen says what the game **demonstrates** (under this game's assumptions: with soul and body kept apart and only agreeing, no fire opens a body, no quiet exchange can be written, and any sign of an exchange is the body's own; a mind continued elsewhere by memory is recorded as the same mind in the moral book while nothing has moved; the original is still there), what it **assumes** (that a point of view can be drawn as a porthole and expression as a log; that "the body must express everything" can be run as a book that writes only what pods show; that the moral law can be run as a second book keyed to a memory claim; that an exchange, if it happened, would leave positions and logs as they were; the signs, the dog, the cat, the diamond, the counter, the bookings; and that two pilots claiming one life is the right picture of a memory-only transfer), and what the text **establishes** (an argument from Leibniz's principles with a dilemma and a hedged exception; no trial, no numbers, no claim that the original persists). Every discovery the game stages follows from the text's rules as formalised, not from a score or a budget: there is no score, and the one designer's constraint (the shift ends only when all three keys are tried) decides nothing about any outcome. The duplicate is the one place the game goes past the text, and it is declared as our reading on About and in the author-note.

**Robustness.** The central discovery (a swap cannot show) rests on the formalisation that a porthole belongs to a position and a log to a pod. The calibration re-runs the exchange invariant on two hundred random fleets with random positions, random portholes and random logs; it holds on every pair. It would fail only if a pilot's name were made part of what the station reads, which is the one thing the text's rule forbids (the station reads bodies). The mark discovery (every sign has a cause) is enforced by construction and checked after every mark, including the memory.
