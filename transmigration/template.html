Transfer station · a game from Leibniz, c. 1702

Beam Me Up, Leibniz

Three hundred years ago, Leibniz asked: could you leave your body and move into another one?

This station is going to try. You are the pilot in the pod on the left. The station will try three ways to get you to the far station. After each one, it asks: which pod are you in now? You tap a pod. Then Leibniz answers.

Get to the far station. After every try, tap the pod you are in.

How to play

You are the pilot of the pod on the left. You want to be at the far station, where the far pod is. It has a pilot too. All pilots look the same.

The station tries three ways to get you there. Each time, do what the screen says (tap your pod, press a key), watch, and then tap the pod you think you are in. Then Leibniz tells you where you really are.

  • Get you out. Tap your pod to burn off a layer. See what is underneath.
  • Swap. You and the far pilot change places. Then pick a sign so everyone can see you arrived.
  • Beam. Everything you remember is sent into the far pilot's head.

At the end you choose how to travel on: by beam or by pod. No points. The verdict says where you and Leibniz agreed. About three minutes.

About this game

Built from two manuscript pages Leibniz wrote around 1702–1705 and never published, On Unities and Transmigration, read in Lloyd Strickland's translation. The old question: can a soul move from one body to another? Leibniz says no, three times, and then allows one thing.

The three tries, in his words

  • Get you out. A living body is a machine made of smaller machines, with no end; fire and other forces only ever disturb the outside. So no body can be broken open, and no soul lifted out.
  • Swap. If two souls changed bodies and nothing in either body showed it, that would be against the order of things, because a body must express everything that happens to it. And if a body did show it, what could the sign be? Every state of a body follows from the one before by the laws of mechanics; there is no room in it for a sign that means "a soul has arrived." The lamp and its timer are the game's picture of that.
  • Beam. For a mind (a soul that can reason and remember, not a dog's), the laws of mechanics could build somewhere else a life that continues mine and claims what happened to me as its own. Morally, that would be me. Leibniz thinks this possible, and would rather identity came with the body.

What is ours

The station, the pods, the lamp and its timer, the clock times, and the two of you at the end. Leibniz does not say the original is still there after the "rebirth elsewhere"; it follows from his own rules (no body is destroyed, nothing leaves a soul), and the game shows it. The question "which pod are you in?" and the booking are the game's; Leibniz's answers are the station's.
