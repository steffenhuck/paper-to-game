How to play

You are the pilot of your pod. You want to be at the far station, where the far pod is, with its own pilot.

The station tries four ways to get you there, one after the other. Press the key, watch what happens, press Next. Then the station asks: where are you now? Pick an answer. Then Leibniz gives his.

  • Move. Lift you out and carry you over. The pod has no door, so the station burns it open.
  • Swap. You and the far pilot change places.
  • Swap with a sign. The same, but the far pod flashes its lamp as you arrive.
  • Beam. Everything about you is sent to the far pod.

At the end you book your passage on to the next station, by beam or by pod. No score; the verdict says where you and Leibniz agreed. Three minutes.

About this game

Built from two manuscript pages Leibniz wrote around 1702–1705 and never published, On Unities and Transmigration, read in Lloyd Strickland's translation. The old question: can a soul move from one body to another? Leibniz says no, three times, and then allows one thing.

The four tries, in his words

  • Burn. A living body is a machine made of smaller machines, with no end; fire and other forces only ever disturb the outside. So no body can be broken open, and no soul lifted out.
  • Swap. If two souls changed bodies and nothing in either body showed it, that would be against the order of things, because a body must express everything that happens to it.
  • Swap with a sign. And if a body did show it, what could the sign be? Every state of a body follows from the one before by the laws of mechanics. There is no room in it for a sign that means "a soul has arrived."
  • Beam. For a mind (a soul that can reason and remember, not a dog's), the laws of mechanics could build somewhere else a life that continues mine and claims what happened to me as its own. Morally, that would be me. Leibniz thinks this possible, and would rather identity came with the body.

What is ours

The station, the pods, the lamp and its timer, the clock times, and the two of you at the end. Leibniz does not say the original is still there after the "rebirth elsewhere"; it follows from his own rules (no body is destroyed, nothing leaves a soul), and the game shows it. The question "where are you now?" and the booking are the game's; Leibniz's answers are the station's.
