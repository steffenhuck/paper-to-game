Beam Me Up, Leibniz

You sit in a pod. Every pod has a pilot, and a porthole that shows the other pods from where it is. You want to be at the far station. The station has a beam.

How to play

The aim: get yourself to the far station, and say who arrived.

What is on the pad

Your pod, with you in it, and the far pod at the far station. A few other pods drift about. Every pod has a porthole, and in the porthole the other pods are drawn from where that pod is. The rings round your pod are its layers. The amber strip is the station's book: it writes what the pods show, and nothing else.

The three steps

  • The beam. Press Beam and watch. Then answer one question: who is at the far station?
  • The fix. Two keys. Burn: fire on your pod, layer by layer; press Burn deeper if you like. Move you: you into the far pod, the far pilot into yours; first say whether the far pod shall show a sign when you arrive. Each try plays out one step at a time; press Next.
  • The booking. Pick how you go on to the next station: by beam or by pod. Neither is wrong. The verdict says what became of you.

No score. Three or four minutes.

About this game

Built from a two-page manuscript Leibniz wrote around 1702–1705 and never published, On Unities and Transmigration, read in Lloyd Strickland's translation. The question of the text is an old one: can a soul move from one body to another? Leibniz's answer is no, in three steps, and then one exception.

What the pieces stand for

  • A pod is a living body. Leibniz: a body is a machine folded in on itself to infinity, so nothing from outside can ever get past its surface. That is the burn.
  • A pilot is a soul. Nothing comes into it from outside and nothing goes out. It perceives everything from where its body is: the porthole.
  • The pod's log is the laws of mechanics: every state of a body follows from the one before, so nothing a body does needs a soul to explain it. That is the lamp.
  • The station's book is Leibniz's rule that a body must show everything that happens to it. A swap that shows nowhere is, by that rule, no swap.
  • The beam is the exception, which the text allows for minds only (souls that can remember and claim; not a dog's). The laws of mechanics could build, somewhere else, a life that continues yours and claims it; morally, Leibniz says, that would be you. He thinks it possible, and would rather identity came with the body.

What is ours

The station, the beam, the lamp, the timer, the clock times, and the two of you at the end. The text says a life "reborn elsewhere" that remembers mine would morally be me; it does not say the original is still there. It follows from the text's own rules (no body is destroyed, nothing leaves a soul), and the game shows it. The question after the beam, and the booking, are the game's, not the text's; Leibniz's own answer is in the verdict.
