About

The paper. Andrej Angelovski, Jordi Brandts and Werner Güth (2026), Bidding for better jobs: an experiment on gender differences in competitiveness without a real-effort task, Theory and Decision, doi:10.1007/s11238-026-10132-9. Open access. Game built from this paper; the paper is the authority where the two disagree.

What the paper found

Most lab evidence that women compete less comes from one kind of experiment: a few minutes of a task, winner takes all. The authors ask whether that is competing as a career knows it, where getting ahead means spending money, time and effort to secure a rank. So they made competing a sealed bid: 168 students in Rome bid, 32 times each, for a top job and two middle jobs in a flat firm (350 / 200 / 200, default 50) and a steep one (450 / 150 / 150, default 50), first inside groups of four and then across eight. The bids of women and men did not differ significantly; women bid a few points higher for the top of the flat firm. Women ended up in the top job more often than their share. Nobody earned more for being at the top: the middle jobs paid better. Their conclusion is not that women are more competitive, but that what you infer about gender and competition depends on how you measure competing, and that neither way is the right one.

The game

You are the experimenter. The paper's whole point is a design decision its authors made, so the game hands you that decision as a switch: what competing means on the students' screen (a race at sums, or a price for the job) and what they can win (one winner, a flat ladder, a steep ladder). Eight sessions of 24 students; two bars each; a notebook; a sentence back to the journalist. The students never change between sessions, so whatever moves in the bars was moved by the apparatus.

What is under the hood

The price apparatus is reconstructed from the paper. Each student's bid for a rung is the rational benchmark (the salary less the 50 everyone gets anyway) plus a deviation drawn to match the paper's Figure 4 and Table 1 (bids for the top about 40 ECU under the benchmark, women about 14 higher in the flat firm; bids for the middle near the benchmark), with the bunching at the benchmark the paper's appendix describes. The paper's own allocation rules then run: the top job to the highest bidder at the second-highest bid, the middle jobs to the next two at the third's. The reconstruction reproduces, to within the tolerances in the calibration file, the paper's bid deviations, the share of top jobs won by women, the earnings by position and the absence of a gender difference in earnings.

The sums apparatus is reconstructed from the literature the paper argues with, as the paper's introduction and section 2 describe it: Niederle and Vesterlund's 2007 lab, where about three in four men and one in three women chose the tournament, men and women performed alike, and the gap went with beliefs about one's own rank and attitudes to risk. Each student here has an ability, a private view of their standing (men's more flattering), an aversion to a gamble (women's stronger), and a taste for the top that does not differ by gender, which is the paper's finding. A student competes when the prize they expect under their own view, less their aversion times the spread, beats the sure alternative. None of these numbers is the paper's data.

Two cells are ours alone. The paper never ran a sums contest with its prize ladders; those cells cross the two switches so the player can test either on its own. In this model a ladder barely moves the gap, because a view of one's own rank still decides who competes. And the one-job price cell is a second-price auction for a single job, with no gender difference, as the auction literature the paper cites reports for that rule.

Claim boundaries

  • What the game demonstrates: that the same students, measured two ways, can show a large gap one way and none the other; and that in the price apparatus women reach the top at least as often and nobody earns more for it.
  • What the game assumes: that the sums gap comes from confidence and nerve rather than from wanting the top (the decomposition is ours; the central contrast survives either extreme, as the calibration file shows); the shape of every individual-level distribution; that a session is one shot, not 32 rounds; two men and two women in every group; the words that turn a difference into "about the same" or "about twice as often".
  • What the paper establishes: the bid, top-job and earnings patterns of its own 168 students in its own design, and the argument that inferences about gender and competitiveness are sensitive to the measurement context. It does not establish why the real-effort gap arises, and it says its results have nothing to say about discrimination or other obstacles on the demand side.

Divergences from the paper

  • The email, the budget and the eight sessions are ours. The session cost is the paper's average earnings of about 12 euro times 24, rounded.
  • One session is six groups of four, one period; the paper's steady-state bids (from period 4 on) are the target.
  • Phase 2 of the paper, with eight bidders across both firms, is not played; its results (lower earnings for everyone, women more often on top) appear in the grey line only.
  • The sums bar counts who chose to compete; the price bar is a bid as a share of the rung's worth. They sit on the same scale with different captions, and never measured the same thing, which is the paper's point.
