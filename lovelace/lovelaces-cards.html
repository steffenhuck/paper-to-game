In 1843 Ada Lovelace wrote out, card by card, how a machine of wheels and punched cards could work out long sums on its own. The machine was never built. Here it is.

The Engine does nothing until you hang cards on it. Then it does exactly what the cards say, one card at a time. If it prints the right answer, a bell rings.

You will make cards, hang them, set the Engine going, and listen for the bell. Then the Engine gets new numbers and runs your cards again.

Hang the cards that make the Engine print what is wanted, for any numbers it is handed.

First a short tour that shows how, then three jobs from her Notes. About eight minutes.

The Countess's own chain

Her Notes end with a much bigger job: the Numbers of Bernoulli, which mathematicians of her day needed and found hard to work out. Her chain for them has a BACK loop inside another loop. Here it runs on this Engine. You hang nothing; you watch.

If the rules of musical notes could be written this way, she wrote, the Engine might compose elaborate and scientific pieces of music of any length.

Lovelace's CardsHow to play

Hang the cards that make the Engine print what is wanted, for any numbers it is handed.

The Engine

The brown box holds columns of number wheels. Each column holds one number and has a short name under it, like a or n. R is an empty column for answers. 1 is a column that always holds 1, which is handy for counting down.

The paper strip on the right shows what is wanted, in pale ink. What the Engine prints appears next to it.

Making a card

A card tells the Engine to do one thing. Tap a sign on the blank card, then tap columns:

  • + − × ÷: tap three columns. The first and second are the numbers to use; the third is where the answer goes. a × n → R means “multiply a by n and put the answer on R”. The same column can be used twice: R × x → R means “multiply R by x and put the answer back on R”.
  • P (print): tap one column. The Engine writes its number on the strip.
  • ↺ (back): choose a number, then tap a column. The Engine goes back that many cards and does them again, as long as that column is not 0. When it reaches 0, the Engine carries on. BACK 2 WHILE n ≠ 0 repeats the two cards above it until n is 0.

The card being made shows three slots: 1st, 2nd and ans. The pink slot is the one your next column tap fills. To change one, tap that slot, then tap a column. Under the card, a line says in words what it will do. Tap Hang the card to put it on the chain. Tap a hung card to take it off again.

Setting it going

The Engine does your cards from top to bottom. A line under the Engine says what each card just did. If the strip matches what was wanted, the bell rings. Then the Engine gets new numbers and runs the same cards again. If the bell rings every time, your cards have held.

If it goes wrong, change the cards and try again. After two tries you can ask to see the Countess's cards.

Three jobs

Multiply three numbers. Double a number again and again. Print the square numbers using only adding. All three come from her Notes.

Lovelace's CardsAbout

The game in one line

An Engine that does exactly what its cards say and nothing else; you make out the cards for three of Lovelace's own examples, and the Engine then runs them on numbers you never saw. A chain that names columns rather than numbers holds for every n; a card that turns the prism back makes a handful of cards do the work of hundreds; and the Engine originates none of it.

What the paper does

In 1842 L. F. Menabrea published a sketch of Charles Babbage's Analytical Engine; in 1843 Ada Lovelace translated it and added seven Notes, longer than the sketch, which are the text this game is built from. Note A separates operations from the things operated on and says the Engine weaves algebraical patterns just as the Jacquard-loom weaves flowers and leaves; the Difference Engine before it did one fixed thing, six additions. Note B draws the Store: columns of figure-wheels, a sign above, a name beneath, and works axn, a·n·x, a/n·x and a+n+x with a = 5, n = 7, x = 98. Note C explains backing: the prism over which the cards pass can revolve backwards so that a card or set of cards does duty again. Note D works two linear equations in eleven operations with three operation cards. Note E names the cycle, and the cycle of cycles. Note F shows three cards doing the work of three hundred and thirty. Note G computes the Numbers of Bernoulli with twenty-five operation cards, "and no more are needed, however great n may be"; and warns that the Engine "has no pretensions whatever to originate anything. It can do whatever we know how to order it to perform."

Mechanism: ported

This game demonstrates the card language of the Memoir and the Notes, run: a Store of columns; cards that put the mill into one of its four states and name the columns that supply it and receive from it; a card that turns the prism back while a column is not nothing, which is Lovelace's backing joined to her Operation 7's feel for n; a bell when the chain ends, and an early bell on a division by nothing (Memoir p. 12). The same interpreter runs Note B's examples with Note B's data, Menabrea's Table 1.3 and Note D's eleven operations against the solutions of their equations, Table 1.1's squares by differences, and the Countess's Bernoulli chain, which prints 1/6, −1/30, 1/42, −1/30 with nine, fourteen and twenty-five operation cards (the calibration check, in the repository).

It assumes our simplifications: one card carries both the operation and its columns, where Lovelace keeps Operation-cards and Variable-cards apart; one contingent card, backward only; constants inscribed on columns at the start instead of cards of numbers; a rack of ten cards and an attendant who stops an Engine after four hundred steps; our data for tasks two and three (a = 3, x = 2, n = 5; the squares to six) so that six figure-wheels suffice; the hidden tests; and two demonstrations of our own, the tour (Note B's numbers a = 5, n = 7, added in two cards, the second using the first card's answer) and a countdown that shows the BACK card at work, plus a plain sentence under every card and a narration of each step, so that the language can be learned by watching before it is used. The card count as the yardstick is the paper's own (Notes D, F, G), the three tasks and the tests are ours. In the coda, Operation 4 divides as the result column requires rather than as the table's symbol column misprints, Operation 24 subtracts so that the printed Number carries the sign formula (8) gives, and the cycle 13–23 is written out for the fourth Number because these cards cannot step a column forward on each pass, as Lovelace's Operation 21 does.

The paper establishes the design of a general engine and what such an engine could do if built: that operations and quantities are separable, that cards can be cycled, that a finite chain computes an unbounded table, that the Numbers of Bernoulli follow from twenty-five cards; and its own limits, that the Engine follows analysis and does not anticipate it. The Engine was never built. Nothing here is evidence about the machine; it is the Notes' language, running.

What is cut

Operation-cards distinct from Variable-cards and the economy of Note D; Retaining and Zero Supply-cards; cards of numbers; the sign discs; forty-digit columns; the symbolic results of Notes A and B; the trigonometric series of Note E; Note F's elimination; the Bernoulli numbers as a task rather than a coda; the stepping column of Operation 21.
