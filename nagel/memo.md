# Zoom Out with Nagel — design memo

Built 19 November 2026 from Thomas Nagel, "What Is It Like to Be a Bat?" (1974). The game: a camera whose zoom ring steps back from your own eyes, three stops, and six things to get across to six readers who do not share your senses. Zoom out on lightning and the whole of it comes into the frame. Zoom out on pain and the pain leaves the frame while your brain fills it. The last round turns the camera round: the bat sends, you receive, and the frame you cannot read is the one you have to judge.

## 0. Given

Mine: signature and counter. Author title: *Zoom Out with Nagel* (what the player does, with the name after it). No ideas given; every choice below is the engine's.

## 1. Paper

Thomas Nagel, "What Is It Like to Be a Bat?", *The Philosophical Review* 83(4), October 1974, pp. 435–450. DOI 10.2307/2183914. Read online at https://www.jstor.org/stable/2183914. Available: the JSTOR scan of the typeset article, sixteen pages, no code, no data; a paper with no numbers in it. Page numbers below are the journal's.

## 2. What the paper hands over

**An argument**, and only that. The game formalises it as an explicit toy (build.md §1) and lists where the formalisation chose something the paper did not (§15 below). The verbal mechanism the toy encodes, in Nagel's own stages:

- Experience has a subjective character: there is something it is like to be the organism, for the organism (p. 436).
- A point of view is a type, not a private possession; one can take up another's point of view to the extent that one is similar to them, and "the more different from oneself the other experiencer is, the less success one can expect" (pp. 441–442, fn. 8: the distance "can fall anywhere on a continuum").
- Objectivity "is a direction in which the understanding can travel": away from the particular viewpoint, toward what can be apprehended from many (pp. 443–444). For lightning, sound, the rainbow, travelling that way brings you closer to the thing, and the appearance is explained as an effect on observers (pp. 437, 443, 445).
- For experience the direction is reversed: "any shift to greater objectivity … does not take us nearer to the real nature of the phenomenon: it takes us farther away from it" (pp. 444–445). "What would be left of what it was like to be a bat if one removed the viewpoint of the bat?" (p. 443).
- A Martian scientist with no vision could understand lightning in full and never the human concept of it (p. 443); a Martian physiologist could observe every physical process in my brain and find no trace of my experience of the Mona Lisa (fn. 10, p. 444); intelligent bats or Martians trying to conceive of us "would be wrong to conclude that there is not anything precise that it is like to be us" (p. 440).
- This does not show physicalism false; it shows we have no conception of how it could be true: "matter is energy" said to a pre-Socratic, the caterpillar locked in the safe (pp. 446–448).
- The proposal: an objective phenomenology, describing the structural features of experience "in a form comprehensible to beings incapable of having those experiences": to a person blind from birth, something of what seeing is like; "one would reach a blank wall eventually" (p. 449). Loose cross-sense analogies ("red is like the sound of a trumpet") are of no use.

## 3. The discovery, and its kind

**A counterintuitive result.** The field (the "reductionist euphoria" of p. 435) and the science-minded public believe that the way to understand anything better is to describe it more objectively: leave your own vantage point, measure, generalise. Nagel shows that for one class of thing, experience, that move does the opposite: every step toward objectivity leaves the thing behind, because the thing just is how it is from a point of view. The game lets the player act on the first belief and meet the second. Underneath it, **a mechanism made intelligible**: objectivity as a direction, and the reversal of its sign for experience.

## 4. The core narrative

You have a camera with a zoom ring. At the near end it photographs the world exactly as you see it; turn the ring and it steps back from your eyes, until at the far end it photographs what any instrument would find, with no eyes in it at all. Some friends want to know about things, and some of them do not have your eyes or your ears, so you have to zoom out before they can read your picture. For a thunderstorm this works perfectly: the further you zoom out, the more of the storm is in the picture. Then a friend who has never seen asks what red is like. You zoom out so that she can read the picture, and when you look at it, the red has gone. It was only ever in the near end. At the end a bat sends you a picture of its own world, and the near end of its ring is one you cannot read at all. Is there nothing in it, or something you cannot see?

## 5. Who lives in the paper

- **The describer** ("we", who "describe it not in terms of the impressions it makes on our senses, but in terms of its more general effects", p. 444). Controls the distance from the viewpoint at which a thing is described. Could be asked to anticipate how much of the thing survives the distance, and who can read the result. **The pick.**
- **The Martian scientist** (p. 443), no vision, instruments and mathematics. Controls what to investigate; could be asked to anticipate what of Earth it can understand (lightning yes, the human concept of lightning no).
- **The Martian physiologist** (p. 444, fn. 10), who has the human brain under instruments. Controls nothing we could hand over; could be asked to conclude whether there is anything it is like to be the human.
- **The human imaginer** (p. 439), trying to be a bat by adding webbing, poor vision, sonar; controls the additions, subtractions and modifications; is told that every result is "what it would be like for me to behave as a bat behaves".
- **The person blind from birth** (pp. 440, 449), the reader the objective phenomenology is for.
- **The bat** (pp. 438–439), whose sonar is "three-dimensional forward perception" and whose experience is beyond our concepts; an intelligent bat (p. 440) is the mirror of us.

## 7. The aim and the verdict

**Aim** (start screen, top of every play screen, first line of How to play): *Get as much of each thing across to its reader as you can.*

**Yardstick in its units, during play:** after every send, the thing itself drawn at the reader's side, as much of it as arrived (whole, part, outline only), with the game's line saying so in words: all of it; most of it; a part, the shape and not the thing; none of it, the cause and not the thing; nothing, unreadable.

**Verdict** (end screen leads with, in this order): the headline, from the player's answer to the bat's blank frame; what Nagel says (three lines: step back and the world gets clearer; step back and an experience drops out of the frame, because it is a way of seeing and not a thing seen; a frame you cannot read is not an empty one); what you found, one line per thing in the game's words; where you and the paper part; what would have followed had your answer stood. Then the six prints in a row, sent beside received.

## 8. Three concepts

**A. The zoom ring (agent; discovery). The pick.** Controls: one zoom ring with three stops (From inside, The shape, The physics) and one Send button. The reader's card says who they are and which senses they have; each stop says which sense it takes to read it; the viewfinder shows the frame at the current stop before the player commits. Thinking before pressing: "Is this thing in this frame? And can this reader read this frame?" Seeing after: the print develops at the reader's side; the thing drawn beside it as much as arrived; the game's line; the reader's line. Discovery moment: round 3, red to a reader blind from birth: the only frames she can read are the ones the red has left. Fun: anticipation (the print developing), agency (the choice of stop is the whole outcome), discovery (the reversal), mastery (round 4 and 5 go right for a reason the player can state). Second playthrough: send the frames you avoided (the physics of red to Ada; the inside of lightning to the Martian) to see what each reader says; answer the last question differently to read the other two fates.

**B. The imagination kit (subject; challenge).** You try to be the bat with Nagel's own three operations: add (webbing, sonar), subtract (sight), modify (hang upside down, dusk hours). Each build is drawn. Thinking before pressing: "which part do I change next?" Seeing after: a figure that is you with additions. The discovery is that every build is "me behaving as a bat". Fun: discovery only. Second playthrough: nothing new. **Fails the walkthrough:** the operations are exchangeable (any build fails the same way), so the choice is one button drawn many times; the result is announced by the game, not produced by anything the player did.

**C. The Martian's report (dilemma; the reckless one).** You are the Martian physiologist with a human brain under your instruments: every cell mapped, every process observed. The human says "it hurts". You write home: there is nothing it is like to be a human; or being a human is this firing; or there is something here that the instruments do not reach. Thinking before pressing: the paper's whole question. Seeing after: the verdict on your report. Fun: discovery. **Fails the author test:** three buttons, one of them the author's, and nothing the player did on the way to earn the answer. What it would have given: the mind-body problem posed from the far side, with the player in the position Nagel says the Martian is in. It is not lost: concept A's last round puts the player in the Martian's chair (the bat's frame comes to them blank, as their red came to the Martian), and the Martian's three reports are the closing question, now with five rounds of the player's own prints as evidence.

**The hybrid** (A's hands with C's decision) is the pick: the ring and the prints, then the Martian's question asked of the player about the bat.

## 10. The pick, and why

A. The ring is the paper's own variable (the degree of objectivity of a description) in the player's hand, and the two rules it discovers are the paper's two sentences about the direction of objectivity. The decisive round has three live options and a wrong branch the paper names (the physics of red to the blind reader: "the cause and not the thing"; the inside of red: "red is like the sound of a trumpet", unreadable). The author test: the Martian physiologist round, where the complete physical account arrives whole and the pain is not in it, is fn. 10 and fn. 11 operated; the bat round is the paper's mirror (p. 440) played, not read. Originality: no game in the table has had the player choose the distance of a description. Runner-up: C, and what would make me switch: a reader who finds the ring too easy after round 3; the fix would be more readers at intermediate distances (a dog, a person deaf from birth) rather than a switch. B's loss: the human-in-a-bat-suit image, read on the About screen.

## 11. The shape

Start; six rounds (one send each); one question; the verdict; How to play and About. Each round has a new thing to find out: (1) an experience lives in the near frame; (2) a thing in the world fills the far frame, and a reader without your eyes needs you to zoom; (3) the collision: the readable frames of red are the ones the red has left, and the shape is the best bridge there is (the objective phenomenology, p. 449); (4) the rule is about the sense shared, not the reader (the trumpet to Ada from inside); (5) the complete physical account of you holds none of the pain, and "is" is a sentence nobody can read yet (pp. 446–447); (6) the mirror: the bat's near frame is blank to you, and you judge it. The question after (6) is the Martian's report (p. 440), answered with the player's own prints as evidence. No presets, no seed, no random draw: the six rounds are the paper's own cases in an order that brings the collision to round 3. No score; the thing itself drawn beside each reader as much as arrived; the verdict in words. Four to five minutes, estimated.

## 12. Controls, mechanism, feedback, moment

Controls: the zoom ring (three stops; starts at From inside every round; dragging or tapping a notch changes the viewfinder at once); *Send*; *Next*; three answer buttons after round 6. Mechanism (`mechanism.js`): `CONFIG.THINGS` with, for each thing and each stop, how much of the thing the frame holds and the game's sentence for it; `CONFIG.READERS` with the senses each reader has; `needs(thing, stop)` the sense a stop takes; `send(round, stop)` returns readable, arrived (fraction), the lines; `verdict(answers)`. Feedback: the print sliding out and developing at the reader's side; the thing drawn beside the reader as much as arrived; the game's line (what you sent, who read it, how much of the thing arrived, and why); the reader's line; the rule in one sentence. Moment: round 3, the ring turned to the frames Ada can read and the red not in them.

## 13. The look

**Register: retro, a 1972 instant camera and its prints.** Where it came from: the paper gives no picture of its own beyond a bat in an attic, and its mechanism is a vantage point moving away from the eyes; a camera with a zoom is the one household object that does exactly that, and the year is Nagel's. The game is a cream card from that decade. **Palette:** cream card `#F4ECDC`; chocolate ink `#3A2A1F`; the rainbow stripe red `#E2402F`, orange `#F0902A`, yellow `#F2C53D`, green `#3F9C5B`, blue `#2F6FB5`; warm grey `#B8AD9C` for what cannot be read. **Type:** Baloo 2 ExtraBold for titles, labels and buttons; Varela Round for everything else; both embedded. **Buttons:** pills, orange fill, chocolate text, no outline, no shadow; the Send button bigger and round-cornered like a shutter. **The one visual rule: colour is the point of view.** A frame from inside is in full colour; a frame of the shape is chocolate ink with one tint; a frame of the physics is ink on white with no colour at all. Turning the ring drains the colour out of the frame; what is left in the far frame is what was never only colour. **The play screen:** the reader's card at the top (portrait, name, the senses they have, their question), the viewfinder in the middle (about 320 × 190, the one big thing), the ring under it with its three labelled notches and the "needs" line, Send under that; all within 700 px. After Send the print slides in under the reader's portrait, and the thing-icon beside it. Pictures answer: the viewfinder, "what would this reader get?"; the print, "what did they get?"; the icon, "how much of the thing is that?".

## 14. What is cut

The particular/type distinction within the subjective (pp. 441–442; a two-stop near end was tried on paper and the second stop had no live use). The imaginer adding webbing (p. 439; read on About). The rainbow, the Mona Lisa, hunger, fear, lust (one case per lesson). Kripke, Davidson, the necessity of the mind-brain relation (fn. 11, p. 448; About). The paper's second half (pp. 446–448: physicalism not shown false; "matter is energy"; the caterpillar) is read in the verdict and on the pain round's result line, not played: the player can act on nothing in it.

## 15. Divergences

- **The three stops** are ours. Nagel's direction is a continuum (p. 443, fn. 8); three stops are the fewest that carry the lesson (near: the viewpoint; middle: the structural description of p. 449; far: the physical account of pp. 443–444).
- **The fractions** (how much of a thing a frame holds) are ours; the paper gives direction only. An experience holds all of itself in the near frame, a part in the shape frame (p. 449: "at least in part", "something would be left out"), none in the physics frame (fn. 10: "no trace"). A thing in the world holds all of itself in the physics frame, a part in the shape frame, and only its look in the near frame (p. 443: "not exhausted by its visual appearance"). The robustness check varies the middle fractions across their whole range and the near fraction of lightning; the best stop per round, and the strategy table's order, do not change.
- **Readability as a threshold** (a frame is read with the senses it is taken with, or not at all) is ours; Nagel's access is graded by similarity (fn. 8). The threshold is the coarse version the screen can state.
- **The readers' sense lists** are ours, from the paper's descriptions (the Martian "with no understanding of visual perception", p. 443; the person "deaf and blind from birth", p. 440, here blind only, so that the trumpet round can exist; the physiologist, p. 444).
- **The pain round's reader is Martian**, so that a frame-4 reader with everything is also one who cannot read from inside; Nagel's physiologist is "a Martian investigating my brain" (p. 444) and also "a human physiologist".
- **The bat round reverses the camera**: the paper's bat does not send; it is the mirror case (p. 440), and the reversal is the game's way of putting the player in the Martian's chair.
- **The sentences the readers say** are the game's, in register, never the paper's words.

## 16. Mechanism kind and claim boundaries

**Toy.** The About screen says what the game demonstrates (under this rule, a thing in the world fills the far frame and an experience empties out of it; a reader without your senses can be reached only by frames the experience has left, so the shape is the best bridge and carries the outline and not the thing; a complete physical account of you arrives whole and holds none of the pain), what it assumes (the three stops; the fractions; readability as all-or-nothing by sense; that the shape frame carries something and the physics frame nothing of an experience), and what the paper establishes (an argument, not evidence: that we have no conception of how the subjective character of experience could be captured objectively, which is a reason to doubt we understand physicalism and not a proof that it is false). Every discovery the game stages follows from the fractions, which encode the paper's stated directions; none follows from a budget or a score, since there is neither. The one discovery that rests on a fraction the paper fixes is the emptiness of the physics frame for an experience (fn. 10: "no trace"); the calibration check shows that setting it to a half moves the best stop for red, which is the lesson's dependence on the paper's claim, stated here and on the About screen.

# Revision 1 (19 November 2026): four rounds, a ring that turns

Built the same day after Steffen played the first build. His three findings, in conversation: the slider promised a continuum and delivered three jumps; the three frames per thing were three separate illustrations rather than one picture seen through a lens, so the lesson was in the captions and not in the motion; and the short summaries were good but belonged after a press, not under the viewfinder while choosing. Agreed, and built this way:

- **Four rounds**, not six: the sunset to Sam, lightning to the Martian, red to Ada, the bat to you. The trumpet goes; its point (readability is about the sense shared, not the reader) is in Ada's reply after red. The pain goes; its point (the complete account of the works that holds none of the experience, and the machine question that follows today) is read in the verdict and on the end screen, not played.
