      Read further

      Nagel does not conclude that mind is not brain. He says we are like someone told that a caterpillar locked in a safe became the butterfly found there weeks later: we may have good reason to believe it without any idea of how it could be so. "The pain is this firing" is a sentence we may one day learn to read; what the bat shows is that no amount of stepping back from the point of view will teach us. His proposal is to go the other way: an objective phenomenology that describes the structure of an experience, as the shape frames did, in terms a creature that cannot have it could follow, knowing one reaches a blank wall eventually.

      How to play

      Get as much of each thing across to its reader as you can.

      Each round a reader asks about a thing. The reader's card says which senses they have and which they lack.

      The viewfinder shows the frame at the zoom ring's stop. From inside holds the thing exactly as it is for you; it can be read only with the sense it was taken with (eyes, ears, a body that feels, sonar). The shape holds the outline of the thing, where it sits among its neighbours and what it makes you do; it can be read with a mind. The physics holds what any instrument would find; it can be read with instruments and sums. The tag on the frame says which.

      Turn the ring and the frame changes. Look at it and ask two things: is the thing in this frame? Can this reader read this frame? Then press Send.

      The frame becomes a print at the reader's side. The small drawing beside the reader fills by how much of the thing arrived: solid is all of it, pale is a part, a dotted outline is none. The game says what crossed and why, in one rule; the reader answers in blue. Press Next.

      Six rounds; every round the ring starts from inside. In the last round the bat holds the camera and you are the reader. Then one question about the frame you could not read, and the verdict.

      About

      About this game

      The paper. Thomas Nagel, "What Is It Like to Be a Bat?", The Philosophical Review 83(4), October 1974, pp. 435–450. DOI 10.2307/2183914. Game built from this paper; the paper is the authority where the two disagree.

      What the paper argues. An organism has conscious experience if there is something it is like to be that organism, for the organism. Most bats perceive the world by sonar, and nothing in our experience lets us imagine what that is like: adding webbing and poor vision to ourselves only tells us what it would be like for us to behave as a bat. A point of view is a type, not a private possession; the more different another creature is, the less of its point of view we can take up. In every other science, understanding grows by moving away from the human point of view toward what any observer could find: lightning is more than its look, and the look is explained as an effect on eyes. For experience the move runs backwards: step away from the point of view and the experience is left behind, because it is how things are from that point of view. A Martian scientist with no eyes could understand lightning in full and never the human idea of it; a Martian physiologist with every cell of your brain on screen would find no trace of your pain, and would be wrong to conclude there is none. None of this shows that mind is not brain. It shows that nobody yet knows how to read the sentence "the pain is this firing", as a pre-Socratic could not read "matter is energy". Nagel's proposal is an objective phenomenology: describing the structure of an experience in terms a creature that cannot have it could follow, as one might explain to a person blind from birth something of what seeing is like, knowing one would reach a blank wall eventually.

      The game. You hold a camera whose zoom ring steps back from your own eyes in three stops. From inside, the frame holds a thing exactly as it is for you, and can be read only with the sense it was taken with. The shape holds the outline of a thing, where it sits among its neighbours and what it makes you do, and can be read with a mind. The physics holds what any instrument would find, and can be read with instruments and sums. Six readers ask about six things from the paper; you choose the stop and send. The print that comes out at the reader's side is what they now have, and the small drawing beside them is how much of the thing that is. At the end the bat holds the camera, and the frame you cannot read is the one you are asked about.

      Mechanism kind: a toy. The paper has no numbers. The game runs a model we built to illustrate its argument, and every number in it is ours: how much of a thing each frame holds (an experience: all of it from inside, a part as the shape, none as the physics; a thing in the world: its look from inside, a part as the shape, all of it as the physics), and the rule that a frame is readable with the sense it was taken with or not at all. The paper gives the direction of each of these and nothing finer; the "shape" stop is its proposal for an objective phenomenology; the readers' senses are drawn from the paper's descriptions of its Martian, its person blind from birth and its physiologist. The game's six readers are invented; their lines are the game's, not the paper's.

      What the game demonstrates: that under this rule, a thing in the world fills the far frame and an experience empties out of it; that a reader without your senses can be reached only by frames the experience has left, so that the shape is the best bridge there is and it carries the outline and not the thing; and that a complete physical account of you can arrive whole and hold none of your pain.
      What it assumes: the three stops; the fractions; readability as all-or-nothing by sense; that the structural frame carries something and the physical frame carries nothing of an experience.
      What the paper establishes: an argument, not evidence: that we have no conception of how the subjective character of experience could be captured by an objective description, and that this is a reason to doubt we understand physicalism, not a proof that it is false. Under this game's assumptions the player meets that argument; the game is no independent evidence for it.

      What is cut. The particular and the type within the subjective (the paper's pp. 441–442); the imaginer adding webbing (p. 439); the rainbow, the Mona Lisa, hunger, fear and lust; Kripke, Davidson and the question whether the mind-brain relation is necessary (fn. 11, p. 448); the caterpillar locked in the safe (p. 447) is on the end screen only.
