Nagel does not conclude that mind is not brain. He says we are like someone told that a caterpillar locked in a safe became the butterfly found there weeks later: we may have good reason to believe it without any idea of how it could be so. "The red is this firing" is a sentence we may one day learn to read; what the bat shows is that no amount of stepping back from the point of view will teach us. His proposal is to go the other way: an objective phenomenology that describes the structure of an experience, as the shape stop did, in terms a creature that cannot have it could follow, knowing one reaches a blank wall eventually. The same question is asked today of machines: a complete account of the works, and whether anything is felt.

How to play

Get as much of each thing across to its reader as you can.

Each round a reader asks about a thing. The reader's card says which senses they have and which they lack.

The picture is what your camera sees. Turn the ring under it and watch: the camera steps back from your own eyes, the colour drains, things leave the frame and other things come in. The ring turns freely, but the shutter fires at only three stops, and the ring settles on the nearest when you let go. Naked eye is the thing exactly as it is for you; it can be read only with the sense it was taken with (eyes, sonar). The shape is the outline of the thing, where it sits among its neighbours; it can be read with a mind. The physics is what any instrument would find; it can be read with instruments and sums. The tag under the picture says which stop you are at and what it takes to read it; the blue marks above the ring show which stops this reader can read.

Look at the picture and ask: is the thing still in it? Then press Send.

The picture becomes a print at the reader's side. The small drawing beside the reader fills by how much of the thing arrived: solid is all of it, pale is a part, a dotted outline is none. One line says what crossed. What that means opens the same stop drawn with its labels, what left the picture on the way there, the rule, and the reader's answer. Next goes on.

Three rounds; every round the ring starts at the naked eye. Then night falls and the camera turns on a bat. Three windows, one for each thing: under the window is a tray of pieces of your own evening, storm and field. Tap the ones you think the bat has; they go into the window. Press That’s my bat: the pieces a bat has turn into the bat’s own idiom, echoes and loud waves and warmth, the pieces it does not have fade, and the ones you missed appear dashed, so the window ends as the shape of the bat’s evening. A line under each piece says why. After the third window the camera goes one stop further in, and the window is blank. Then one question about that blank frame, and the verdict in steps; press Go on.

About

About this game

The paper. Thomas Nagel, "What Is It Like to Be a Bat?", The Philosophical Review 83(4), October 1974, pp. 435–450. DOI 10.2307/2183914. Game built from this paper; the paper is the authority where the two disagree.

What the paper argues. An organism has conscious experience if there is something it is like to be that organism, for the organism. Most bats perceive the world by sonar, and nothing in our experience lets us imagine what that is like: adding webbing and poor vision to ourselves only tells us what it would be like for us to behave as a bat. A point of view is a type, not a private possession; the more different another creature is, the less of its point of view we can take up. In every other science, understanding grows by moving away from the human point of view toward what any observer could find: lightning is more than its look, and the look is explained as an effect on eyes. For experience the move runs backwards: step away from the point of view and the experience is left behind, because it is how things are from that point of view. A Martian scientist with no eyes could understand lightning in full and never the human idea of it; a Martian physiologist with every cell of your brain on screen would find no trace of your pain, and would be wrong to conclude there is none. None of this shows that mind is not brain. It shows that nobody yet knows how to read the sentence "the pain is this firing", as a pre-Socratic could not read "matter is energy". Nagel's proposal is an objective phenomenology: describing the structure of an experience in terms a creature that cannot have it could follow, as one might explain to a person blind from birth something of what seeing is like, knowing one would reach a blank wall eventually.

The game. Two halves. In the first you hold a camera whose zoom ring steps back from your own eyes. The ring turns freely and the picture changes under your thumb: the colour drains, things leave the frame and other things come in. The shutter fires at three stops. At the naked eye the frame holds a thing exactly as it is for you, and can be read only with the sense it was taken with. The shape holds the outline of a thing, where it sits among its neighbours, and can be read with a mind. The physics holds what any instrument would find, and can be read with instruments and sums. Three readers ask about three things from the paper; you choose the stop and send. The print that comes out at the reader's side is what they now have, and the small drawing beside them is how much of the thing that is. In the second half night falls and the camera turns on a bat. Three windows, one for each of the same three things: under each is a tray of pieces of your own evening, storm and field, and you tap the ones you think the bat has. The window then settles into the shape of the bat's evening: the pieces a bat has turn into its idiom, echoes and loud waves and warmth, the pieces it does not have fade, and the ones you missed appear dashed. After the third window the camera goes one stop further in, and the window is blank. Then one question about that blank frame, and the verdict. This is the third build (19 November 2026); the earlier ones are kept in the repository.

Where the second half steps away from the paper. Nagel writes that we can ascribe general types of experience to a bat on the basis of its structure and behaviour (sonar as "a form of three-dimensional forward perception"; versions of pain, fear, hunger and lust) and no more. The windows take him at his word and go further than he does: which pieces a bat has is decided by bat biology the paper does not contain, summarised from general references (Neuweiler, The Biology of Bats, 2000; Fenton and Simmons, Bats: A World of Science and Mystery, 2015): most small bats have poor eyesight that tells light from dark and no colour, hearing far sharper than ours, sonar as the main sense and a good nose; they feel warmth as we do and fly at dusk. The pieces, the idiom of the window and the lines are the game's. What the windows show is therefore the shape of a bat's evening as a biologist would draw it, which is Nagel's objective phenomenology done by hand; the blank one stop further in is the paper's claim, and the game has not shown you what it is like to be a bat and could not.

Mechanism kind: a toy. The paper has no numbers. The game runs a model we built to illustrate its argument, and every number in it is ours: how much of a thing each frame holds (an experience: all of it from inside, a part as the shape, none as the physics; a thing in the world: its look from inside, a part as the shape, all of it as the physics), and the rule that a frame is readable with the sense it was taken with or not at all. The paper gives the direction of each of these and nothing finer; the "shape" stop is its proposal for an objective phenomenology; the readers' senses are drawn from the paper's descriptions of its Martian, its person blind from birth and its physiologist. The game's readers are invented; their lines are the game's, not the paper's.

What the game demonstrates: that under this rule, a thing in the world fills the far frame and an experience empties out of it; that a reader without your senses can be reached only by frames the experience has left, so that the shape is the best bridge there is and it carries the outline and not the thing; that the physical account of red can arrive whole, to a reader who can read it, and hold none of the red; and that the shape of a bat's evening can be built from what is known of bats, while the stop beyond it stays blank.
What it assumes: the three stops; the fractions; readability as all-or-nothing by sense; that the structural frame carries something and the physical frame carries nothing of an experience; and, in the second half, the bat biology named above and the game's choice of pieces.
What the paper establishes: an argument, not evidence: that we have no conception of how the subjective character of experience could be captured by an objective description, and that this is a reason to doubt we understand physicalism, not a proof that it is false. Under this game's assumptions the player meets that argument; the game is no independent evidence for it.

What is cut. The particular and the type within the subjective (the paper's pp. 441–442); the imaginer adding webbing (p. 439; the windows are its cousin, built from pieces instead of webbing); the rainbow, the Mona Lisa, the trumpet, pain, hunger, fear and lust (the physiologist with the whole brain on screen, fn. 10 and p. 444, is read on the end screen rather than played); Kripke, Davidson and the question whether the mind-brain relation is necessary (fn. 11, p. 448); the caterpillar locked in the safe (p. 447) is on the end screen only.
