A keeper's problem, from Benjamin (1936)Benjamin's
Veil

You keep one face. It is painted, it has a halo, and hardly anyone has ever seen it. Every age brings a new way to copy a face, and the people press closer.

Each age you set the veil (how near the people may come), let the age pass, and see who came and what is left of the halo. Six ages, each with a new way to copy a face, then one last choice about where the face goes.

Over six ages, let the face move as many people as it can.

A face moves the people who see it, and moves them more when they see its halo. Only the face itself carries the whole halo; a copy carries a trace, and every copy you make spends some of the original's. Kept in the dark the halo stays whole, but each age's eyes are harder to move than the last, and each age has more people.

How to play

Over six ages, let the face move as many people as it can.

  1. Each age, set the veil. A ladder of red slabs: in the dark (only you see the face), on the day (the faithful, once a year, before the face itself), and then the ways of copying a face as the ages bring them: on coins, in woodcuts, in lithographs, in photographs, on film. Each age adds one rung, marked new; the old rungs stay. Tapping a slab shows you what the people would see by that means: the painting itself, or the copy. Nothing is spent until you let the age pass.
  2. Let the age pass. The crowd walks in (how many is what the means gave you), and the heads the halo moved are rimmed in gold. A copy carries only a trace of the halo, and every copy spends some of the original's. A line says what happened. The bar under it is moved this age: how many of the people this age could have moved you moved; gold for the part the halo did, red for the part being seen did.
  3. The halo does not grow back, and it does not fade on its own: kept in the dark it stays whole. But each age's eyes are harder to move than the last: the more copying there is in the world, the less a halo moves the people, whatever you did.
  4. The tally. Each age counts the people it moved, and each age has more people than the last, so the late ages weigh more. There is no running total; the verdict at the end sets your six ages against the most a keeper could have done.
  5. The verdict: how many people your face moved, of what was possible, in words; your six ages beside the most a keeper could have done; what Benjamin says about the path you took; and his last page, 1936, as a reading.

Nothing is random. The same choices give the same ages every time, so a second run is a different plan, not a different draw.
