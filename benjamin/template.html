A keeper's problem, from Benjamin (1936)Benjamin's
Veil

You keep one face. It is painted, it has a halo, and hardly anyone has ever seen it. Every age brings a new way to copy a face, and the people press closer.

Each age you set the veil (how near the people may come), let the age pass, and see who came and what is left of the halo. Seven ages, then one last choice about where the face goes.

Over seven ages, let the face move as many people as it can.

A face moves the people who see it, and moves them more while it still has its halo. Being seen needs nearness; the halo needs distance, and once spent it does not grow back. Each age there are more people than the last.

How to play

Over seven ages, let the face move as many people as it can.

  1. Each age, set the veil. Five slabs, from in the dark (only you see the face) to on the screen (everyone, together, close up). An age only offers the slabs its technique allows: there is no print before the woodcut, no screen before film. Tapping a slab parts the red curtains and brings the face nearer. Nothing is spent until you let the age pass.
  2. Let the age pass. The crowd walks in (how many, and how near, is what the veil gave them); the halo thins by how near you let them; copies go out in the age's technique. A line says what happened. The bar under it is moved this age: how many of the people this age could have moved you moved; gold for the part the halo did, red for the part being seen did.
  3. The halo does not grow back. What you spend is spent. And each age arrives with eyes of its own: the more copying there is in the world, the thinner the halo arrives, whatever you did.
  4. Under the bar, five small frames show what the halo would be at each veil this age, yours ringed in red.
  5. The tally. Each age counts the people it moved, and each age has more people than the last, so the late ages weigh more. There is no running total; the verdict at the end sets your seven ages against the most a keeper could have done.
  6. 1936: the halo is gone and four places want the face. Pick one. Then the verdict: how many people your face moved, of what was possible, in words, and what Benjamin says about the path you took.

Nothing is random. The same choices give the same ages every time, so a second run is a different plan, not a different draw.
