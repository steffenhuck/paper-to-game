# Design memo — *Benjamin's Veil*

Walter Benjamin, *Das Kunstwerk im Zeitalter seiner technischen Reproduzierbarkeit*, third version (1936–39). A game in which the player keeps one face through seven ages, decides each age how near to let the people come to it, and watches the halo go.

## 0. Given

Mine, no ideas. No precedent overridden by instruction; the choices below are the engine's.

## 1. Paper

Walter Benjamin, "Das Kunstwerk im Zeitalter seiner technischen Reproduzierbarkeit (Dritte Fassung)", in *Gesammelte Schriften* I.2, ed. Rolf Tiedemann and Hermann Schweppenhäuser (Frankfurt: Suhrkamp, 1974), pp. 471–508; written 1936–39, first published in this form 1955 (*Schriften*). No DOI. The second version is in *Gesammelte Schriften* VII, pp. 350 ff.; the French version appeared in the *Zeitschrift für Sozialforschung* 5 (1936). An English translation of this third version is in *Illuminations* (1968) and, under the title "The Work of Art in the Age of Its Technological Reproducibility", in *Selected Writings* vol. 4 (Harvard, 2003). Available: the 38-page PDF of the German text (typeset, with the footnotes), nothing else. The paper is an essay; there are no tables, figures or data, and no numbers at all beyond the dates of inventions and Huxley's reading-public arithmetic in footnote 21.

## 2. What the paper hands over

An **argument**, primary and only. The essay is a verbal mechanism: technical reproduction detaches the work of art from its here-and-now, which is the seat of its authenticity and its aura; the masses' wish to bring things close, together with the techniques that let them, changes perception itself, so that the aura decays everywhere, not only in copies; with the aura goes the work's grounding in ritual (cult value), and what replaces it is exhibition value and, finally, a grounding in politics. The essay then reads photography and film through this mechanism (the face as the last refuge of cult value; the film actor tested by the apparatus; the star cult as a rebuilt aura; the cameraman as surgeon; reception in distraction) and closes with the political fork: fascism aestheticises politics, communism politicises art.

To play it, the argument is formalised into a toy (section 16): one face, seven ages, one control (the veil: how near the face is let), and two quantities Benjamin keeps apart, cult value and exhibition value, traded against each other at a price that is ours. The places where the toy chose something the paper did not are listed in section 15.

## 3. The discovery, and its kind

**A counterintuitive result** carried by **a mechanism made intelligible**. The stranger's belief: the halo (the aura, the thing that makes the original special) belongs to the picture, and copies are the threat to it, so a keeper who refuses to copy keeps it. Benjamin: the aura is "the unique appearance of a distance, however near" (section III), a relation between the thing and the eyes looking at it; and the eyes are made by the age. Once an age has learned to want things close and to take them in copies, the aura withers even for the thing that was never copied: the veiled original is seen with the eyes of people who own the postcard. The keeper who hides the face does not keep the halo. He keeps a certificate (footnote 8: authenticity as the collector's fetish). The mechanism underneath: cult value lives on unapproachability (footnote 7), exhibition value on reach; each age's technique raises what reach is possible; and with photography, "exhibition value begins to drive back cult value along the whole line" (section VI).

Second discovery, at the end: when the aura is gone the longing for it is not, and where that longing is served, Benjamin says what comes of it: the market's authentic original, the star's "personality" (section X), the leader's face at the rally and the war it leads to (epilogue), or the people filming themselves (section X, the Soviet film; section XIII). The game stages this as a committed fork, not a lecture: the player chooses, then sees.

## 4. The core narrative

There is one face, painted long ago. People love it, and part of why they love it is that they hardly ever get to see it: it lives in the dark, far away, and it has a halo. You are its keeper. Every age, a new way of making copies arrives, and the people press closer; you decide how near to let them. If you show the face, everyone can see it, but the halo gets thinner and never grows back. If you hide it, the halo stays bright, for a while. Then something strange happens: the halo fades even though you never lifted the veil, because the people's eyes have changed. They have seen so many pictures that nothing looks far away any more. In the end there is no halo left to keep, only a face that everyone owns, and the last question is what that face is for.

## 5. Who lives in the paper

- **The keeper of the work** (the priest of the cella, the cleric with the veiled Madonna, the prince with his gallery, the publisher, the studio): controls how near the work is let, how often it is shown, whether and how it is copied. Could be asked to anticipate what an age's eyes will do to the halo, and how far an age's technique can carry the face.
- **The masses**: Benjamin's real engine (section III: the wish to bring things close; section XV: the matrix). Control nearness demanded; could be asked to anticipate what their own wanting does to the thing wanted.
- **The work of art itself** (the elk on the cave wall, the god in the cella, the Madonna, the Sistine Madonna of footnote 11): its "choice" is none; what varies is where it is put and how many of it exist.
- **The film actor** (sections VIII–X): performs before the apparatus, in fragments, tested; controls the performance, not the cut; could be asked to anticipate the examiner-public.
- **The cutter and the cameraman** (sections VIII, XI, XIII): assemble the fragments under a new law; the surgeon who penetrates the tissue of reality.
- **The public in the cinema** (sections XII, XIV, XV): the distracted examiner, trained by shocks.
- **The theorists** (section VII): asked the wrong question. Not a player.

**What this game does differently.**

- **Position: agent, the keeper of a single work.** Cantor's player was also an agent, but a constructor in a contest; this one is a custodian making a timing decision under a changing world. The keeper is the one figure in the essay with a real lever (hide, show, copy), and the player can say what they did without "guess": *I kept the face veiled until the age of the photograph, then put it in print.*
- **Format: challenge, an optimal-stopping problem,** with the discovery inside it. Hubble and Watson were discovery; Cantor strategic. Here the player plans when to open the veil, and the plan is defeated or confirmed by an age that changes the people's eyes whatever the player does.
- **Device: the halo and the crowd (one of our own).** The face sits in a frame; behind its head a gold halo whose thickness is the aura; in front of it a crowd whose number is the reach and whose distance is the nearness; in the margin, copies in the age's technique. Nothing is struck, no ribbon bows, no dot lands on a plate.
- **Chassis: a 1930s photomontage poster** (Worldly family; the last three were Instrument, Loud, Drawn). Poster cream ground after a black sky, a red bill and kraft. Ink black, constructivist red, halftone grey, and gold leaf for the halo only. Oswald (condensed poster capitals) for display, Libre Franklin for text. Buttons are red slabs with a black rule, no radius. Nothing brass, no tickets, no tags.

## 7. The aim and the verdict

**Aim**, on the start screen, at the top of the play screen, first line of How to play:

> Keep the face's hold on the people through seven ages.

"Hold" is defined on the start screen in one line before the player presses anything: *A face holds people in two ways: by its halo, which needs distance, and by being seen, which needs nearness. Each age you get one veil to set.*

**Verdict**, the lines the end screen leads with:

> *What you did:* You held the people for about four fifths of what was possible. You kept the veil down through the cathedral and the court, and lifted it in the age of the photograph.
>
> *What Benjamin says:* The halo was never in the face. It was the distance between the face and the people, and the age sets the distance. Hide the face and the halo still goes, because the people's eyes have changed; show it early and the age cannot yet carry it far. Once the copies are everywhere the face belongs to politics.
>
> *What that means:* [the fork's line, by choice: the vault / the studio / the rally / the newsreel]

The yardstick during play is in the aim's units: after each age, "hold this age" as one bar, gold for the halo's part and red for the crowd's part, and the seven-age strip at the top fills one slot per age. The best possible is not shown until the end.

## 8. Three concepts

**A. The Keeper (agent; challenge with the discovery inside).** One face, seven ages, one control: the veil, five positions from "in the cella, priests only" to "on the screen, everyone together", capped each age by what the age can copy. Before pressing the player weighs: the halo now, how much of it this veil spends, how far this age's technique carries the face, and the sense that the people are pressing closer. After committing they see the crowd arrive, the halo thin, the copies appear in the margin, the age's hold bar. Discovery moment: the age of the photograph arrives and the halo thins to almost nothing before the player has touched the veil; the counterfactual row under the picture shows the halo thin at every veil position. Fun: anticipation (the age passes after the commit), agency (when to open is a real plan with a real optimum), discovery (the halo goes anyway), mastery (a second run opens at a better age). Replay: the always-veiled run and the open-early run are different strategies with different fates, and the fork at the end has four endings.

**B. The Screen Test (subject; discovery).** The player is the film actor of sections VIII–X. A scene is shot in fragments out of order (the knock, the jump from the window, the shot fired behind the actor), the cutter assembles it, and the public examines. The player controls how much of themselves to give each take; the aura of the stage (the here-and-now before a live audience) is gone and the studio offers a "personality" instead. Deliberation: which take to hold back for the live stage, which to give to the apparatus. Discovery: the assembled film is better than any take and the actor is not in it. Fun: anticipation and discovery; little agency, since the cut is the cutter's. Replay: none worth having. The walkthrough stalls at minute two: the actor has no quantity to trade, and the essay does not supply one.

**C. The Examiner (subject; reckless; real time, no words on the play screen).** Sections XIII–XV. The player is the public in the cinema. Images come at them as shocks, in the ballistic manner Benjamin describes; something in each must be caught (a hand reaching for a lighter, slowed; a crowd, enlarged), first in a painting that holds still and can be contemplated, then in a film that will not. The player gets better at catching in distraction, round by round: Benjamin's claim that film is the training instrument of a new apperception. No words during play; a counter of catches. Discovery: the second mode works better than the first once learned, and the player did not concentrate to do it. Fun: mastery, agency, a little discovery. Replay: a skill run. It would fail if the training effect is not felt within five minutes, and it carries only sections XIII–XV; the aura, the cult, the fork would be left out.

## 9. The first five minutes

**A, the pick.** Minute one: a cream poster with a face in a black frame, a gold halo behind its head, the title in red condensed capitals. Two lines say the face is theirs to keep and that it holds people by its halo (distance) or by being seen (nearness). They press BEGIN. Age one, "The cave": a line says the face is painted on the wall of a cave, that there is no way to copy a face yet, and that only the painter and the spirits see it. Two veil slabs are live (in the dark; shown to the clan), three are grey with "no way to copy a face yet". They tap "shown to the clan": the curtain over the frame parts a little and the face comes a little nearer. They press LET THE AGE PASS. A handful of figures walk in and stand far off; the halo loses a little of its thickness; one line: "The clan saw it. The halo is a shade thinner, and it will not grow back." The age's bar: nearly all gold, a sliver of red. The strip at the top gets its first slot. Minute two: the temple, the cathedral. The player is now holding the veil down (the halo is most of the hold, and the techniques carry nothing far), and grinning slightly at having worked that out. The cathedral says "new this age: the woodcut", and the print slab comes alive. They look at it: print spends three quarters of the halo and reaches a few hundred. Not yet. Minute three: the court, then the print shop. The halo is visibly thinner at the start of the court age although they did nothing; the line says "the age's eyes have changed: there are engravings in the houses now". They frown. The print shop: the halo arrives at about half; the print slab says "the lithograph: in every house". They weigh it: half a halo kept against most of the people reached. Many open here; some hold one more age. Minute four: the studio. The halo arrives at a quarter and the counterfactual row shows it at a quarter whatever they do. The discovery: the halo was going anyway. They open the veil if they have not, and the crowd floods in close. Minute five: the cinema, everyone together, then 1936: the halo is gone, the longing is not; four slabs, each a place to put the face; they choose, the face is drawn there, three lines say what Benjamin says of it; then the verdict. They can say: *I kept it veiled until the photograph, then opened it; the halo went anyway.*

**C, the runner-up.** Minute one: a black screen, a painting of a street, a question: "Where is the lighter?" They look, find it, tap. Minute two: the same street as a film, fast; the lighter is there for a third of a second; they miss it twice, then catch it. Minute three: the catches come easier; the counter climbs. Minute four: they notice they are not looking for it any more, it arrives in the corner of the eye; this is the point. Minute five: a verdict about distraction as a mode of mastery. Honest, playable, and about one fifth of the essay.

## 10. The pick, and why

**A, The Keeper.** The essay's central mechanism (aura as distance; perception made by the age; cult value giving way to exhibition value; then politics) is the thing the player operates, and the one decision the essay allows a keeper (how near to let the people) is the player's one control. The deliberation is real: an optimal-stopping problem whose optimum lies where Benjamin dates the turn, at lithography and photography. The discovery lands inside the play, after a commitment, at the age of the photograph. The sentence afterwards has no "guess".

**Author test.** Benjamin would recognise the cella, the veiled Madonna, the woodcut arriving late in the Middle Ages, lithography putting graphic art into everyday life, photography driving cult value back along the whole line, and the face as cult value's last refuge; he would enjoy the player hiding the face through four ages and finding a certificate where the halo had been; and he would insist on the fork, which the game gives him as its last age. The colleague who knows the answer still has to choose the age to open, and the heuristics table (section 17) shows the choice matters.

**Runner-up: C.** Switch if the author wants a game about the new perception (sections XIII–XV) rather than about the aura and its fate. It would be shorter, wordless, and a skill run.

**What the reckless concept would have given.** A play screen with no words and a real-time loop in which the player feels their own apperception change; the paper's claim that film trains perception, staged as training. Given up: the clock, the catching, the silence. Kept from it: the counterfactual row, which puts the discovery in front of the player's eyes rather than in a sentence.

## 11. The shape

Seven ages, one veil each, then one fork, then the verdict. Nine screens: start, play (the same screen, redrawn seven times), the fork, the end; plus How to play and About. No presets: the ages are the essay's own sequence, in its own order, since the discovery needs the halo to have been held before it goes.

The ages and their sources:

| Age | Date on screen | New this age (the technique) | Veil ceiling | Source |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| The cave | long ago | none: the painted wall | shown to the clan | section V (the elk, a magic instrument, shown to fellow men, meant for the spirits) |
| The temple | antiquity | casting and coining, not for faces | in the forecourt | section I (the Greeks' two techniques); section V (the god in the cella, only for the priest) |
| The cathedral | the Middle Ages | the woodcut | in the nave | section I (woodcut); section V (Madonnas veiled nearly all year; sculptures invisible from the ground) |

| The studio | about 1880 | the photograph | in print, every house, and in every hand | section I; section VI (exhibition value drives back cult value; the face as last refuge) |

Score: yes. Hold per age (cult part plus crowd part) summed over seven ages, shown at the end as a fraction of the best possible in words, with the age the player opened named. The fork is not scored; it is read.

## 12. Controls, mechanism, feedback, moment

- **Control:** the veil. Five slabs: *in the dark* (the cella: the keeper and the spirits), *shown on the day* (the clan, the congregation, the feast), *on the wall* (whoever comes), *in print* (as many as the age can print), *on the screen* (everyone, together). Each age enables up to its ceiling; the rest are grey with the reason. Every age starts with no slab chosen. One commit button: LET THE AGE PASS. The commit screen shows the veil parting and the face coming nearer (what the control does) and nothing of the crowd, the halo's loss or the bar (the payoff).
- **Mechanism (toy, section 16):** the halo is a stock. When an age arrives it is multiplied by that age's eyes (the world's reproduction, exogenous); when the veil is set it is multiplied by one minus the nearness (irreversible). Cult value this age is the halo after both. Reach this age is the age's reach at that veil, cut if the face is kept farther than the age's people demand. Hold is cult value plus reach.
- **Feedback:** the crowd walks in (number = reach, distance = nearness); the halo thins; copies in the age's technique appear in the margin; a line says what happened in words; the bar in hold units; the strip at the top fills. After the age, a row of five small frames shows the halo as it would have been at each veil this age, with the player's choice ringed: the paper's answer after the player's.
- **Moment:** the studio age arrives; the halo thins to a quarter before the veil is touched; the counterfactual row is thin all the way across.

## 13. The look

**Register: a 1930s photomontage poster** (Worldly family: the constructivist and Heartfield line). It comes from the essay's own time and company: Benjamin wrote it in Paris exile in 1936 for the Institut's journal, quoting Vertov and the Dadaists, in the years of the photomontage poster, in which a halftone face, a red diagonal and a block of condensed capitals do all the work. The poster is itself a product of the techniques the essay is about. Comic is not the register; neither is the journal.

- **Palette:** poster cream `#EFE6D2` (the ground); ink `#141210` (text, frames, the crowd, the engraved and lithographed face); red `#C8321E` (titles, slabs, the veil, the crowd's part of the bar); halftone grey `#8A857B` (the photographed and filmed face, secondary text); gold leaf `#D4A017` with a lighter edge `#F0CC5A` (the halo and the halo's part of the bar, nothing else).
- **Type:** Oswald (SIL OFL), a condensed grotesk, in capitals with wide tracking for the title, the age names, the slabs and the bar labels; Libre Franklin (SIL OFL) regular, bold and italic for text. Both embedded as data.
- **Buttons:** red slabs, no radius, cream Oswald capitals, a 3 px ink rule along the bottom edge; pressed, ink with cream capitals. The veil slabs are the same shape, stacked, with the ceiling greyed; the chosen one goes ink.
- **The one visual rule:** gold is the halo and only the halo, and the face is always drawn in the technique of its age: flat ochre on the cave wall, white marble in the temple, flat icon colour with the gold leaf behind it in the cathedral, cross-hatching at the court, grain in the print shop, a halftone dot screen in the studio, a film frame with sprocket holes in the cinema, a red-and-black poster in 1936. The copies in the margin are small versions in the same technique.
- **Play screen at 380 px:** the aim line in ink (two lines, 17 px) with ? at the right; the age band (red, Oswald, "AGE 4 OF 7 · THE COURT · ABOUT 1500") and under it one line of orientation in Franklin ("New this age: the engraving. A face can be printed a few hundred times."); the picture, 340 × 300: a black frame, the face at the scale its nearness gives, the halo behind the head, the red veil hanging from the top and parting by the slab chosen, the floor in front where the crowd stands (answers "how near is the face, and how big is its halo?"); the margin to the right of the frame, 30 px, where copies stack (answers "how many are out?"); the five slabs, 48 px high (answers "who gets to see it?"); LET THE AGE PASS, 56 px. After the commit, in the same place as the slabs: the line in words, the hold bar (gold then red, 24 px high, labelled HOLD THIS AGE), the counterfactual row of five small frames (56 px each), and NEXT AGE. The strip of seven slots sits under the age band throughout.

## 14. What is cut

The film actor and the apparatus (sections VIII–X) beyond one line at the fork; the cameraman as surgeon (XI); the optical unconscious, slow motion and the close-up (XIII) beyond the "on the screen" veil's close-up; Dadaism and the shock effect (XIV); reception in distraction and architecture (XV); the Vienna school and the late Roman art industry (III); the Sistine Madonna's own story (footnote 11); the theorists' wrong question (VII); the reader becoming a writer (X) beyond one line at the newsreel; Huxley's arithmetic (footnote 21); Marinetti's manifesto beyond the sentence *fiat ars, pereat mundus*. The preface and the epilogue's Marxian framing are kept to the About screen.

## 15. Divergences

- **One face across eight thousand years.** The essay's examples are many works (the elk, the Venus, the Madonna, a Picasso). The game follows one face so the halo can be a stock the player watches. Our choice.
- **The halo as a stock, spent irreversibly.** Benjamin: the here-and-now cannot be restored and the aura withers; he does not say that showing spends it in proportion to nearness. The proportion is ours; the irreversibility is his.
- **The age's eyes as a multiplier.** Benjamin says perception is historically conditioned and that the masses' wish for nearness and the reproduction techniques change it; the schedule by which it changes, with the big drops at lithography and photography, is read from section I and section VI, and its numbers are ours.
- **Reach, demand and the cut for distance.** That people stop coming to a thing kept farther than they have learned to want it is Benjamin's "Bedürfnis" of section III; the exponential cut is ours.
- **Hold as cult value plus reach at equal weight.** The price that trades the two is ours (section 16); the robustness check in section 17 shows the shape of the result does not depend on it.
- **The compressed clock.** Seven ages, one decision each; the essay's chronology is kept, its spacing is not.
- **The 1936 fork.** The four places are the essay's (footnote 8, section X, the epilogue, sections X and XIII); putting them before a keeper as a choice is ours. Benjamin does not score them and nor does the game.

## 16. Mechanism kind and claim boundaries

**Kind: toy.** The game runs a model built to illustrate the essay's argument. No part of it is ported from the paper (there is nothing to port) or reconstructed from data (there are none).

- **The game demonstrates** that, under its assumptions, a keeper who hides a work does not keep its aura once the age has learned to take things in copies; that a keeper who shows early spends the aura for a reach the age cannot yet give; that the best age to open the veil is the age of the print and the photograph; and that when the aura is gone the longing for it can be served in four places, each with the ending Benjamin gives it.
- **It assumes** the toy: one face; a halo that is a stock, multiplied each age by the age's eyes and, when shown, by one minus the nearness; reach by veil and age from our table; a cut for keeping the face farther than the age demands; hold as cult value plus reach at equal weight; seven ages at the essay's dates. All numbers are ours and sit in the config block.
- **The paper establishes** the argument's claims only as an argument: that the aura is a relation of distance; that perception is historically conditioned and that mass reproduction changes it; that with photography exhibition value drives back cult value; that art thereby loses its grounding in ritual and gains one in politics; and that fascism aestheticises politics while communism politicises art. It establishes none of these by evidence, gives no quantities, and the game's optimum opening age is a property of the toy, consistent with where the essay dates the turn, not a finding of the essay.

**Where the discoveries come from.** The halo going under the veil follows from the exogenous eyes, which are the essay's claim (perception changes with the age). The optimum age to open follows from the eyes and the reach table together, both ours but read from section I and VI; it is checked in section 17 against other prices and other schedules. The fork's endings are the essay's own sentences, not the mechanism's.

## 17. Calibration plan and result

What `calibration.js` checks, against the essay's claims (there are no numbers to check against):

- (P1) *The halo withers under the veil.* With the veil never lifted, the halo at the cinema age is below a tenth of its start, and most of the loss comes after the court (sections III, VI).
- (P2) *With photography, exhibition drives back cult along the whole line.* The first age at which reach at the age's widest veil exceeds the halo of the never-shown face is the studio, and the print shop is the first age where the two are within a tenth of each other (section VI; section I on lithography).
- (P3) *Unapproachability.* Cult value this age falls strictly with nearness, at every age (footnote 7).
- (P4) *Irreversibility.* A halo once spent never recovers: the stock is non-increasing over the ages for every strategy (section II).
- (P5) *Reach grows with the technique.* The widest reach available grows strictly from age to age (section I).
- (P6) *Heuristics.* Always veiled, always widest, open at the court, at the print shop, at the studio, at the cinema, and the exhaustive optimum. The optimum opens at the print shop or the studio; always veiled and always widest both land below three quarters of it.
- (P7) *Robustness.* P6's shape (the optimum in the print-shop-to-studio window; both ends punished) survives the crowd's part weighted at half and at double, and survives an eyes schedule that declines a third more slowly.
- (P8) *The fork.* The rally gives the largest 1936 hold of the four and ends in war; the newsreel gives no halo; the vault gives a certificate and a crowd of one (footnote 8, section X, the epilogue).
- (W) *Words.* No decimals in any string the screen shows; every veil slab and every fork slab has a label and a one-line meaning.
