Veil

You keep one face. It is painted, it has a halo, and hardly anyone has ever seen it. Every age brings a new way to copy a face, and the people press closer.

Each age you set the veil (how near the people may come), let the age pass, and see who came and what is left of the halo. Six ages, each with a new way to copy a face, then one last choice about where the face goes.

Over six ages, let the face move as many people as it can.

A face moves the people who see it, and moves them more while it still has its halo. Being seen needs nearness; the halo needs distance, and once spent it does not grow back. Each age there are more people than the last.

How to play

Over six ages, let the face move as many people as it can.

  1. Each age, set the veil. A ladder of red slabs: in the dark (only you see the face), on the day (the faithful, once a year), and then the ways of copying a face as the ages bring them: on coins, in woodcuts, in lithographs, in photographs, on film. Each age adds one rung, marked new; the old rungs stay. Tapping a slab parts the red curtains and brings the face nearer. Nothing is spent until you let the age pass.
  2. Let the age pass. The crowd walks in (how many, and how near, is what the veil gave them); the halo thins by how near you let them; copies go out by the means you chose (coins, sheets, prints, photographs, film). A line says what happened. The bar under it is moved this age: how many of the people this age could have moved you moved; gold for the part the halo did, red for the part being seen did.
  3. The halo does not grow back. What you spend is spent. And each age arrives with eyes of its own: the more copying there is in the world, the thinner the halo arrives, whatever you did.
  4. Under the bar, small frames show what the halo would be at each rung this age, yours ringed in red.
  5. The tally. Each age counts the people it moved, and each age has more people than the last, so the late ages weigh more. There is no running total; the verdict at the end sets your six ages against the most a keeper could have done.
  6. 1936: the halo is gone and four places want the face. Pick one. Then the verdict: how many people your face moved, of what was possible, in words, and what Benjamin says about the path you took.

Nothing is random. The same choices give the same ages every time, so a second run is a different plan, not a different draw.

AboutTHE PAPER AND THE GAME

The game in one line

You keep one face through six ages, from the cave to the cinema. Each age brings a new way to copy a face (coins, woodcuts, lithographs, photographs, film), and the old ways stay. Each age you set the veil (how far the face goes, and by what means) and let the age pass; the crowd comes or does not, the halo thins and never grows back, and each age arrives with eyes of its own. In 1936 the halo is gone and four places want the face.

What the paper argues

Walter Benjamin's essay (written in Paris in 1936 and reworked until 1939; this is the third version) says that a work of art has always been copiable, but that technical reproduction (the woodcut, the engraving, lithography, photography, film) is something new: it detaches the work from its here-and-now, the one place and moment it exists in, which is the seat of its authenticity. What a copy cannot carry he calls the aura: "the unique appearance of a distance, however near it may be." The aura, he argues, is not in the object but in the relation between the object and the eyes of its age; and the masses' wish to bring things close, together with the techniques that let them, changes those eyes, so that the aura withers everywhere, not only in the copies. With the aura goes the work's grounding in ritual: art's first use was in cult, and cult value wants the work hidden and unapproachable. Reproduction pushes the other pole, exhibition value, and "with photography, exhibition value begins to drive back cult value along the whole line." What replaces ritual as art's foundation is politics. The essay then reads film through this (the actor tested by the apparatus; the star cult as a halo rebuilt to be sold; the cameraman as a surgeon; the public as a distracted examiner), and ends with the fork: fascism aestheticises politics, giving the masses a face to look at and ending in war; communism answers by politicising art.

Mechanism: a toy

This game demonstrates that, under its own assumptions, a keeper who hides a face does not keep its halo once the age has learned to take things in copies; that a keeper who shows it early spends the halo for a reach the age cannot yet give; that the best age to open the veil is the age of the print and the photograph, about where the essay dates the turn; and that when the halo is gone the longing for it can be served in four places, each with the ending Benjamin gives it.

It assumes the toy. One face. A halo that is a stock, multiplied by each age's eyes when the age arrives (the world's copying, which this keeper does not control) and by one minus the nearness when the veil is set, and never restored. A table of how many people each veil reaches in each age, and a cut when the face is kept farther than the age's people have learned to want it. What an age is worth: the people it moved, which is how many saw the face, times the halo they saw it with, plus a fifth for merely being seen; and each age has more people than the last (doubling, counted by their square root, so the late masses weigh more without drowning the early ages). Those three numbers are ours, settled by playing the plans against each other: under them, a halo nobody sees moves nobody, showing from the first age is a little worse than opening step by step, opening late is much worse, and the best path opens one step wider as each technique arrives, going to print at the lithograph, where Benjamin dates the turn. The check that ships with the game shows that shape survives being-seen priced at a tenth and at two fifths, the people counted more flatly and more steeply, and eyes that change a third more slowly. The essay has no numbers; every number in the game's config block is ours and is tagged with the passage it was read from.

The paper establishes its claims as an argument, not by evidence: that the aura is a relation of distance; that perception is historically conditioned and that mass reproduction changes it; that with photography exhibition value drives back cult value; that art thereby loses its grounding in ritual and gains one in politics; and that fascism aestheticises politics while communism politicises art. It gives no quantities. The game's best age to open the veil is a property of the toy, consistent with the essay's dating, not a finding of the essay. Your hold is a score under our rules, not a measurement of anything.

What is cut

The film actor and the apparatus (sections VIII to X) beyond one line at the fork; the cameraman as surgeon (XI); the optical unconscious, slow motion and the close-up (XIII); Dadaism and the shock effect (XIV); reception in distraction and the example of architecture (XV); the Sistine Madonna's own story (footnote 11); Huxley's arithmetic (footnote 21); the preface's Marxian framing; most of the epilogue beyond its last lines.
