## 2. What the paper hands over

**Primary: a model**, ported. Lehmann's simplified Earth: radius 6370 km; a core of radius 5/9 of the Earth's; a constant P-wave speed of 10 km/s in the mantle and 8 km/s in the core, so rays are straight lines inside each shell and bend at the boundary by the law of refraction. From this she computes, by graphical ray tracing, where a ray leaving the epicentre at take-off angle *i₀* comes up: a ray grazing the core (i₀ = 33.75°) emerges at 112.5°; the same ray, refracted into the core, at 186.2°; as i₀ falls to about 26° the core rays run back to 153.9° and then forward again to 180° at i₀ = 0. Nothing lands between 112.5° and 153.9°: the shadow zone. Then the three-shell Earth: an inner core of radius 8/10 r₀ sin 16° = 0.2205 r₀ (about 1400 km), with a higher speed, 8.6 km/s. The ray with i₀ = 16° just touches it (emerging at 160.7°); rays with i₀ between 16° and 14.85° are totally reflected off it and come up between 160.7° and 118.8°; rays steeper than 14.85° pass through it and come up from 118.8° out to 180°. So the inner core throws waves into the shadow.

**Secondary: an argument**, reported. The observations the model has to meet: P-waves beyond about 105° are weak; P′ is observed, with smaller amplitudes, inside the shadow down to about 105° and up to the real focal distance of 143°; diffraction round the core had been the explanation; Lehmann's P′₃ waves have a large vertical component and grow with distance, which diffraction cannot give. And Part II, four stations near 150° recording the 1929 Buller earthquake, four pulses where two or three were expected. The paper's judgement on itself: "a hypothesis which seems to hold some probability, although it cannot be proved from the data at hand." The epilogue: Gutenberg and Richter's 1200 km inner core two years later; Jeffreys 1939 showing diffraction untenable; twenty years of argument about the speed jump; Birch and Bullen on a solid inner core.

## 3. The discovery, and its kind

**A mechanism made intelligible.** How a ball of slow rock in the middle of the Earth bends waves away from a whole band of the far side, and how a smaller ball of faster rock inside it bounces them back into that band. The player operates the mechanism with Lehmann's own control, the take-off angle, and then with her own hypothesis, the inner ball's size and speed. There is a counterintuitive corner inside it: a wave that dives only a hair steeper than the one that grazes the core does not land a hair further on but seventy degrees further, on the far side of the shadow, and no angle at all lands in between.

## 4. The core narrative

The Earth is a ball. When the ground shakes somewhere, the shaking runs right through the ball and comes out on the far side, where listening posts feel it. But the middle of the Earth is made of slower stuff, and it bends the shaking the way a glass of water bends a straw, so there is a band on the far side where the shaking never arrives. It is a shadow. Posts in the shadow felt a faint shaking anyway. Inge Lehmann drew the paths the shaking takes and found that if there is a harder, faster little ball inside the slow middle, the shaking bounces off it and lands right in the shadow. So the Earth is a ball inside a ball inside a ball, and the little one in the middle was found by a woman in Copenhagen with cardboard cards in oatmeal boxes.

## 5. Who lives in the paper

- **The wave** (a P-wave ray): controls its take-off angle; could be asked to anticipate where it comes up. The paper's agent; every figure is made of it.
- **Lehmann**: controls the model Earth (how many shells, their sizes, their speeds) and computes where rays land; anticipates whether the posts in the shadow get a wave. The experimenter and the theorist.
- **The listening posts** (seismograph stations at Ivigtut, Abisko, Scoresby Sund, Pulkovo near 150°): control nothing; they hear or they do not, and the vertical component tells how steeply the wave arrived.
- **The Earth**: the thing being probed. Its interior is the hidden truth.
- **The earthquake** (Buller, 1929): the source; it fires at every angle at once.

## 7. The aim and the verdict

**Aim** (start screen, top of the play screen, first line of How to play): *Reach every listening post on the far side of the Earth with a wave.* In the second act the line becomes: *Build the inside of the Earth so the posts in the shadow hear the quake too.*

**Yardstick**, in the aim's units: posts reached, shown as the ring of posts itself, lit or dark, with "N of 18 posts have heard you" under it. In act two, the four shadow posts, lit or dark.

**Verdict** (the end screen leads with these, in words):

- *What you did.* "You fired N waves into Lehmann's two-shell Earth and reached every post you could. Four posts, from 115° to 145° round from the quake, sat in a shadow no angle could reach. Then you put a ball inside the core, [about the size of the Moon / a bit smaller than the Moon / half the Moon …], running [a little faster / much slower / …] than the core, and [all four / three / … / none] of the shadow posts heard the quake."
- *What the paper says.* "Lehmann's ball was a bit smaller than the Moon and ran a touch faster than the core around it. Waves bounce off it and land in the shadow, arriving steeply, with a strong up-and-down, and growing stronger with distance, which is what the posts recorded. She called it a hypothesis that seemed probable and could not be proved from the data at hand."
- *What that means.* "It held. Two years later the inner core was computed at about 1200 km; by 1946 it was agreed to be solid. The Earth is a ball inside a ball inside a ball, and the innermost was found with cardboard cards and a pencil."

No number is the verdict. The posts-reached count is the yardstick during play; the end screen leads with the three paragraphs above.

## 8. Three concepts

**A. The Wave** (agent, then controller; discovery with a target inside). *Controls*: one dial, how steeply the wave dives (0° straight down, 90° along the ground); FIRE. In act two, two dials, the inner ball's size and its speed against the core's, and QUAKE, which fires at every angle at once. *Deliberation*: "my 40° shot came up at 100°, my 30° shot at 174°; the post at 135° should be somewhere between; 35°? 33°?" and later "the core bends waves too far round; I need something that bends them back, or bounces them." *After the press*: the wave travels, straight in the mantle, kinks at the core, comes up somewhere on the rim; a post lights or does not; the shot stays drawn. *Discovery moment*: the hair's breadth between 33.75° and 34°, a jump of seventy degrees with nothing in between; then the bounce off the faster ball landing exactly in the gap. *Fun*: anticipation (where will this one come up?), agency (the mapping is learnable and strange), discovery (the wedge appears in the player's own drawing), mastery (the second time through, every reachable post in six shots, and the ball built in one quake). *Replay*: try a slower ball on purpose and watch waves go past the centre and round; try the smallest ball that still fills the shadow.

**B. The Oatmeal Boxes** (experimenter; discovery). Lehmann at her garden table with cards from stations at 149°, 150°, 150.4°, 150.6°: the player reads the four records, marks the pulses on each, and must decide whether they show two waves, three or four, with the Buller earthquake's foreshocks complicating the count. *Deliberation*: which wiggle is a pulse. *Discovery*: four lines through the points means four waves, one more than two shells allow. *Fun*: discovery, some agency. *Why it fails the walkthrough*: a stranger cannot read a seismogram, the records are not in the paper beyond one figure, and every decision is "is that a pulse?", which is a guess dressed as a reading. The paper's own Part II is honest that the data were insufficient.

**C. The Earth** (subject; the reckless one: wordless, real time). The player *is* the Earth. Quakes go off round the rim every few seconds and fire their fans live; the player drags the inner ball bigger or smaller with a thumb and presses to harden or soften it, and the ring of posts glows where waves land, with the four shadow posts marked. No words on the play screen at all. *Deliberation*: continuous tuning by eye. *Discovery*: the moment the shadow posts glow. *Fun*: agency, discovery, a toy-like pleasure. *Replay*: none beyond fiddling. *Walkthrough*: lovely for a minute; but the stranger never learns why there is a shadow, because they never fired a single wave and watched it bend, and the jump at 33.75° is lost in the fan. Its gift, the whole fan firing at once with the player's ball in the middle, is taken into A's second act.

## 10. The pick, and why

**A, The Wave.** The player is inside Lehmann's figure, doing what she did: choosing a take-off angle and seeing where the ray comes up, then choosing an inner core and seeing whether the shadow posts hear. The deliberation is real and gets sharper (the mapping from dive to landing is learnable, and then it breaks), the discovery lands twice inside play (the jump, then the bounce), and the sentence afterwards has no "guess": *I fired waves at every angle and none could land in a band on the far side; then I put a faster ball inside the core and the waves bounced off it into the band.* Originality: no game in the table has had the player aim a thing and watch it travel and bend, nor built one of the paper's figures from the player's own shots. Calibration is a gift: the model is tiny and every number in the paper comes out of it to the decimal.

**Author test.** The author of the commentary, and anyone who teaches this, would recognise Figure 2a forming shot by shot, the 33.75° grazing ray, the 153.9° caustic and the 118.8° reflected ray, and would enjoy the one thing a lecture cannot give: a stranger discovering the shadow by trying to shoot into it.

**Runner-up: C, The Earth.** Switch if the author wants a silent sixty-second piece for a lecture slide, accepting that the shadow is then shown, not found.

**What the reckless concept would have given.** Live play with the whole fan and a ball you shape by hand. Kept: the second act fires the whole fan at once, and the ball grows in the picture as the dial moves. Given up: real time, and silence.

## 11. The shape

Two acts, one long round each, four screens: start; act one (fire waves into the two-shell Earth, as many shots as the player likes, ended by the commitment button *That's every post I can reach*, which fires the full fan and names the shadow, then the twist card); act two (the inner ball, four quakes, the fourth final, or an early *That's my Earth*); verdict. No presets: the two regimes are the paper's two figures, 2a then 2b, in the paper's order because that is the order of the discovery. No score. The yardstick is the ring of posts. Target length five to seven minutes; a player who knows the answer takes three.

## 12. Controls, mechanism, feedback, moment

- **Controls.** Act one: a slider, *Dive*, 0° to 90° in half-degree steps, defined on screen ("0° is straight down through the middle; 90° is along the ground"); FIRE; *That's every post I can reach*. Act two: a slider, *Size of the ball*, from a tenth to a half of the Earth's radius (the core is five ninths), shown as the ball in the picture and in words (half the Moon, about the Moon, bigger than the Moon); a slider, *Speed in the ball*, from a quarter slower than the core to a quarter faster, shown in words (much slower … same as the core … much faster) and as the ball's colour from dark to bone; QUAKE; *That's my Earth*. Every slider starts in the same place each round (dive at 45°; the ball at a quarter of the radius, same speed as the core).
- **Mechanism.** Lehmann's model, ported: straight rays, constant speeds per shell, Snell's law at each boundary, total reflection where the sine exceeds one, Δ summed from the chord geometry; the fold of Δ past 180° onto the mirror side. Posts at 5°, 15°, …, 175°; a post hears a ray that comes up within two degrees of it. The fan is 180 rays, half a degree apart.
- **Feedback.** The wave travelling (a gold line drawn along its path, a kink at each colour change); the landing point on the rim; the post lighting; the drum under the Earth writing the arrival with an up-and-down whose size is the cosine of the arrival angle; the fired shots staying as threads so that the fan forms; the posts-heard line in the aim's units.
- **Moment.** Act one: 34° comes up at 112°, 33.5° at 176°, and the player's own fan has a wedge in it. Act two: rays glance off the pale ball and come up inside the wedge.

## 15. Divergences

- **Posts at 5°, 15°, …, 175° with a two-degree ear.** Ours; the paper's stations are four near 150°. Chosen so that the shadow holds exactly four posts (115° to 145°) and the posts at 105° and 155° are reachable, which keeps the model's limits (112.5°, 153.9°) and the posts' verdicts consistent.
- **The fold of Δ past 180°.** Lehmann draws the 186.2° ray continuing past the antipode (her "2a"). The game draws it the same way on the full circle and reads its landing on the mirror side, so a ray at 186° lights the post at 175° from the other direction. The paper's rays are symmetric; the game fires both sides of the fan.
- **The inner ball's dials.** Size from a tenth to a half of the Earth's radius; speed from three quarters of the core's to five quarters. Ours, as the space a player can search; Lehmann's ball (0.2205 r₀, 8.6 km/s) is inside it, off-centre on both dials so that neither default is the answer.
- **The fan at once.** In act two the quake fires every angle, which is what an earthquake does; in act one the player fires one wave at a time, which is what Lehmann's pencil did.
- **Clock.** A wave takes about a second and a half on screen; in the Earth it takes about twenty minutes.
- **Two-degree ear.** Ours. A post hears a ray within two degrees of it; without an ear no ray would ever hit a point.
- **Words.** "Dive" for the angle of incidence at the surface; "the ball" for the inner core; "post" for station; "the shadow" is the paper's own word.

## 16. Mechanism kind and claim boundaries

**Ported.** The game runs Lehmann's simplified Earth as the commentary reports it: straight rays, one constant P-wave speed per shell (10, 8, and for the inner ball 8.6 km/s), the law of refraction at each boundary via the conserved perpendicular distance r·sin i, total reflection where the sine would exceed one, and Δ summed from the chord geometry. No randomness anywhere.

- **The game demonstrates** what this model does: a slower core leaves the band 112.5°–153.9° unreachable by any angle; a smaller, faster ball inside it bounces rays into that band from 118.8° on, arriving steeply; a much slower ball fills the band the other way, past the far point.
- **The game assumes** the model's simplifications (straight rays, one speed per shell, no energy lost), and ours: eighteen posts every ten degrees, a three-degree ear, the two dials' ranges and starting places, four quakes, the Moon as a yardstick, the drum's up-and-down as the cosine of the dive.
- **The paper establishes** the ray geometry of the two models (its numbers come out of the mechanism to the decimal) and that an inner core with a higher speed fits what the stations in the shadow recorded where diffraction does not. It does not establish that the inner core exists; Lehmann says so, and the game's verdict quotes her saying so.

**Where the discovery comes from.** The shadow and its filling follow from the model's geometry, not from our rules. The calibration check varies the ear (1°–4°) and shifts the posts by five degrees: the posts called unreachable always lie inside 112.5°–153.9°, and every shadow post that lies where her inner-core rays land is lit by her ball. With posts at 115° the sliver 112.5°–118.8° that her own model leaves dark shows up as a dark post under her ball too; the posts were placed at 10°, 20°, …, 180° so that the model's own story is clean, and the memo and About say so. The "faster beats slower" lesson is the model's: a ball a little slower (0.9) leaves the first three shadow posts dark; one a quarter slower fills the band from the far side, which the verdict names rather than hides.
