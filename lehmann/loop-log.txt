VERDICT
  You found the inner core.
  After real earthquakes, one band of listening posts only quivers. You imagined an Earth with one core and tried five directions: the band stayed silent, and it turned out that no direction can ever make it quiver. Then you imagined an Earth with a core within the core. One of your three dives came up in the band and made it quiver. Only the second Earth does what the real one does.
  --
  That is what Inge Lehmann worked out in 1936, by hand, with a pencil. Real listening posts in that band had felt a faint wave. An Earth with one core could not explain it. An Earth with a small core inside the core, where waves run faster, could. She did not say she had proved it. She called it an idea that fitted better, and asked others to check.
  --
  They checked, and she was right. The inner core is about 1220 km from its centre to its edge: smaller than the Moon. Lehmann never said what it is made of. Others worked that out later, and by 1971 it was clear: a solid ball of iron, inside a core of liquid iron.
  "inside the core there is an inner core in which the velocity is larger"

READ FURTHER
  The three earthquakes.
    They are real, and they are the ones Lehmann’s paper discusses: South Pacific, 26 June 1924; Buller, New Zealand, 16 June 1929; Hawke’s Bay, New Zealand, 2 February 1931. What the posts do on screen is a simple picture of what was recorded: real posts in that band felt a faint wave, far weaker than the posts on either side.
  One wave, or all of them?
    A real earthquake does not choose a direction. It sends waves every way at once, and each post feels whichever of them arrive there. In the game you follow one wave at a time, because that is the only way to see which direction ends up where. Lehmann did the same with her pencil. “Every direction at once” is the whole earthquake.
  Why only a quiver?
    Many waves come up at most posts, so they shake hard. Only a thin sliver of the steepest waves meets the inner core: with every direction drawn, about 7 of the 179 waves land in the band. So the posts there feel little. Lehmann worked this out with formulas; the game only counts waves.
  The game is kinder than the Earth.
    In the game one post always answers: the nearest one lights, or, in the band, the nearest one quivers. Really a post only feels a wave that comes up right beside it, and in the band that is very hard to do. The post at 150° is the easy one: any dive from 12.5° to 13.6° comes up beside it. The other three each need a dive that is right to about a tenth of a degree, because near the edge of the inner core a tiny change of direction swings the wave a long way: 14° comes up at 144°, 15° at 122°.
  The Earth in the picture is a sketch.
    It is the one Lehmann drew on purpose: straight paths, one speed in each layer. In the real Earth the paths curve and the band sits a little differently, from about 105° to 143°. Her inner core was about 1400 km from centre to edge; today it is measured at 1220 km.
  There was another explanation.
    In 1936 most scientists said the faint waves had crept round the edge of the core. Much of Lehmann’s paper argues against that: the real records showed the ground moving strongly up and down, as it does when a wave comes up steeply from deep below, and creeping waves would not do that. The game leaves that argument out and sets only the two imagined Earths side by side.
  What she did not say.
    Her paper never says the inner core is solid. It gives it one property: waves run faster in it. Francis Birch proposed in 1940 that it is solid iron, and that was shown in 1971 from the way the whole Earth rings after a great earthquake.
