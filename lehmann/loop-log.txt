VERDICT
  You found the inner core.
  You were the earthquake. In the real earthquake, four posts only quivered. With one core, no direction you chose could make them do anything: they sat in a shadow. With a core within the core, a steep dive is turned into that shadow, and you made two of the four posts quiver yourself. So the Earth with a core within the core is the one that does what the real Earth did.
  --
  That is what Inge Lehmann worked out in 1936, by hand, with a pencil. Real listening posts inside the shadow had felt a faint wave. An Earth with one core could not explain it. An Earth with a small core inside the core, where waves run faster, could. She did not say she had proved it. She called it an idea that fitted better, and asked others to check.
  --
  They checked, and she was right. The inner core is about 1220 km from its centre to its edge: smaller than the Moon. Lehmann never said what it is made of. Others worked that out later, and by 1971 it was clear: a solid ball of iron, inside a core of liquid iron.
  "inside the core there is an inner core in which the velocity is larger"

READ FURTHER
  One wave, or all of them?
    A real earthquake does not choose a direction. It sends waves every way at once, and each post feels whichever of them arrive there. In the game you follow one wave at a time, because that is the only way to see which direction ends up where. Lehmann did the same with her pencil. “Every direction at once” is the whole earthquake.
  Why only a quiver?
    Many waves come up at most posts, so they shake hard. Only a thin sliver of the steepest waves meets the inner core: in the full picture, about 7 of the 179 waves drawn land in the shadow. So the posts there feel little. Lehmann worked this out with formulas; the game only counts waves.
  The game is kinder than the Earth.
    In the game, any wave that comes up in the shadow makes the nearest ~ post quiver. Really a post only feels a wave that comes up right beside it, and that is very hard to do. The post at 150° is the easy one: any dive from 12.5° to 13.6° comes up beside it. The other three each need a dive that is right to about a tenth of a degree, because near the edge of the inner core a tiny change of direction swings the wave a long way: 14° comes up at 144°, 15° at 122°.
  The Earth in the picture is a sketch.
    It is the one Lehmann drew on purpose: straight paths, one speed in each layer. In the real Earth the paths curve and the shadow sits a little differently, from about 105° to 143°. Her inner core was about 1400 km from centre to edge; today it is measured at 1220 km.
  There was another explanation.
    In 1936 most scientists said the faint waves had crept round the edge of the core. Much of Lehmann’s paper argues against that: the real records showed the ground moving strongly up and down, as it does when a wave comes up steeply from deep below, and creeping waves would not do that. The game leaves that argument out and sets only the two Earths side by side.
  What she did not say.
    Her paper never says the inner core is solid. It gives it one property: waves run faster in it. Francis Birch proposed in 1940 that it is solid iron, and that was shown in 1971 from the way the whole Earth rings after a great earthquake.
