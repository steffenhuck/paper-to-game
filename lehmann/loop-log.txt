VERDICT
  You found the ball.
  You were the earthquake. You fired twelve waves into an Earth of two layers: a mantle, where waves run fast, and a core, where they run slower. Waves that missed the core came out on your own side of the Earth, no further than 113° round. Waves that went through the core were bent so hard that they came out near the far side. The band in between, with the posts from 120° to 150°, never got a wave at all: the shadow. Then you saw what real posts in the shadow had written, and went back in. Diving steeply, you were bent deep inside the core, where no colour changed: you touched a hidden ball six times, and lit all four shadow posts.
  --
  Inge Lehmann could not ride a wave. She had only what the posts wrote down. After the New Zealand earthquake of 16 June 1929, posts inside the shadow had felt a wave: hardly any sideways push, but a clear up-and-down, and stronger the further round they stood. The usual answer was that a little shaking creeps round the edge of the core. She thought that could hardly be right any more. So she drew the Earth you have just played in, with one speed in the mantle and a slower one in the core, and then put a third layer inside it: a ball about 1400 km from its centre to its edge, a bit smaller than the Moon, where waves run a little faster again. Then she worked out by hand where every dive comes up. Waves steeper than 16° meet the ball. A few bounce off it, faintly. The rest go through, are bent away from the middle, and come up between 119° and 180° round: steeply, right across the shadow, and stronger the further round they land. That is what the posts had felt. She did not claim more than that. She called the ball a hypothesis that seemed fairly probable but could not be proved from the records she had, and she ended by asking others to look.
  --
  They looked, and the ball was there. Two years later Gutenberg and Richter worked out its size from the times the waves took: about 1200 km from centre to edge. Today it is measured at about 1220 km. In 1939 Jeffreys showed that creeping waves could not explain the shadow posts at all. One thing Lehmann never said is that the ball is solid: her paper only says that waves run faster in it. That idea came in 1940, from Francis Birch: iron squeezed so hard that it freezes, inside a core of liquid iron. It was shown to be right only in 1971, from the way the whole Earth rings after a great earthquake. So the picture we have now is a rocky mantle, a liquid core, and a solid ball in the middle, the inner core. Lehmann found it by hand, with her records on cardboard cards kept in oatmeal boxes.
