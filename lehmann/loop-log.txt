VERDICT
  Your ball does what hers does.
  You were the earthquake. You fired 12 waves into an Earth made of two layers: a mantle, where waves run fast, and a core, where they run slower. Each time you chose the direction, and the listening posts told you where your wave came out. Waves that missed the core came out on your side of the Earth, no further than 113° round. Waves that went through the core were bent so hard they came out near the far side. And a band in between, from 120° to 150° round, never got a wave at all: the shadow. Then you built a smaller ball inside the core where waves run a little faster in the ball than in the core, and fired a quake at every angle: all four shadow posts felt it.
  --
  In 1936 the posts inside the shadow had felt something after all: weak, but real, and the ground there bounced mostly up and down, the way it does when a wave comes up steeply. Most scientists said the waves had crept round the outside of the core. Inge Lehmann worked out, by hand, that creeping waves could not come up steeply and would not get stronger further round. So she tried another idea: a smaller ball inside the core, a bit smaller than the Moon (about 1400 km from its centre to its edge), where waves run a little faster in it than in the core around it. A wave that hits faster rock at a glancing angle bounces off it, and her bounced waves come up from about 119° round: right inside the shadow, steeply, and stronger the further round they land. That matched what the posts had felt. She wrote that the idea seemed likely, but could not be proved with what she had.
  --
  She was right. Two years later Gutenberg and Richter worked out the ball’s size from the times the waves took: about 1200 km from centre to edge. In 1939 Jeffreys showed that creeping waves could not explain the shadow posts at all. For about twenty years people argued over whether waves really run faster in the ball; they do. By 1946 everyone agreed on the picture we still have: a rocky mantle, a liquid core, and a solid ball in the middle, the inner core. Lehmann found it with cardboard cards, oatmeal boxes and a pencil, in her garden in Copenhagen, in a job where research was not even part of her duties.
  "We take it that, as before, the Earth consists of a core and a mantle, but that inside the core there is an inner core in which the velocity is larger than in the outer one."
