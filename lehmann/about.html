Find Your Inner Core with Lehmann

About

Built from Inge Lehmann, “P′”, Publications du Bureau Central Séismologique International, Série A, Travaux Scientifiques 14:87–115 (1936), read in full from a scan of the original. For what came after, and for a guided reading, see Martina Kölbl-Ebert, “Inge Lehmann’s paper: ‘P′’ (1936)”, Episodes 24(4):262–267, December 2001, at episodes.org. Game built from the paper; the paper is the authority where the two disagree.

What the paper tells

Earthquake waves arrive late on the far side of the Earth, and in a band of the far side the ordinary ones hardly arrive at all. By 1936 this was explained by a core of slower material: waves entering it are bent toward the middle. Lehmann opens by drawing the paths exactly, on an Earth simplified on purpose to two shells of constant speed, 10 km/s in the mantle and 8 km/s in a core five ninths of the Earth’s radius: straight rays, bent only at the boundary. The ray that just grazes the core leaves at 33.75° and comes up 112.5° round; the core rays come back no nearer than 153.9°. Nothing lands in between.

But stations inside the real shadow did record a wave, which she calls P′3. It had been thought small. She shows that it is not: the sideways movement is small, but the up-and-down movement can be considerable, and it grows with distance. Her Figure 3 prints the records of the Buller earthquake in New Zealand, 16 June 1929, from Irkutsk (110.8°), Sverdlovsk (135.0°) and Baku (137.4°). The accepted story was diffraction: waves creeping round the edge of the core. She writes that this can hardly be considered probable any more, and suggests a third shell: an inner core, radius 8/10·r0·sin 16°, about 1400 km, in which waves run faster, 8.6 km/s. Rays leaving at 16° just touch it; between 16° and 14.86° they are totally reflected, a ray she calls of quite small intensity; steeper rays pass through and come up from 118.8° round out to 180°, steeply, and stronger with distance. Those are the characteristics observed. She offers it as a hypothesis that cannot be proved from the data at hand. The second half of the paper reads four stations near 150° for the same earthquake and ends by asking other investigators to look for suitable material.

The paper never says that the inner core is solid; it gives it one property, a higher speed. Gutenberg and Richter computed its size two years later; Jeffreys showed in 1939 that diffraction could not account for the records; Birch proposed in 1940 that it is solid, and Dziewonski and Gilbert showed it in 1971 from the free oscillations of the Earth.

What the game does

It sets two possibilities side by side: an Earth with one core, and an Earth with a core within the core. The listening posts carry what was recorded: every post felt the earthquake, four of them only faintly. You are the wave. In the first stage you fire eight waves into the Earth with one core and watch where each comes up; none can reach the four faint posts, and the full fan shows that no dive ever can. A card states Lehmann’s idea. In the second stage you fire four waves into the Earth with a core within the core; a dive steeper than 16° meets the inner core, is bent away from the middle or bounces off, and comes up inside the shadow. The full fan then reaches every post, the four in the shadow by only a few waves. You say which Earth fits the records, and the verdict tells you what Lehmann said.

The mechanism, and what it claims

Kind: ported. The game runs Lehmann’s own model: straight rays, one constant speed per shell, the law of refraction at each boundary, total reflection where the sine would exceed one, and the epicentral distance summed from the chord geometry. Every number she gives comes out of it: the grazing ray at 33.75° coming up at 112.5°, the same ray refracted to 186.2°, the core rays running back to 153.9°, the ray at 16° touching the inner core and coming up at 160.7°, total reflection from 14.86° coming up at 118.8°, and her Table II of rays through the inner core, to the tenth of a degree.

The game demonstrates what the two model Earths predict: with one core, a band of the far side that no wave reaches; with a core within the core, a few steep waves turned into that band.

The records on the posts are ours, a simplification of what the paper reports. The paper says that waves are observed inside the real shadow with smaller amplitudes, and prints three such records of the 1929 Buller earthquake (Irkutsk, Sverdlovsk, Baku). The game turns that into “four posts felt it faintly” and places those four where the model’s shadow is. The real shadow runs from about 105° to 143°, not 112.5° to 153.9°; she notes that her simplified curves deviate from the observed ones. The game’s reason for the faintness, that few waves land there, stands for her intensity calculation and is not that calculation.

The game assumes the model’s simplifications, which Lehmann chose on purpose: straight rays where the real Earth’s curve, one speed per shell where speed really changes with depth, and no loss of energy at any boundary. The posts every ten degrees, the three-degree ear within which a wave reaches a post, and the eight and four waves are ours.

What the game leaves out of the argument. In 1936 the faint waves had another explanation: diffraction, waves creeping round the edge of the core. Much of the paper argues against it, from the strong up-and-down movement of the records and their growth with distance. The game sets only the two Earths against each other, and so makes the choice look easier than it was. In the paper, too, the reflected wave is faint and the wave through the inner core is the one that matches the records; in the game both simply come up in the shadow.

The paper establishes that, in the two-shell model, no ray lands between 112.5° and 153.9°; that an inner core of about 1400 km with a higher speed sends rays into that band from 118.8° on, arriving steeply; and that this fits what the stations in the shadow recorded better than diffraction does. It does not establish that the inner core exists: Lehmann says so herself.

Where the discovery comes from. The shadow and its filling follow from the model’s geometry, not from our rules: the calibration check shows the same four posts unreached with the ear at one, two, three or four degrees, and the same posts reached with her inner core; with the posts shifted by five degrees the story is the same except that a post at 115°, in the sliver between 112.5° and 118.8° that her own model leaves dark, stays unreached.

The numbers

Every number sits in the config block at the top of the mechanism, tagged with its page: the radii and speeds of the three shells, the paper’s own ray results and Table II used by the check, the three stations of Figure 3, the later dates, and everything ours (posts, ear, waves, the words).

What is left out

Lehmann’s travel-time curves and intensity calculations; the S-waves; the curved rays of the real Earth beyond a line here; her layer of gradually decreasing speed that explains the weak waves just beyond 100°; the second half’s reading of the records near 150°; most of the biography.
