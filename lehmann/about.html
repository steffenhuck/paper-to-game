Lehmann’s Shadow

About

Built from Martina Kölbl-Ebert, “Inge Lehmann’s paper: ‘P′’ (1936)”, Episodes 24(4):262–267, December 2001 (Classic Paper in the History of Geology), which summarises and quotes Inge Lehmann, “P′”, Publications du Bureau Central Séismologique International, Série A, 14:87–115 (1936). The Episodes article is at episodes.org, volume 24, issue 4. Game built from this paper; the paper is the authority where the two disagree.

What the paper tells

Earthquake waves arrive late on the far side of the Earth, and in a band of the far side the ordinary ones hardly arrive at all. By 1936 this was explained by a core of slower rock: waves entering it are bent toward the middle, so none comes up between about 112° and 154° round from the quake. Lehmann drew the paths exactly, on a model Earth simplified to two shells of constant speed, 10 km/s in the mantle and 8 km/s in a core five ninths of the Earth’s radius: straight rays, bent only at the boundary. Her Figure 1, redrawn as Figure 2a of the article, is the fan of those rays with the empty wedge of the shadow in it.

But seismographs inside the shadow did record something: faint waves, mostly up-and-down, which she called P′3, stronger the further round the station stood. The accepted story was that waves crept round the outside of the core. That could not give a strong vertical movement growing with distance. Lehmann proposed instead a third shell: an inner core, radius 8/10·r0·sin 16°, about 1400 km, in which waves run faster, 8.6 km/s. Rays meeting it at a shallow slant are totally reflected and come up from 118.8° round; rays going through it come up from the same point out to the far side. The shadow is filled. She wrote that the hypothesis seemed probable and could not be proved from the data at hand. Part II of her paper reads four stations near 150° recording the 1929 Buller earthquake in New Zealand, where four pulses appear instead of two or three. Two years later Gutenberg and Richter computed an inner core of about 1200 km; in 1939 Jeffreys showed that the creeping explanation could not hold; the inner core was argued over for twenty years and found to be solid by Birch and Bullen in the 1940s.

She did all of it by hand, on cardboard cards kept in oatmeal boxes in her garden in Copenhagen, with no assistants and no computer, at a geodetic institute that did not count research as part of her job.

What the game does

You are the wave. A dial sets how steeply it dives into Lehmann’s two-shell Earth; you fire it and watch it run straight, bend at the core, and come up somewhere on the rim, where eighteen listening posts stand every ten degrees. Each fired wave stays drawn, so your shots build her Figure 2a, and the four posts from 120° to 150° that no angle reaches are the shadow. When you declare that you have reached every post you can, the whole fan fires and the shadow is named. Then the twist: those posts heard something. You build Lehmann’s inner ball with two dials, its size and its speed against the core’s, and a quake fires at every angle at once; you have four quakes to make the four shadow posts hear it. The verdict says what your ball did, what hers did, and what became of her idea.

The mechanism, and what it claims

Kind: ported. The game runs Lehmann’s own model as the article reports it: straight rays, one constant speed per shell, the law of refraction at each boundary, total reflection where the sine would exceed one, and the epicentral distance summed from the chord geometry. Every number she gives comes out of it: the grazing ray at 33.75° coming up at 112.5°, the same ray refracted to 186.2°, the core rays running back to 153.9° at about 26°, the ray at 16° touching the inner core and coming up at 160.7°, total reflection from 14.85° coming up at 118.8°.

The game demonstrates what this model does: that a slower core leaves a band of the far side unreachable by any angle, and that a smaller, faster ball inside it bounces waves into that band and arrives steeply. It also shows, honestly, what the model does with a much slower ball: waves plunge through it, cross the far point and come round into the band from the other side.

The game assumes the model’s simplifications, which Lehmann chose on purpose: straight rays where the real Earth’s curve upward, one speed per shell where speed really rises with depth, and no loss of energy at any boundary. The posts every ten degrees, the three-degree ear within which a post hears a wave, the ranges of the two dials and the four quakes are ours. The drum’s up-and-down is the cosine of the arrival angle, which equals the dive in this symmetric model; it stands for the vertical component the article discusses, not for a computed amplitude.

The paper establishes that, in the two-shell model, no ray lands between 112.5° and 153.9°; that an inner core of about 1400 km with a higher speed sends rays into that band from 118.8° on, arriving steeply; and that this fits what the stations in the shadow recorded, where diffraction does not. It does not establish that the inner core exists or how fast it is: Lehmann says so herself, and the proof came from others over the following decade. The real shadow runs from about 105° to 143°, not 112.5° to 153.9°; the article says the simplified model’s curves “deviate from those empirically determined.”

Where the discovery comes from. The shadow and its filling follow from the model’s geometry, not from our rules: the calibration check shows the same four posts dark with the ear at one, two, three or four degrees, and the same posts lit by her ball, and with the posts shifted by five degrees the story is the same except that a post at 115°, in the sliver between 112.5° and 118.8° that her own model leaves dark, stays dark under her ball too. That a slightly slower ball leaves the shadow dark and a much slower one fills it from the far side are also the model’s results, and the verdict names the second when a player finds it.

The numbers

Every number sits in the config block at the top of the mechanism, tagged with its page: the radii and speeds of the three shells, the article’s own ray results used by the check, the observed limits of the real shadow, Gutenberg and Richter’s 1200 km, and everything ours (posts, ear, dials, quakes, the Moon as a yardstick for the ball’s size, the words).

What is left out

Lehmann’s energy and amplitude calculations; the S-waves; the curved rays of the real Earth beyond a line here; Part II’s reading of the Buller records beyond the verdict’s epilogue; the diffraction debate beyond a clause; most of the biography.
