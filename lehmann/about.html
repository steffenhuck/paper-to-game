Lehmann’s Shadow

About

Built from Inge Lehmann, “P′”, Publications du Bureau Central Séismologique International, Série A, Travaux Scientifiques 14:87–115 (1936), read in full from a scan of the original. For what came after, and for a guided reading, see Martina Kölbl-Ebert, “Inge Lehmann’s paper: ‘P′’ (1936)”, Episodes 24(4):262–267, December 2001, at episodes.org. Game built from the paper; the paper is the authority where the two disagree.

What the paper tells

Earthquake waves arrive late on the far side of the Earth, and in a band of the far side the ordinary ones hardly arrive at all. By 1936 this was explained by a core of slower material: waves entering it are bent toward the middle. Lehmann opens by drawing the paths exactly, on an Earth simplified on purpose to two shells of constant speed, 10 km/s in the mantle and 8 km/s in a core five ninths of the Earth’s radius: straight rays, bent only at the boundary. The ray that just grazes the core leaves at 33.75° and comes up 112.5° round; the core rays come back no nearer than 153.9°. Nothing lands in between.

But stations inside the real shadow did record a wave, which she calls P′3. It had been thought small. She shows that it is not: the sideways movement is small, but the up-and-down movement can be considerable, and it grows with distance. Her Figure 3 prints the records of the Buller earthquake in New Zealand, 16 June 1929, from Irkutsk (110.8°), Sverdlovsk (135.0°) and Baku (137.4°). The accepted story was diffraction: waves creeping round the edge of the core. She writes that this can hardly be considered probable any more, and suggests a third shell: an inner core, radius 8/10·r0·sin 16°, about 1400 km, in which waves run faster, 8.6 km/s. Rays leaving at 16° just touch it; between 16° and 14.86° they are totally reflected, a ray she calls of quite small intensity; steeper rays pass through and come up from 118.8° round out to 180°, steeply, and stronger with distance. Those are the characteristics observed. She offers it as a hypothesis that cannot be proved from the data at hand. The second half of the paper reads four stations near 150° for the same earthquake and ends by asking other investigators to look for suitable material.

The paper never says that the inner core is solid; it gives it one property, a higher speed. Gutenberg and Richter computed its size two years later; Jeffreys showed in 1939 that diffraction could not account for the records; Birch proposed in 1940 that it is solid, and Dziewonski and Gilbert showed it in 1971 from the free oscillations of the Earth.

What the game does

You are the wave. A dial sets how steeply it dives into Lehmann’s two-shell Earth; you fire it and watch it run straight, bend at the core, and come up somewhere on the rim, where eighteen listening posts stand every ten degrees. After six waves the game rings one post, at 130°, and asks you to hit it; no dive can. After the twelfth wave the whole fan fires and the shadow is named. A card then shows what three real posts wrote in 1929, with the clue the paper turns on: the ground went up and down, so the wave came up steeply. You go back into the same Earth with twelve more waves. Lehmann’s inner core is in it but not drawn: a steep wave is bent where no colour changes, leaves a white mark on the ball’s edge, and comes up inside the shadow. When the four shadow posts are lit, or the waves run out, the ball is drawn and the fan fires through three shells.

The mechanism, and what it claims

Kind: ported. The game runs Lehmann’s own model: straight rays, one constant speed per shell, the law of refraction at each boundary, total reflection where the sine would exceed one, and the epicentral distance summed from the chord geometry. Every number she gives comes out of it: the grazing ray at 33.75° coming up at 112.5°, the same ray refracted to 186.2°, the core rays running back to 153.9°, the ray at 16° touching the inner core and coming up at 160.7°, total reflection from 14.86° coming up at 118.8°, and her Table II of rays through the inner core, to the tenth of a degree.

The game demonstrates what this model does: that a slower core leaves a band of the far side unreachable by any angle, and that a smaller, faster ball inside it sends steep waves into that band.

The game assumes the model’s simplifications, which Lehmann chose on purpose: straight rays where the real Earth’s curve, one speed per shell where speed really changes with depth, and no loss of energy at any boundary. The posts every ten degrees, the three-degree ear within which a post feels a wave, the two sets of twelve waves and the job at 130° are ours. The drum’s up-and-down is the cosine of the arrival angle, which equals the dive in this symmetric model; it stands for the vertical component the paper discusses, not for a computed amplitude. The three records on the card are sketches after her Figure 3, sized by eye, not the real traces.

Where the game goes beyond the paper. In the game you see your own path, so you find the ball by being bent. Lehmann had no paths, only arrival times and the look of the records, and she inferred the ball from those. In the game the reflected waves light posts as brightly as the transmitted ones; in the paper the reflected wave is faint and it is the transmitted wave that matches the records. The verdict says both.

The paper establishes that, in the two-shell model, no ray lands between 112.5° and 153.9°; that an inner core of about 1400 km with a higher speed sends rays into that band from 118.8° on, arriving steeply; and that this fits what the stations in the shadow recorded better than diffraction does. It does not establish that the inner core exists: Lehmann says so herself. The real shadow runs from about 105° to 143°, not 112.5° to 153.9°; she notes that her simplified curves deviate from the observed ones.

Where the discovery comes from. The shadow and its filling follow from the model’s geometry, not from our rules: the calibration check shows the same four posts dark with the ear at one, two, three or four degrees, and the same posts lit by her ball; with the posts shifted by five degrees the story is the same except that a post at 115°, in the sliver between 112.5° and 118.8° that her own model leaves dark, stays dark.

The numbers

Every number sits in the config block at the top of the mechanism, tagged with its page: the radii and speeds of the three shells, the paper’s own ray results and Table II used by the check, the three stations of Figure 3, the later dates, and everything ours (posts, ear, waves, the job, the Moon as a yardstick for the ball’s size, the words).

What is left out

Lehmann’s travel-time curves and intensity calculations; the S-waves; the curved rays of the real Earth beyond a line here; her layer of gradually decreasing speed that explains the weak waves just beyond 100°; the second half’s reading of the records near 150°; most of the biography.
