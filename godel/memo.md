# Gödel's Loophole — design memo

Gödel (1931), On formally undecidable propositions, as chalk on a green board: a rulebook for whole numbers that proves or refutes sentences; shapes with a hole in them, numbered; fill a hole, run the rulebook, and watch the chalk settle it. Write a shape of your own that points at its own number, and the chalk never stops: a sentence the rulebook can neither prove nor refute, and which is true. Then try to close the loophole three ways, and watch each fail the way the paper says.

Idea given: "maybe some kind of animated blackboard SVGs … discard if there is a better solution." Taken: the register is chalk on a blackboard, and the chalk writes, ticks, crosses and loops as animated strokes. Where it overrode precedent: the skill's rule "after a dark screen, a light one" (the previous game, *Leibniz's Worlds*, is a night sky) is overridden by the Given; to keep the distance, the board is slate green rather than black, it sits in a wooden frame on a plaster wall, and nothing on it glows.

## 1. Paper

Kurt Gödel, "Über formal unentscheidbare Sätze der Principia Mathematica und verwandter Systeme I", *Monatshefte für Mathematik und Physik* 38 (1931): 173–198. DOI 10.1007/BF01700692. Received 17 November 1930. What was available: a typeset English translation (B. Meltzer's, 1962, as circulated in a PDF; the bracketed numbers [173]–[198] are the original pagination), 37 pages, the whole paper: the informal sketch (§1), the system P and the 46 recursive definitions (§2), Propositions V–XI, and the closing note promising a Part II that was never needed. No code, no data; the paper is an argument and a construction, and the game is built from the argument. The German original can be read at the Monatshefte (Springer) and in Gödel's *Collected Works* I (Oxford, 1986), which also has the standard van Heijenoort translation.

## 2. What the paper hands over

**Primary: an argument that is also a construction.** Every formula of the system is a finite string of basic signs; assign the signs numbers and strings become numbers; so "formula", "proof", "provable" become properties of numbers, and the system can talk about them (§1, §2 definitions 1–46; Proposition V: every recursive relation is representable). Then the construction of §1: list the formulas with one free variable (the "class-signs") as R(1), R(2), …; write [R(n); n] for the n-th one with its own number put in its hole; let K be the set of n for which [R(n); n] is *not* provable; "n is in K" is itself a class-sign, say R(q); then [R(q); q] says of itself that it is not provable. If it were provable it would be true, so not provable; if its negation were provable, it would be provable too (given ω-consistency). So it is undecidable, and, being unprovable, true (Proposition VI, and the paragraph after footnote 15). The construction works for any extension by a recursive class of axioms, so adding the sentence as an axiom only produces a new one (p. 190–191); adding its negation gives a consistent but ω-inconsistent system, which asserts that a proof exists and refutes every candidate (p. 61 of the translation, [189]). And a system that could prove its own consistency could prove the undecidable sentence, so it cannot (Proposition XI).

**Secondary: nothing quantitative.** The only numbers are the sign codes (0 → 1, f → 3, ~ → 5, ∨ → 7, Π → 9, ( → 11, ) → 13, variables as primes above 13 to a power) and the prime-exponent coding of strings. They are shown on the About screen and not played.

## 3. The discovery, and its kind

**A counterintuitive result, delivered by a mechanism made intelligible, in the "every attempt must fail" shape.** The stranger's belief, which was Hilbert's: write down the right rules for whole numbers and every question about them can be settled, proved or refuted, by grinding through the rules. The paper: no rulebook can do it. There is always a sentence it can neither prove nor refute, that sentence is true, and it can be built by hand: make a sentence that talks about the rulebook, leave a hole in it, and fill the hole with the sentence's own number. The second half: every repair fails in a way the paper names. Add the sentence as a rule and the new rulebook has a loophole of its own; add its opposite and the rulebook starts claiming that a proof exists while rejecting every proof you show it; ask the rulebook to certify that it never contradicts itself and it cannot, because that certificate would prove the loophole.

## 4. The core narrative

There is a book of rules for doing sums, and a clerk who settles questions with it: give the clerk a sentence about numbers and the clerk works through the rules until it can put a tick (proved) or a cross (refuted). Hilbert said: with a good enough book the clerk settles everything. Gödel found a trick. He numbered all the sentences that have a hole in them, 1, 2, 3, … Then he wrote one more: "the clerk does not tick sentence number □ with its own number in the hole." Say that one is number 6. Now put 6 in the hole: "the clerk does not tick sentence 6 with 6 in the hole." That sentence is talking about itself. If the clerk ticks it, the clerk has ticked something false. If the clerk crosses it, the clerk is saying it ticks it, which is false too. So the clerk can do neither, and the sentence, which says the clerk won't tick it, is true. You can add it to the book as a new rule, but then the clerk has a new book and Gödel writes the trick again for the new book. You can add the opposite instead, and then the clerk claims there is a page in the old book that proves the sentence, and crosses every page you show it. And you can ask the clerk to certify that the book never contradicts itself, and the clerk cannot: that certificate would prove the very sentence it cannot prove.

## 5. Who lives in the paper

- **The system P, the rulebook** (axioms I–V and the rules of inference; any recursive extension c). Controls nothing; it settles. Could be the player as subject: receive sentences, prove or refute, meet the one that breaks you.
- **The constructor, Gödel.** Controls which class-sign to write and which number to substitute. Anticipates: what the rulebook will do with a sentence about itself.
- **The axiom-adder, Hilbert's heir.** Controls which sentence to add to the rulebook (17 Gen r, its negation, or a consistency statement). Anticipates: whether the loophole closes.
- **The coder.** Controls the map from signs to numbers (Φ). The paper's own instrument, handed over.
- **The class-signs R(n)**, the sentences with a hole, numbered. The things listed; one of them is the loophole.
- **The sentence 17 Gen r** itself: "asserts its own unprovability". Could be played as a subject with no choice.
- **The proof-pages** (x B y: x is a proof of y): the ledger the rulebook reads, which the ω-inconsistent rulebook claims a page of and then denies page by page.

## 7. The aim and the verdict

**Aim**, the line on the start screen, at the top of every play screen, and first in How to play: *Find a sentence the rulebook can neither prove nor refute. Then try to close the loophole.*

**Verdict**, the end screen's first lines, in words:

- *What the paper found.* Any rulebook for whole numbers that works by fixed rules has a sentence it can neither prove nor refute, and the sentence is true. Add it as a rule and the new rulebook has a loophole of its own. Add its opposite and the rulebook says a page proves it and crosses every page. And no such rulebook can certify that it never contradicts itself.
- *What you found.* The loophole on your N-th sentence, by your own hand or by Gödel's; whether you tried the sentence that praises itself first (the rulebook proves that one); which rule you added first and what the rulebook did; Rulebook 2's loophole on your first try or not; and your bet on the certificate.
- *Where you part.* Where the player let Gödel write it, or bet that the rulebook would certify itself.
- *Had yours stood.* Had the rulebook ticked its own certificate, it would have ticked the loophole, and a rulebook that ticks a false sentence contradicts itself: the only rulebook that certifies itself is a broken one.

The yardstick during play is in the aim's units: sentences run, settled, unsettled. No score.

## 8. Three concepts

**A. Gödel's Loophole** (agent: the writer of sentences and rules; discovery, then every fix fails). Controls: tap a shape on the board, tap a number to put in its hole, run the rulebook; later, three rows of chalk chips to write shape 6 ("proves / does not prove", "shape □ or 1–5", "filled with □ or 1–5"); later still, a choice of rule to add, and a bet. Deliberation: first, which number in which shape might stump it (none does: it computes); then, the rulebook settles shapes 4 and 5, which are *about* the rulebook, so talking about it is not enough; then, what if a shape talks about *itself*: the shape cannot contain its own number before it is written, unless the number is left as a hole and the hole is filled with the shape's number; and which way round, "proves" or "does not prove". Discovery moment: the player fills shape 6 with 6, the chalk writes "to settle this, first settle: shape 6 filled with 6, which is this very sentence", and a pink loop draws from the sentence back to its start and keeps going; then the game says: it cannot prove it, and what it says is that it cannot prove it, so it is true. Fun: discovery (the loop), agency (every fill settles in front of you, by a rule you can read), mastery (Rulebook 2's loophole found at once), anticipation (the fixes). Second playthrough: the other order of fixes; the self-praising sentence if you did not try it; otherwise low, and the game is short.

**B. Gödel's Code** (experimenter with the paper's own instrument; the reckless one). The board shows the paper's code table (0 → 1, f → 3, ~ → 5, ∨ → 7, Π → 9, brackets 11 and 13, variables as primes) and a short formula. Act one: encode it by hand, sign by sign, the number growing as a product of prime powers on the board (2^a · 3^b · 5^c …, shown as a tower). Act two, the mechanism run backwards: a number is given and the player factorises it by tapping primes and reads off the signs, to find a sentence that contains its own number. Deliberation: which prime, which power. Discovery: the decoded sentence names the number it came from. Fun: mastery of a mechanical skill; the moment of decoding. Replay: new formulas. Half expected to fail because the numbers are enormous after four signs (2^1 · 3^3 · 5^5 is already 843,750), the player's actions are arithmetic on a phone, and the self-naming sentence cannot be produced by the player's hand at this size: it has to be given, which puts the discovery back into a line the builder wrote.

**C. You Are the Rulebook** (subject; the player settles sentences). Sentences arrive one at a time on the board; the player, reading the rules on the board's edge, ticks or crosses each, and the rules are small enough to follow (even, prime, bigger than). Then sentences about the player's own ticks arrive ("you ticked the second sentence"), settleable by looking back. Then: "you will not tick this sentence." Tick it and the game shows you have ticked a false sentence; cross it and you have claimed you tick it; a third button, "leave it", is incompleteness. Deliberation: real, at the last sentence, and small before it. Discovery: whichever you press is wrong, and the failing is shown as the result. Fun: anticipation, discovery. Replay: none. Given up as the spine because the first eight sentences are a quiz with answers the player can see, and because the liar sentence arrives ready-made: the stranger says "that is a trick sentence, not arithmetic", and the game cannot answer, since the player did not build it. Its last sentence survives as the chalk's own reasoning in A: "if I tick it … if I cross it …".

## 10. The pick, and why

**A, Gödel's Loophole.** The player writes the paper's own variable, the class-sign S and the number q, and runs the paper's own machine, Bew, on it; the self-praising sentence is a live wrong branch (the rulebook proves it) and the fixed-shape branches are live too (they settle); the counterintuitive moment arrives as a loop drawn by the rulebook, not as a line written by the builder; and the second act plays the three repairs the paper itself discusses, each failing differently. The sentence afterwards has no "guess": *I wrote a sentence about the rulebook with a hole in it, filled the hole with the sentence's own number, and the rulebook could neither prove nor refute it; and every rule I added to fix that gave me a new one.* Originality: no game in the table has had the player compose a sentence from parts, or run a proof-checker, or watch a loophole regrow.

**Author test.** Gödel would recognise R(n), [R(n); n], K, S = R(q), Bew, the extension clause, the ω-inconsistent c′ and Proposition XI; the toy rulebook's reading of its own ledger is his Proposition V in a generous form. A logician playing it has one thing to find that the paper does not say: the self-praising sentence is proved, which is Löb's theorem of 1955, and the About screen says so.

**Runner-up: C, You Are the Rulebook.** Switch if the author wants the stranger to feel the trap in their own hand rather than watch it happen to a machine; its last sentence and its three buttons are the chalk's reasoning in A, and it could be an opening scene of A if the first act runs long.

**What the reckless concept would have given.** B's prime tower, the paper's actual mechanism for turning sentences into numbers, which A replaces with "the shape's number is its line on the board", as the paper's own §1 sketch does. Kept from it: the code table on the About screen and a line on the board that the number of a shape stands for the shape. Given up: the factorising, the enormous numbers, the decoding.

## 11. The shape

Two acts on one board, then a verdict. Rounds are the player's runs.

- **Act I, scene 1: the rulebook.** Shapes 1–5 on the board; the player runs sentences freely (shape, number, run). After three runs the chalk box *Write shape 6* appears. Each run teaches: number facts settle; shapes about the rulebook settle too.
- **Act I, scene 2: shape 6.** The composer; six runs. A run that settles rubs out the result and the player may rewrite shape 6 or refill it. The loophole ends the scene. If six runs pass without it, Gödel's chalk writes shape 6 ("does not prove, shape □, filled with □") and the player still fills and runs it; the verdict says so.
- **Act I, scene 3: the loophole.** The loop, the game's statement that the sentence is true, and *Close the loophole*.
- **Act II, round 1: a rule.** Choose which sentence to add, the loophole or its opposite. Added, the consequence plays: for the loophole, shape 7 appears and the player has three runs to find Rulebook 2's loophole (Gödel fills it after three); for the opposite, the player shows pages of the ledger (their own runs), each refuted, and the chalk names ω-inconsistency.
- **Act II, round 2: the other rule.** The same, the other way.
- **Act II, round 3: the certificate.** A bet (will Rulebook 1 prove that it never contradicts itself?), then the run, then the chain.
- **Verdict.** In words; then the paper's lines; Play again; How to play; About.

Presets are the paper's own regimes: the base system (Rulebook 1), c + 17 Gen r (Rulebook 2), c + Neg(17 Gen r) (Rulebook 2′), and Wid(c) (the certificate). No score. Target five to seven minutes.

## 12. Controls, mechanism, feedback, moment

- **Controls.** Act I: tap a shape (1–6), tap a number (1–6), *Run the rulebook*; the composer's three rows of chips and *Write it on the board*. Act II: two rule boxes and *Add it*; *Page 1*, *Page 2* …; the bet's two boxes and *Run*.
- **Mechanism (toy; `mechanism.js`).** Shapes are either number facts (even, prime, bigger than 3) or sentences about a rulebook: "Rulebook e [proves | does not prove] shape A filled with B", where A and B are each a hole (□, taking the number filled in) or a fixed number. A rulebook has an edition and a list of added rules. To settle shape s filled with n, a rulebook works it out: number facts by arithmetic; a sentence about Rulebook e by settling the inner sentence with Rulebook e and reading the result off its ledger (proved → "proves" is proved; refuted → "proves" is refuted; "does not prove" the other way round); an added rule settles its sentence outright. If settling the inner sentence requires the sentence being settled (a cycle), the cycle is read: if every link in it is "proves", the sentence is proved (it says only that it is proved, which proving makes true); if any link is "does not prove", it is unsettled, and so is every sentence that depends on it. A rulebook with the certificate of an earlier edition among its rules proves that edition's loophole (Proposition XI's chain); no rulebook proves its own certificate.
- **Feedback: the chalk hand.** The rulebook's working appears in white chalk, line by line; the mark draws itself: a tick, a cross, or a pink loop from the sentence's end back to its start that keeps moving. A counter in the aim's units: sentences run, settled, unsettled.
- **The moment.** Shape 6 filled with 6: "Shape 6 filled with 6 is: this very sentence." The loop. Then the game's own line: it cannot prove it; it says it cannot prove it; so it is true.

## 14. What is cut

The arithmetisation itself (the sign codes and prime-exponent coding; shown on About, not played); the 46 recursive definitions and Proposition V (the toy rulebook simply "works things out" and "reads its ledger"); types and the system P's axioms; ω-consistency as a condition (shown only as the pathology of Rulebook 2′, never as a term the player needs); Propositions VII–X (arithmetical and predicate-calculus forms); Rosser's 1936 strengthening; the sequel that was never written. Shape numbering is by line on the board, as in the paper's §1 sketch, not by code.

## 15. Divergences

- **Shapes are numbered by their line on the board,** not by Gödel's code. The paper's §1 does the same ("we think of the class-signs as being somehow arranged in a series"); the code is what makes the series expressible inside the system, and the toy takes that for granted.
- **The rulebook reads its own ledger.** The toy lets a rulebook certify "does not prove S" for any sentence S it has refuted. The real system can certify "proves S" when it does (Σ1 completeness) but cannot certify "does not prove S" for any S without certifying its own consistency, which Proposition XI forbids. The toy rulebook is therefore *stronger* than the paper's, and the loophole opens all the same; the About screen says this.
- **The self-praising sentence is proved.** A sentence that says only "Rulebook 1 proves this sentence" gets a tick, with the reason "if I prove it, it says I prove it". That is Löb's theorem (1955), not in the paper; it is in the game as the live wrong branch of the composer. A cycle with any "does not prove" link is unsettled, which matches the real system (proving any such sentence would prove consistency).
- **Adding the certificate as a rule** is not in the paper; the game only runs the certificate and shows the chain of Proposition XI.
- **"Page k of the ledger proves S"** stands for x B y; the pages are the player's own runs, so that the ω-inconsistent rulebook rejects pages the player has seen. The paper's pages are all proofs, in number order.
- **The budget** (three runs before the composer opens; six runs in the composer; three to find Rulebook 2's loophole) is ours, so that the game ends.
- **"Proves / refutes / unsettled"** for "provable / negation provable / undecidable"; "contradicts itself" for inconsistent; "ω-inconsistent" is said once, as the paper's word, after the pathology has been seen.

## 16. Mechanism kind and claim boundaries

**Toy.** The game runs a model built to illustrate the paper's argument. A rulebook is a settler with an edition and a list of added rules: it works out number facts (even, prime, bigger than 3) by arithmetic; it settles a sentence about a rulebook ("Rulebook e proves / does not prove shape A filled with B") by settling the inner sentence with Rulebook e and reading the verdict off that rulebook's ledger; an added rule settles its sentence outright, and a rule of an edition holds in every later edition built on it. If settling needs the very sentence being settled (a circle), the circle is read: all links "proves" → proved (the self-praising sentence; Löb 1955); any link "does not prove" → unsettled, and so is every sentence that depends on an unsettled one. The certificate of edition e is settled by the chain of Proposition XI: it would prove e's loophole, which e cannot prove.

- **Demonstrates:** that a rulebook which can read its own ledger is still stumped by a shape that refers to its own line through a hole; that the sentence is true; that c + G has a loophole of the same shape (and so on, edition after edition); that c + ¬G asserts a page and refutes every page; that Wid(c) would prove G.
- **Assumes:** the toy above. Two assumptions are generous to the rulebook: it may certify "does not prove S" for any refuted S (the paper's system cannot certify any "no" without Wid; the check runs the game with `GENEROUS_LEDGER = false` and the loophole opens there too, with seven of the ten fixed meta-sentences left unsettled); and the self-praising sentence is proved (Löb, not the paper).
- **The paper establishes:** Propositions VI, VIII and XI for P and every ω-consistent recursive extension, by arithmetisation. Nothing about rulebooks not of that kind; nothing about "incompleteness" beyond one true unprovable sentence per system and no inner consistency proof.
- **The discovery comes from the paper, not from the rules.** The loophole follows from the settling rule applied to a self-referring "does not prove"; the budgets, the chips and the bet are ours and move nothing in the mechanism. The calibration check confirms the loophole is the only unsettled sentence in the composer with other number facts, three fixed shapes, and seven (where a board holding a self-praising shape has a second loophole through a two-sentence circle, which the paper's logic also gives).
