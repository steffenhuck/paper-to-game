Gödel's Endgame

There is a marking robot in the classroom, the Marker. Write any sentence about whole numbers on the board and press one of its two buttons: a tick if the sentence follows from its rules, a cross if the opposite does. The Marker is never wrong: a mark that would come out wrong, it refuses.

Hilbert said: with good enough rules, a Marker like this marks everything. Gödel, twenty-five, picked up the chalk.

Write lines on the board and mark them with the Marker's two buttons. Write one that talks about the Marker. Then try to fix what you find.

Write a line that neither button can mark. Then try to close the loophole.

Gödel (1931), Monatshefte für Mathematik und Physik 38:173–198. A game built from the paper.

    How to play

    Write a line that neither button can mark. Then try to close the loophole.

    The board

    At the top is the Marker: every line on the board gets a tick (the line follows from its rules) or a cross (the opposite does), and the Marker is never wrong. Lines are numbered by their place on the board. Two lines are there when you arrive, one ticked and one crossed. The marks sit at the right of each line.

    Writing a line

    Below the board's lines is the line you are writing, in yellow, and under it the chips that make it. A number: tap a number, then a fact (is even, is a prime, is bigger than 3). About the Marker: tap will tick or will never tick, then a line number; the last chip is the number your new line will get, "this line". Tap Write it on the board and the line goes on the board, waiting for its mark.

    Marking a line

    You press the Marker's buttons: Tick it or Cross it. The Marker writes its working in white chalk; a mark that comes out right goes on the line, and a mark that would come out wrong is refused, with the reason, and you press the other. Tap the working to make the chalk finish. Next line clears the chips. You have six lines; if every one gets a mark, Gödel's chalk writes a line for you, and you still press the buttons.

    The loophole

    A line about the Marker's own mark on that very line is judged by one rule: a mark must come out right. When both buttons are refused, a third appears, Leave it unmarked. A line with no mark is shown with a pink loop that never closes: that is a loophole. The board then says what follows from the line not being ticked.

    Closing the loophole

    Add a rule to Marker 1: the line itself (so it gets a tick by rule), or its opposite (so it gets a cross). Touch a rule to see it written under the Marker's name; Add it makes a new Marker, 2 or 3. With the line as a rule, you have three lines to write Marker 2 a line that neither button can mark. With the opposite as a rule, Marker 3 holds that Marker 1 ticks the line; ask it about day after day and see what it says. Then the other rule. Last, a bet: will Marker 1 tick its own promise, "Marker 1 never marks wrong"? Run it and see.

    The verdict

    In words: what the paper found, what you found, where the two part, and what would have followed had your result stood.

    What the paper says

    By 1930 all of mathematics had been written as a few axioms and a few rules of inference, and it was supposed that the right axioms would settle every question. Gödel shows they cannot. His trick: every sentence of the system is a string of signs; give each sign a number (0 is 1, the successor sign is 3, "not" is 5, "or" is 7, "for all" is 9, the brackets 11 and 13, variables are primes above 13) and a string becomes one number, the product of prime powers 2a·3b·5c…; so "is a formula", "is a proof of", "is provable" become properties of numbers, and the system, which is about numbers, can talk about them (his definitions 1–46 and Proposition V). Then he builds a sentence that says of itself that it is not provable: list the sentences with one hole in them, put each one's own number in its hole, and let the sentence "the sentence with my number, with its number in its hole, is not provable" be one of them. Prove it and the system has proved something false; refute it and the system has proved it provable; so, if the system never proves falsehoods (he needs only a weaker condition, ω-consistency), it does neither, and the sentence, which says it will not be proved, is true (Proposition VI). The construction works again for any system you build by adding axioms, as long as they can be listed by a rule, so adding the sentence only produces a new one. Adding its negation gives a system that never contradicts itself outright but asserts that a proof exists while refuting every candidate: ω-inconsistent. And a system that could prove "I never contradict myself" could prove the undecidable sentence from it, so it cannot prove its own consistency (Proposition XI). This holds for Principia, for set theory, and for ordinary arithmetic with a few rules.

    What the game does

    It puts a marking robot on a board and its two buttons in your hands. Every line gets a tick (it follows from the rules) or a cross (its opposite does); the Marker never marks wrong, so a button that would come out wrong is refused, with the reason, and a third button, Leave it unmarked, appears only when both have been refused. Lines are numbered by their place on the board, as in the paper's opening sketch, and a line may say what a Marker does with a line, including the line it is written on; that is the paper's self-reference, and the prime coding above is what makes "line 6" sayable inside arithmetic, which the game takes for granted. "Marker 1 will tick line 6" written on line 6 is ticked; "Marker 1 will never tick line 6" written on line 6 gets no mark and is true: the paper's undecidable sentence. The second act plays the paper's three repairs: make the line a rule (Marker 2, with an unmarkable line of its own), make its opposite a rule (Marker 3, which swears Marker 1 ticks line 6 and says no to every day you ask about), and ask the Marker to tick the promise that it never marks wrong (the chain of Proposition XI).

    What kind of mechanism this is

    Toy. The game runs a model built to illustrate the paper's argument, and where the argument had to be made small, the toy chose. A Marker works out number facts; it marks a line about another line's mark by reading that mark off the board, and gives no mark to a line about a line that has none; for a line about its own mark on that very line it asks whether a mark would come out right, trying the tick first, and gives no mark if neither would; an added rule marks its line outright, and a rule of an earlier version holds in later ones.

    • What the game demonstrates: that a marker which is never wrong, and can read its own board, still has a line it cannot mark, and that the line is true; that making it a rule gives the next marker a line of the same kind; that making the opposite a rule makes the marker swear a thing happens and deny every day; and that the marker's own promise would let it tick the line it cannot tick.
    • What the game assumes: everything in the paragraph above. Two assumptions are generous to the Marker. First, it may read a cross off its board and tick "Marker 1 will never tick line 2"; the paper's system can certify a proof it has but never a "never" without certifying its own consistency, so the real system has more unmarkable lines than the toy, not fewer (the headless check runs the weaker Marker too, and the loophole opens there as well). Second, the line that says "Marker 1 will tick this line" is ticked; that is Löb's theorem of 1955, which the paper does not treat. "Day k" stands for the paper's relation "x is a proof of y", which the system can check; "will tick" stands for "provable", which it cannot search to the end.
    • What the paper establishes: Propositions VI, VIII and XI for the system P (Principia's logic over the Peano axioms) and for every ω-consistent extension by a recursively definable class of axioms, by the arithmetisation the game does not play. Nothing about markers not of that kind, and nothing about "incompleteness" beyond this: for each such system, one true sentence it cannot prove, and no proof of its own consistency inside it.

    What is cut, and where the game departs

    The coding of signs as numbers, the 46 definitions, the system P's axioms and types, the double substitution of the paper's §1 (a sentence with a hole, filled with its own number; here a line simply names a line, and "this line" is one of the lines it can name), Propositions VII to X, Rosser's 1936 strengthening that removes the ω-consistency condition, and the sequel Gödel announced and never needed to write. "Tick" and "cross" stand for "provable" and "negation provable"; "no mark" for "undecidable"; "marks wrong" for "proves a falsehood"; "never marks wrong" for consistency (the paper's is the weaker "never proves a sentence and its negation"). The budget of lines is the game's, so that the game ends. Earlier builds are kept in previous/: the chalk rulebook with shapes and holes, the four-key printing machine, and the three-act Gödel's Endgame with Hilbert's machine, the prime packing of a sentence into one number, and the sentence with a quoted gap; what this build keeps from them is Hilbert's honest machine with two buttons in the player's hands and a third that appears only when both fail, and what it drops for a younger reader is the packing act (said above instead) and the quoted gap (a line names a line instead).
