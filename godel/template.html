Gödel's Endgame

David Hilbert has a machine that types sentences, some of them about the machine itself. It is honest: it never prints a sentence that is false.

Hilbert bets that, given time, it prints every sentence that is true.

You will type sentences on four keys, read what each one is about, and make one sentence talk about itself.

Win the bet: type a true sentence the machine will never print.

One more thing

Gödel's machine had no keys. It only knew whole numbers.

His rulebook could only say things like “12 is even”. So he gave every sign a number. Tap to turn your sentence into numbers.

Your sentence is one number

Now “the machine prints sentence number such-and-such” is a fact about a number, just like “12 is even”. And TWICE is a sum: from a number, work out the number of the copied sentence. Building PRINTS and TWICE out of plain arithmetic is most of the paper. The four keys were your shortcut.

Read further
  1. Honest is more than Gödel needed. He did not assume the rulebook never says anything false, only that it never contradicts itself (in a slightly stronger form he called ω-consistency). Then it can print neither your sentence nor its opposite. In 1936 Rosser got the same result from plain consistency.
  2. TWICE is Gödel's trick. In the paper, a formula with a gap is filled with its own number. Your box filled with the front of the sentence is exactly that.
  3. The four-key machine is how the logician Raymond Smullyan liked to tell the argument. The game borrows his picture; the paper does it with numbers.
  4. The opposite way round. “The machine prints this very sentence” (PRINTS TWICE [PRINTS TWICE]) is not settled by honesty alone. Löb showed in 1955 that a real rulebook does print it.
  5. Adding the opposite. Hilbert could teach the machine the opposite of your sentence instead. The machine would not contradict itself outright, but it would claim that some proof of your sentence exists and reject every proof it is shown. The paper's word: ω-inconsistent.
  6. The last request is the paper's final proposition, now called the second incompleteness theorem. Gödel sketched it and promised a Part II with the details. It was never needed.

How to play

The aim. Win Hilbert's bet: type a true sentence that his honest machine will never print.

The keys

PRINTS [X] says: the machine prints X.
NOT in front turns “prints” into “never prints”.
TWICE [X] stands for X, then a box with X inside. So PRINTS TWICE [NOT] says: the machine prints NOT [NOT].
The box is the gold field under the machine: type the keys into it as words, with spaces (for example not prints), then press Enter or Feed it in. Or tap the black chips, which type a word and feed it in for you. ⌫ takes the last word out.

The two lines

Under the machine, the game shows what your sentence talks about. Pieces that match are underlined in violet. When the two lines are the same, the sentence talks about itself.

The six steps

1. Try to put a sentence inside its own box, and see why it never works. 2. Learn TWICE on two small targets. 3. Make the sentence talk about itself. 4. Ask whether the honest machine can print it. 5. Hilbert builds Machine II: beat it too. 6. Hilbert's last request.

Stuck? Rub out and try again. After a few tries the game points at the answer, and then offers to show it.
