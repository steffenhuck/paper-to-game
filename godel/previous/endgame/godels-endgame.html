Gödel's Endgame

David Hilbert has a machine for sentences about whole numbers, like 2 × 3 = 6. It is honest: it never prints a false sentence. And Hilbert makes a promise:

“Give my machine any sentence. It will print it, or print its NOT. Always.”

You will play the machine, learn how a sentence becomes a number, and then write one sentence of your own.

Read further
  1. Why multiplication. With addition alone a machine can settle every sentence about whole numbers (Presburger, 1929). Multiplication is what lets one number hold a whole list, and so a whole sentence or a whole proof. That is the step that lets arithmetic talk about its own machine.
  2. The filing trick is Gödel's. He gave each sign a number and packed strings of signs as products of prime powers, just as you did. His codes are different (odd numbers for the basic signs), and he then built “is a proof of” out of forty-six definitions, all of them arithmetic.
  3. The gap is Gödel's trick too. In the paper, a formula with a free spot is filled with its own number. “Written twice, the second time in quotes” is the same move in words (the philosopher W. V. Quine's way of saying it).
  4. Honest is more than Gödel needed. He assumed only that the machine never prints a sentence and its NOT together (in a slightly stronger form). Rosser (1936) got by with exactly that.
  5. The last request. The machine can't print “I never print a false sentence” either: if it could, it could follow the argument you just made and print your sentence. That is the paper's last proposition, now called the second incompleteness theorem.
  6. What it does not say. Not that mathematics is broken or that some truths are unknowable. You know your sentence is true. No single fixed machine catches every truth about whole numbers.

How to play

The aim. Hilbert promises his machine prints every sentence about whole numbers or its NOT. Find a sentence where it can do neither.

Act 1: play the machine

Write a sentence with the keypad (or tap Give me one) and hand it to the machine. First it checks it is a sentence at all: xyz or 2 + = 4 are not. Then you decide, as the machine: Print it if it is true, Print its NOT if it is false. The machine never prints a false sentence.

Act 2: sentences become numbers

Pack a sentence into one number by multiplying primes, then unpack a number by dividing. Then judge a sentence about the machine itself.

Act 3: the sentence

Choose what goes in the gap. The two lines under the machine show your sentence and what it talks about; when they are the same, it talks about itself. Then try to print it.

Built from B. Meltzer's English translation (1962). Game built from this paper; the paper is the authority where the two disagree.

What the paper does

Hilbert hoped for a fixed rulebook for whole numbers that settles every question about them: proves each sentence or its opposite. Gödel showed it cannot be done. First he gives every sign a number and packs strings of signs into single numbers with prime powers, so that “this is a proof of that” becomes a fact about numbers, which the rulebook can state. Then he fills a sentence's gap with the sentence's own number and gets a sentence that says of itself that it cannot be proved. If the rulebook never contradicts itself, it proves neither that sentence nor its opposite, and the sentence is true. A bigger rulebook has its own such sentence. And the rulebook cannot prove that it never contradicts itself.

How the game tells it

The rulebook is a machine, and “proves” is “prints”. Act 1 plays Hilbert's promise on sentences with + and ×. Act 2 plays the paper's packing of strings into numbers, with small codes of our own (the paper's are odd numbers for its basic signs) so that the numbers stay readable. Act 3 tells the self-referring sentence in words: “written twice, the second time in quotes” is the philosopher W. V. Quine's way of saying Gödel's filling of a gap with its own number. The machine is assumed honest, which is more than Gödel assumed (see Read further on the end screen).

The mechanism

A toy. The game runs a small model built to illustrate the argument, not the paper's formal system.

  • Demonstrates: that sentences with + and × are each settled by an honest machine; that one number can hold a whole sentence and give it back; that a sentence about the machine's printing is a sentence about a number; that exactly one filling of the gap makes the sentence talk about itself; that the honest machine can then print neither it nor its NOT, and that it is true.
  • Assumes: an honest machine; and that “the machine prints the sentence with number N” can be written with + and × alone. The game says so and shows one case; Gödel proved it with forty-six definitions.
  • The paper establishes: these results for Principia Mathematica and every system like it whose rules can be listed, assuming only that it never contradicts itself (in a slightly stronger form). Nothing about systems whose rules cannot be listed.
