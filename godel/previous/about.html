About

The paper. Kurt Gödel, "Über formal unentscheidbare Sätze der Principia Mathematica und verwandter Systeme I", Monatshefte für Mathematik und Physik 38 (1931): 173–198. DOI 10.1007/BF01700692. Received 17 November 1930. The game was built from an English translation (B. Meltzer, 1962); the standard translation is in Gödel's Collected Works I (Oxford, 1986). Game built from this paper; the paper is the authority where the two disagree.

What the paper says

By 1930 all of mathematics had been written as a few axioms and a few rules of inference, in Whitehead and Russell's Principia and in set theory, and it was supposed that the right axioms would settle every question. Gödel shows they cannot. His trick: every sentence of the system is a string of signs; give each sign a number (0 is 1, the successor sign is 3, "not" is 5, "or" is 7, "for all" is 9, the brackets 11 and 13, variables are primes above 13) and a string becomes one number, the product of prime powers 2a·3b·5c…; so "is a formula", "is a proof of", "is provable" become properties of numbers, and the system, which is about numbers, can talk about them (his definitions 1–46 and Proposition V). Then list the sentences with one hole in them, R(1), R(2), …; write [R(n); n] for the n-th one filled with its own number; and let K be the set of n for which [R(n); n] is not provable. "n is in K" is itself a sentence with a hole, somewhere in the list, say R(q). Then [R(q); q] says of itself that it is not provable. Prove it and the system has proved something false; refute it and the system has proved it provable; so, if the system never proves falsehoods (he needs only a weaker condition, ω-consistency), it does neither, and the sentence, which says it will not be proved, is true (Proposition VI). The construction works again for any system you build by adding axioms, as long as they can be listed by a rule, so adding the sentence only produces a new one. Adding its negation gives a system that never contradicts itself outright but asserts that a proof exists while refuting every candidate: ω-inconsistent. And a system that could prove "I never contradict myself" could prove the undecidable sentence from it, so it cannot prove its own consistency (Proposition XI). This holds for Principia, for set theory, and for ordinary arithmetic with a few rules.

What the game does

It puts a rulebook on a board and lets you run it. Shapes are sentences with a hole; their number is their line on the board, as in the paper's opening sketch (the prime coding above is what makes that line number expressible inside the system, and the game takes it for granted). Shapes 1 to 3 are facts about numbers, which the rulebook works out. Shapes 4 and 5 are about the rulebook itself; it settles those by settling the inner sentence and reading off its own ledger. Shape 6 you write from parts: "Rulebook 1 proves" or "does not prove", "shape □" or a shape number, "filled with □" or a number. The one that cannot be settled is the paper's S = R(q): "Rulebook 1 does not prove shape □ filled with □", filled with 6. The second act plays the paper's three repairs: add the sentence (Rulebook 2, with a shape of its own, and a loophole at shape 7 filled with 7), add its opposite (Rulebook 2′, which proves that a page of the ledger proves the sentence and refutes every page you show it), and ask the rulebook to certify itself (the chain of Proposition XI).

What kind of mechanism this is

Toy. The game runs a model built to illustrate the paper's argument, and where the argument had to be made small, the toy chose. A rulebook here is a settler: it works out number facts, it may read "proved" or "refuted" off its own ledger for any sentence it has settled, and an added rule settles its sentence outright. If settling a sentence needs that same sentence, the circle is read: if every link in it is "proves", the sentence is proved (it says only that it is proved, which proving makes true); if any link is "does not prove", it is left unsettled, and so is every sentence that depends on it.

  • What the game demonstrates: that a rulebook which can read its own ledger is still stumped by a sentence that refers to its own line through a hole; that the sentence is true; that adding it gives the next rulebook a sentence of the same shape; that adding its opposite makes the rulebook assert a page and refute every page; and that the rulebook's own certificate would prove the loophole.
  • What the game assumes: everything in the paragraph above. Two assumptions are generous to the rulebook. First, the toy lets it certify "does not prove S" for any S it has refuted; the paper's system can certify a proof it has but never a "no" without certifying its own consistency, so the real rulebook has more unsettled sentences than the toy, not fewer (the headless check runs the game with the weaker rulebook too, and the loophole opens there as well). Second, the sentence that only says "Rulebook 1 proves this sentence" gets a tick; that is Löb's theorem of 1955, which the paper does not treat. Under the toy, any circle with a "does not prove" in it is unsettled, which is what the paper's logic gives.
  • What the paper establishes: Propositions VI, VIII and XI for the system P (Principia's logic over the Peano axioms) and for every ω-consistent extension by a recursively definable class of axioms, by the arithmetisation the game does not play. It does not establish anything about rulebooks that are not of that kind, and it does not say that mathematics is "incomplete" in any sense beyond this: for each such system, one true sentence it cannot prove, and no proof of its own consistency inside it.

What is cut, and where the game departs

The coding of signs as numbers, the 46 definitions, the system P's axioms and types, Propositions VII to X (the arithmetical and predicate-calculus forms), Rosser's 1936 strengthening that removes the ω-consistency condition, and the sequel Gödel announced and never needed to write. "Proves" and "refutes" stand for "provable" and "negation provable"; "unsettled" for "undecidable"; "contradicts itself" for "inconsistent"; "page k of the ledger proves S" for the paper's relation x B y, with the pages being your own runs rather than all proofs in order. The budget of runs is the game's, so that the game ends.
