Letters to the Editor · Nature, 11 February 1939

Meitner's Drop

For four years chemists have fired neutrons at uranium and filled a shelf of test tubes. Five of them hold brand-new elements, heavier than anything on Earth. You are the uranium drop. Pinch yourself in two where you choose, or hold together, then watch where the pieces land on the chemists' table.

The more charge a drop carries, the less skin it has left. Lead has 82 charges, uranium 92. The third drop is imagined: 100 charges, more than any element anyone has seen.

Eight tubes to explain.

Your pieces land in the cell with their own number, then climb: too many neutrons for their size, they change into the next element, one cell to the right, again and again, until they settle.

How to play

Explain every tube on the chemists' shelf. You have four neutrons.

Three drops

Tap a drop and a neutron lands on it. A drop's skin holds it together and its charge pulls it apart. Lead holds. Uranium wobbles, necks in the middle and just holds. An imagined drop of 100 charges falls apart on its own.

The shelf

Eight test tubes, each a body the chemists found. The table runs in order of charges, so a piece with 56 charges is barium, element 56. Five dashed tubes were filed as brand-new elements, 93 to 97. A tube is explained when a piece lands or climbs into its column: elements in one column behave alike in chemistry.

The bench

You are the uranium drop. Drag the red line across it, or nudge it with the two arrows. The two numbers are the charges each piece gets; together they are always 92. In the table below, the red box shows where each piece lands, the dots show how it climbs (one cell to the right at a time, about four or five times), and the black box shows where it stops. The line under the drop says how often a pinch there goes through.

Split here pinches the drop at the line. Near the middle the skin has given up, so the pinch always goes through; far out the skin may pull the drop back. A drop pulled back keeps the neutron, so that neutron is spent without a split. The draw is by chance, from a seed shown on the last screen, so a game can be replayed.

Hold together: the drop swallows the neutron and rings without splitting. That is certain, and it makes uranium-239, which sits in uranium's own cell and then moves one cell on.

When a tube is explained it slides down into the piece's cell and turns red, and its card on the shelf says what it really is. Next neutron brings a fresh neutron. The stage ends when every tube is explained or the neutrons are spent.

Thorium

The chemists shot neutrons at thorium too, and found two more tubes, filed as radium and actinium. Same buttons, same rules, three neutrons.
