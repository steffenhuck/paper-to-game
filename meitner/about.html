The paper. Lise Meitner and O. R. Frisch, "Disintegration of Uranium by Neutrons: a New Type of Nuclear Reaction", Nature 143, 239–240, 11 February 1939, a letter dated 16 January from Stockholm and Copenhagen. DOI 10.1038/143239a0. Game built from this paper; the paper is the authority where the two disagree.

What the paper says

Fermi's group, and then Hahn, Meitner and Strassmann, bombarded uranium with neutrons and found a growing list of radioactive bodies, nine periods by 1937, six of them assigned to elements beyond uranium. The assignment rested on one assumption: a nucleus can only chip, since only particles of one or two charges were known to leave one, and Gamow's theory of the Coulomb barrier ruled out a shower of them. So a body that behaved like osmium was filed as eka-osmium, element 94, rather than osmium (76) or ruthenium (44). Then Hahn and Strassmann found that three of the new bodies could not be separated from barium, while mesothorium, an isotope of radium, separated readily in the same experiment: isotopes of barium (56) were coming out of uranium (92).

Meitner and Frisch explain it with Bohr's picture of a heavy nucleus as a drop of liquid. A charged drop's surface tension is lowered by its charge, and a rough estimate puts it at zero near charge 100; uranium therefore has only a small stability of form and, after capturing a neutron, may divide into two drops of roughly equal size. The two repel each other with about 200 Mev, calculated from nuclear radius and charge, and that energy is available from the difference in packing fraction between uranium and the middle of the periodic table: the pieces weigh less than the whole. The process is classical, no tunnelling needed. The pieces carry uranium's surplus of neutrons and shed it by beta decay, each giving a chain. If one piece is barium the other is krypton, which would decay through rubidium, strontium and yttrium to zirconium; some supposed barium-lanthanum-cerium chains may be strontium-yttrium-zirconium chains. The periods ascribed to elements beyond uranium are probably light elements too: the 10 s and 40 s bodies may be masurium (43) decaying through ruthenium, rhodium, palladium and silver into cadmium. Nuclear isomerism need no longer be assumed. Thorium's "radium" and "actinium" are probably the same fission. The 24-minute body chemically identified with uranium is probably real uranium-239, formed by resonance capture, going over into an eka-rhenium that appears inactive; its compound state lives a million times longer than the drop takes to divide, perhaps a symmetrical motion that does not favour fission. Frisch's word for the division, "fission", in quotation marks in the letter, is borrowed from biology.

What the game does

You are the uranium drop. The chemists' shelf is the paper's borrowed record: eight test tubes, each a radioactive body Hahn and Strassmann found, standing in the cell of the 1939 periodic table where the chemists filed it. Five stand in dashed cells beyond uranium. You cut the drop where you choose, anywhere from 1 to 91 (the paper leaves the ratio of the division to "finer structural features and perhaps partly on chance"; the game gives the choice to your hand and keeps the chance: a cut in the middle always works, a cut further out fails at the stated odds, and a drop whose cut fails keeps its neutron, the paper's resonance capture). The pieces land on the table and change by beta decay, one cell to the right each time; a tube lights when a piece stands in its column, because two elements in one column act alike at the bench. Before you fire, the game shows the cut: the chance as a hill under the drop, the pieces' path on the table (a red box where each starts, a dot for each change, a black box where it stops), the columns that would light, and one line in words naming the tubes. This is the mechanism's own result, computed in advance and checked by calibration against the cut when it is made; the third build hid it and the author found the game too hard to understand. The shelf of eight cards under the table keeps the count and says what each lit tube really is. The discovery is seen as the tubes sliding down out of the dashed row into the middle of the table. Then thorium, with its two tubes. An optional side trip from the start screen, three drops, shows why a drop can split at all.

Mechanism kind: toy

The game runs a model built to illustrate the paper's argument, not the paper's own equations, which it does not have. Under this game's assumptions: the pieces share the drop's neutrons in proportion to their charges; each then climbs to the valley of stability given by a standard formula, Z* = A / (1.98 + 0.0155 A2/3), one cell per beta decay; the blast is the Coulomb energy of two touching spheres with a radius constant of 1.55 fm, fitted so that the equal split gives the paper's 200 Mev; the skin of a drop is 1 − (Z²/A)/39.4, fitted so that it vanishes at charge 100; the cut may go anywhere from 1 to 91 (pieces lighter than potassium, 19, stand on a thin strip above the table and light nothing, since the 1939 table has no tube there); a cut within three protons of the middle always goes through and beyond that its odds fall as 1 / (1 + ((a − 3) / 4.5)2.5) with a the distance from the middle, so the barium cell is a one-in-four shot, a cut at 52 | 40 three in four, and a cut at the very edge under one in a hundred; the draws come from a seed shown on the verdict screen; four neutrons for uranium and three for thorium; and a tube lights from any element in its column. All of these are ours and sit in the config block of the mechanism.

What the game demonstrates: that a drop picture with these rules reproduces the paper's story, and makes "roughly equal size" a thing to weigh: the five "new elements" come down from safe cuts near the middle, barium needs a cut that may fail, and a failed cut is a drop that kept its neutron. Barium's partner is krypton; a piece near masurium climbs through the five columns the chemists had filed beyond uranium; the 24-minute body is reached only by a failed cut; thorium's "radium" and "actinium" are lit by barium and lanthanum; and the climb reproduces the shape of the three chains the paper names (krypton to zirconium, barium to cerium, masurium to cadmium), though not every endpoint (our barium climbs on to neodymium).

What the game assumes: the toy above; the odds curve, which is ours entirely (the paper says only "roughly equal" and "perhaps partly on chance", and nothing about how often an uneven division happens); a deterministic neutron share where the paper says "varying proportions"; a 1939 table that places thorium, protactinium and uranium under hafnium, tantalum and tungsten, with the transuranics in the next five columns, which is how the chemists' "eka-" names got their chemistry; that the five transuranic tubes are eka-rhenium to eka-gold (the paper names eka-rhenium and eka-osmium; the others follow the 1937 scheme of its reference 2); that the rare earths share lanthanum's cell and its chemistry. The one-cut sweep of all five "new elements" depends on the climb being four or five steps long, which the paper's own chains state; under rules with shorter chains they still come down, in two cuts.

What the paper establishes: the argument that a heavy charged drop can divide classically, with about 200 Mev released and paid for by the packing fraction; the identification of barium's partner as krypton; the conjecture, stated as "possible, and seems to us rather probable", that the transuranic periods are light elements; the suggestion that thorium fissions too; and the reading of the 24-minute body as genuine uranium-239. It establishes nothing about which cut is likelier, nothing about the exact chains, and nothing about neutron emission or chain reactions, which are not in this paper at all; they came in the weeks after it.

What is simplified or changed

The first build let every cut go through, and the author found that fission came too easily; the second build (26 November 2026) added the odds and cut the budget from five neutrons to four. The third build (26 November 2026) was rebuilt for a smart eleven-year-old: the shelf became a readable checklist of eight cards, the climb of each piece was drawn before the cut, the explanations were cut to a few plain sentences, and a short briefing screen now explains the table once. The mechanism, the numbers and the calibration were unchanged.

The fourth build (26 November 2026) followed the author's note that the third was too hard to understand. The cut may now go anywhere from 1 to 91, with the chance drawn as a hill under the drop. The game shows what a cut will do before it is made. The Hold button is gone: a failed cut is the drop catching the neutron, which is how the 24-minute tube is reached, so aiming at barium either lights barium or lights the 24-minute tube. The words are plainer (number for charge, cut for cut, tube lit for explained) and the physics of the skin moved to an optional side trip. The mechanism's numbers are unchanged.

The fifth build (27 November 2026) added hand-holding on the author's note that it still needed more. Before the first cut the game says what to watch for whether the drop splits or the cut fails. Under the table, every cut says what it lights and what you will learn if it splits and if it fails. After each result, one line says what you learned, and a "still dark" line says where to look next. These lessons are the paper's three findings in a sentence each (pieces from the middle can pass for new elements; barium comes out with krypton as its partner; a neutron can just get caught, the 24-minute stuff) and thorium's one; they are not new claims.

The sixth build (27 November 2026) removed the briefing screen that sat between the title and the first cut. It repeated the table, its goal line did not match what the screen let you do, and it made a hard game harder. Play now goes straight to the first cut, and the one sentence it needed, what makes a tube light, is the guide above the first neutron. Tapping a tube in the table still says what else stands in its column.

The seventh build (27 November 2026) rewrote the verdict. It used to be four fixed sections. It is now a scoreboard of the chemists' five "new elements" (how many the chemists claimed, how many you took away, how many the paper took away) followed by a short story of your own run. The paper took away all five and left one thing open, element 93 from the 24-minute uranium. Outside the paper: Meitner and Hahn reported the real element protactinium (91) in 1918, which the closing paragraph mentions.

The eighth build (27 November 2026) followed the author's note that the table was still the hardest part and that the game should be about whether the "new elements" were really discovered. The play screen now opens with that question. The shelf of eight tubes sits above the table under the heading "Is it really new?", with a count of tubes explained, and the table is explained as where the pieces land. The mechanism is unchanged.

A thing the paper could not know. The odds in this game favour an even split, which is the paper's picture of "two nuclei of roughly equal size". Measurements made soon after 1939 showed that uranium most often splits unevenly, into one heavier and one lighter piece, and that barium with krypton is among the common pairs. So barium is the unlikely tube in this game and a common product in the real thing. The game keeps the paper's picture because the paper is the game's authority; the odds curve is ours, and it says nothing about how often an uneven split happens in nature.

The half-lives of the nine periods are dropped except the 24 minutes; the 10 s and 40 s bodies are folded into the masurium tubes. Mesothorium and the separation itself stay here. The size of the shove (about 200 Mev, million electron-volts, in the paper), the mass the pieces lose for it (about a fifth of a proton between them, by E = mc2) and the origin of the word "fission" are in the paper's text above and no longer on the play screens. Nuclear isomerism, Gamow's theory and the lifetime of the compound state are mentioned only in the verdict and here. Cerium, named in the paper's chains, has no tube of its own: in 1939 tables cerium's column was ambiguous (the paper's own parallel, barium-lanthanum-cerium against strontium-yttrium-zirconium, puts it under zirconium), so it is named in the result lines instead. The clock is compressed: a climb that takes seconds to days on the bench takes two seconds on screen. "Masurium" is the 1939 name for element 43; it is called technetium today, and the 1925 claim to have found it was not sound. The imagined drop of 100 charges is marked as imagined on screen; element 100, fermium, was made in 1952 and does not fall apart unprovoked, since the real critical charge lies higher than the paper's rough estimate.
