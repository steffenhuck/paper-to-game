Frisch, "Disintegration of Uranium by Neutrons: a New Type of Nuclear Reaction", Nature 143, 239–240, 11 February 1939, a letter dated 16 January from Stockholm and Copenhagen. DOI 10.1038/143239a0. Game built from this paper; the paper is the authority where the two disagree.

What the paper says

Fermi's group, and then Hahn, Meitner and Strassmann, bombarded uranium with neutrons and found a growing list of radioactive bodies, nine periods by 1937, six of them assigned to elements beyond uranium. The assignment rested on one assumption: a nucleus can only chip, since only particles of one or two charges were known to leave one, and Gamow's theory of the Coulomb barrier ruled out a shower of them. So a body that behaved like osmium was filed as eka-osmium, element 94, rather than osmium (76) or ruthenium (44). Then Hahn and Strassmann found that three of the new bodies could not be separated from barium, while mesothorium, an isotope of radium, separated readily in the same experiment: isotopes of barium (56) were coming out of uranium (92).

Meitner and Frisch explain it with Bohr's picture of a heavy nucleus as a drop of liquid. A charged drop's surface tension is lowered by its charge, and a rough estimate puts it at zero near charge 100; uranium therefore has only a small stability of form and, after capturing a neutron, may divide into two drops of roughly equal size. The two repel each other with about 200 Mev, calculated from nuclear radius and charge, and that energy is available from the difference in packing fraction between uranium and the middle of the periodic table: the pieces weigh less than the whole. The process is classical, no tunnelling needed. The pieces carry uranium's surplus of neutrons and shed it by beta decay, each giving a chain. If one piece is barium the other is krypton, which would decay through rubidium, strontium and yttrium to zirconium; some supposed barium-lanthanum-cerium chains may be strontium-yttrium-zirconium chains. The periods ascribed to elements beyond uranium are probably light elements too: the 10 s and 40 s bodies may be masurium (43) decaying through ruthenium, rhodium, palladium and silver into cadmium. Nuclear isomerism need no longer be assumed. Thorium's "radium" and "actinium" are probably the same fission. The 24-minute body chemically identified with uranium is probably real uranium-239, formed by resonance capture, going over into an eka-rhenium that appears inactive; its compound state lives a million times longer than the drop takes to divide, perhaps a symmetrical motion that does not favour fission. Frisch's word for the division, "fission", in quotation marks in the letter, is borrowed from biology.

What the game does

You are the uranium drop. The chemists' shelf is the paper's borrowed record: eight test tubes, each a radioactive body Hahn and Strassmann found, standing in the cell of the 1939 periodic table where the chemists filed it. Five stand in dashed cells beyond uranium. You pinch the drop where you choose (the paper leaves the ratio of the division to "finer structural features and perhaps partly on chance"; the game gives it to your hand), or hold it together (the paper's resonance capture). The pieces land on the table and climb by beta decay; a tube lights when a piece stands in its column, because two elements in one column act alike at the bench. The discovery is produced by your pinch and seen as the tubes sliding down out of the dashed row into the middle of the table. Then thorium, with its two tubes.

Mechanism kind: toy

The game runs a model built to illustrate the paper's argument, not the paper's own equations, which it does not have. Under this game's assumptions: the pieces share the drop's neutrons in proportion to their charges; each then climbs to the valley of stability given by a standard formula, Z* = A / (1.98 + 0.0155 A2/3), one cell per beta decay; the blast is the Coulomb energy of two touching spheres with a radius constant of 1.55 fm, fitted so that the equal split gives the paper's 200 Mev; the skin of a drop is 1 − (Z²/A)/39.4, fitted so that it vanishes at charge 100; the pinch is clamped to pieces of at least 28 protons; and a tube lights from any element in its column. All of these are ours and sit in the config block of the mechanism.

What the game demonstrates: that a drop picture with these rules reproduces the paper's story: barium's partner is krypton; a piece near masurium climbs through the five columns the chemists had filed beyond uranium; the 24-minute body is reached only by holding; thorium's "radium" and "actinium" are lit by barium and lanthanum; and the climb reproduces the shape of the three chains the paper names (krypton to zirconium, barium to cerium, masurium to cadmium), though not every endpoint (our barium climbs on to neodymium).

What the game assumes: the toy above; a deterministic neutron share where the paper says "varying proportions" and "perhaps partly on chance"; a 1939 table that places thorium, protactinium and uranium under hafnium, tantalum and tungsten, with the transuranics in the next five columns, which is how the chemists' "eka-" names got their chemistry; that the five transuranic tubes are eka-rhenium to eka-gold (the paper names eka-rhenium and eka-osmium; the others follow the 1937 scheme of its reference 2); that the rare earths share lanthanum's cell and its chemistry. The one-pinch sweep of all five ghosts depends on the climb being four or five steps long, which the paper's own chains state; under rules with shorter chains the ghosts still come down, in two pinches.

What the paper establishes: the argument that a heavy charged drop can divide classically, with about 200 Mev released and paid for by the packing fraction; the identification of barium's partner as krypton; the conjecture, stated as "possible, and seems to us rather probable", that the transuranic periods are light elements; the suggestion that thorium fissions too; and the reading of the 24-minute body as genuine uranium-239. It establishes nothing about which pinch is likelier, nothing about the exact chains, and nothing about neutron emission or chain reactions, which are not in this paper at all; they came in the weeks after it.

What is simplified or changed

The half-lives of the nine periods are dropped except the 24 minutes; the 10 s and 40 s bodies are folded into the masurium tubes. Mesothorium and the separation itself stay here. Nuclear isomerism, Gamow's theory and the lifetime of the compound state are mentioned only in the verdict and here. Cerium, named in the paper's chains, has no tube of its own: in 1939 tables cerium's column was ambiguous (the paper's own parallel, barium-lanthanum-cerium against strontium-yttrium-zirconium, puts it under zirconium), so it is named in the result lines instead. The clock is compressed: a climb that takes seconds to days on the bench takes two seconds on screen. "Masurium" is the 1939 name for element 43; it is called technetium today, and the 1925 claim to have found it was not sound. The imagined drop of 100 charges is marked as imagined on screen; element 100, fermium, was made in 1952 and does not fall apart unprovoked, since the real critical charge lies higher than the paper's rough estimate.
