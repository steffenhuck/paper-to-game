Meitner's Drop

1938. Chemists fire neutrons at uranium and find strange new stuff. Five of the substances seem to be brand-new elements, heavier than uranium, numbers 93 to 97. Lise Meitner and Otto Frisch think something else happened: the uranium split in two.

You are Meitner and Frisch. Choose where the uranium might split, and see how many of the five "new elements" your pieces can explain. You have four tries.

Built from Lise Meitner and O. R. Frisch, "Disintegration of Uranium by Neutrons: a New Type of Nuclear Reaction", Nature 143, 239–240 (1939).

How to play

You are Meitner and Frisch. The chemists say they found five new elements, 93 to 97. You think the uranium split in two and the pieces only look like new elements. You have four tries.

The five "new elements"

Each one is a substance that the chemists could not tell from an old element: "like rhenium", "like osmium" and so on. Elements in one column of the table act alike in chemistry. So when one of your pieces turns into an old element that stands in the same column, the "new element" is explained: it was never new.

The cut

Drag the red line, or use the arrows. The two numbers are your two pieces; together they are always 92. The hill shows how likely it is that uranium splits there: sure in the middle, rare far out. The row of five boxes under the drop shows which "new elements" this cut would explain. Then press Test this cut.

The table shows the cut before you make it: a red box where each piece starts, a dot for each time it changes into the next element, a black box where it stops. If a cut fails, the drop just catches the neutron and nothing is explained.
