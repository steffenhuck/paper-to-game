## 1. Paper

Lise Meitner and O. R. Frisch, "Disintegration of Uranium by Neutrons: a New Type of Nuclear Reaction", *Nature* 143, 239–240 (11 February 1939), Letters to the Editor, dated Jan. 16. DOI 10.1038/143239a0; readable at nature.com/articles/143239a0. Available for this build: the PDF scan of the two pages and a transcript made from it by OCR and proofread against the page images, with footnote numbers checked. No code, no data beyond what the letter states. The letter is a page and a half; every number in the game that is the paper's is one of its five: Z = 92, 56, 36, 43, 94, 76, 44; c. 200 Mev; surface tension vanishing near Z of the order of 100; the 10 s, 40 s and 24 min periods; the compound state's lifetime a million times the time to divide.

## 2. What the paper hands over

Primary: an **argument**. The liquid-drop picture of the heavy nucleus (after Bohr), with one quantitative turn: the surface tension of a charged drop is lowered by its charge and vanishes near Z ≈ 100, so uranium "has only small stability of form" and, nudged by a captured neutron, may divide into two drops of roughly equal size, which repel each other with about 200 Mev, paid for by the difference in packing fraction between uranium and the middle of the periodic table. Then the consequences, argued verbally: the fragments carry uranium's neutron surplus and climb by beta decay, each giving a chain; barium's partner is krypton (92 − 56); the "transuranic" periods are probably light elements (the osmium-like body is ruthenium's neighbour, the 10 s and 40 s bodies masurium); nuclear isomerism is no longer needed; thorium's "radium and actinium" are the same fission; the 24-min body is genuine ²³⁹U, made by resonance capture, going to a quiet eka-rhenium.

Secondary: an **estimate**, borrowed: Hahn and Strassmann's chemistry (refs 4 and 5), the record the argument explains. At least three radioactive bodies from neutron-bombarded uranium that cannot be separated from barium, while mesothorium, an isotope of radium, separates readily in the same experiment; the barium–lanthanum–cerium chains; the nine periods of ref 2, six filed beyond uranium; thorium's radium- and actinium-like periods (ref 8). The game formalises the argument into a toy with three rules (section 16, after build.md) and uses the chemistry as the record on the shelf.

## 3. The discovery, and its kind

**A counterintuitive result, carrying a mechanism made intelligible.** Everyone, the authors included for four years, assumed that a bombarded nucleus can only chip: only particles of one or two charges were known to leave a nucleus, and Gamow's Coulomb barrier forbade a shower of them, so every new body had to sit near Z = 92, and a body that behaved like osmium was filed as eka-osmium (94), not osmium (76) or ruthenium (44). The paper shows the opposite: the nucleus splits in half, classically, like a drop whose skin has nearly given up, and in doing so it dissolves the whole edifice of transuranic elements into the middle of the periodic table. The mechanism (charge against surface tension; the packing fraction paying for 200 Mev) is what makes the surprising thing plain.

## 4. The core narrative

A very heavy drop is held together by its skin, but it carries so much electric charge that the skin has almost given up. Tap it with one small particle and, instead of a chip flying off, the whole drop pinches in the middle and snaps into two drops, which shove each other away with enormous force. The chemists who had been tapping these drops for years kept finding new stuff and filing it as new, never-seen elements heavier than anything known. The paper says: no. Look at the two halves. Count what is in them. The new stuff is old stuff, ordinary middle-weight elements, and the mountain of heavy elements was never there.

## 5. Who lives in the paper

- **The uranium nucleus**, the drop. Controls, in the paper's words, "the precise ratio of sizes", depending on structure "and perhaps partly on chance", and how its neutrons are shared; or it can take the neutron in and ring symmetrically instead (the resonance capture). Candidate player: the agent whose choice is where to divide.
- **The neutron.** Adds the energy. Nothing to decide.
- **The two fragments.** Climb by beta decay toward the right mix. Their only control is how far they climb, which the paper gives to physics.
- **Hahn and Strassmann at the bench.** Control the carriers and the separations; their record is the shelf. Candidate player: the experimenter, with the paper's own instrument (fractional crystallisation against barium and mesothorium).
- **The assigner of elements**, the prejudice itself: whoever decides that an osmium-like body is 94, not 76 or 44. Candidate player: the agent whose choice is a Z.
- **Meitner and Frisch**, the theorists: reconcile two numbers (the repulsion of two charged halves; the mass lost between uranium and the middle of the table) and reread four years of chemistry. Candidate player: the controller of the drop model.
- **Surface tension and charge**, the two forces. Candidate control: the drop's charge, to find where the skin gives up.

## 7. The aim and the verdict

The aim, as the player reads it: **Account for every tube on the chemists' shelf. Pinch the drop where you choose, or hold it together; you have five neutrons.**

The verdict leads with, in words:

- *What the paper found.* Uranium splits in two like a drop whose skin has nearly given up; the halves fly apart with about 200 Mev, paid for by the weight they lose. Barium's partner must be krypton. The bodies filed beyond uranium are probably middle-weight elements, masurium and its neighbours, and the puzzle of their many periods needs no nuclear isomerism. The 24-minute body is real uranium-239, made when the drop holds together and rings. Thorium splits the same way.
- *What you found.* The pinches the player made and what each lit; which tubes they brought down from the row beyond uranium, which they left there; whether they found the hold; how many neutrons it took.
- *Where the two part.* Any tube left in the dashed row; any piece the player sent where the paper's drop would not go (a very uneven pinch, named as such).
- *What would have followed had yours stood.* "Three of the chemists' periods would still be elements nobody has ever seen, and their chains would still need isomerism to explain." Or, for a full shelf: "Nothing: the row beyond uranium is empty, as the paper leaves it, except one quiet eka-rhenium."

## 8. Three concepts

**A. The drop that chooses where to split** (subject; challenge). The player is the uranium nucleus. One control: a pinch line dragged along the drop; the two proton counts show live either side of it. A second button, *Hold together*: the drop takes the neutron in and rings evenly (the paper's resonance capture). Deliberation before pressing: which cell do I want a piece to reach, given that pieces climb after landing, and that the partner is forced (92 minus mine)? After: the snap and the blast, the landing, the climb, the tubes sliding down. Discovery moment: the five tubes filed beyond uranium cannot be landed on (no pinch reaches 93), but a piece pinched at 43 climbs through their five columns one after another, and the tubes come down into row five. Fun: anticipation (the pinch is set, the drop snaps), agency (the climb is a rule the player learns and aims with), discovery (the shelf descends), mastery (a second run clears the shelf in three neutrons). Second playthrough: three neutrons instead of five, the hold found first, an uneven pinch tried for its smaller blast.

**B. Hahn's bench** (experimenter; discovery). The player separates radioactive bodies with carriers: add barium, crystallise, count; add mesothorium as control; try to part the body from the barium. Then assign a Z. Deliberation: which carrier distinguishes radium from barium; whether to believe the chemistry or the physics. Discovery: the body will not leave the barium; it is barium. Fun: discovery, some anticipation. Second playthrough: little; the procedure is the same. It fails the author test: the paper is the physicists' reading of the chemists' record, and this game plays the chemists. It also hands a stranger a lab technique before it hands them a thing to want.

**C. Thumb on the drop** (controller; discovery) — the reckless one. A wordless play screen: a drop of chosen charge (lead 82, thorium 90, uranium 92, an imagined 100), deformed in real time under the finger; the skin pulls it back, the charge pushes it on; release, and it rings or snaps. The only text on screen is the charge. Discovery: the threshold near 100, and that uranium needs only a touch. Fun: agency, discovery. Second playthrough: none beyond trying the four. It fails the walkthrough at minute two: the drop snaps and the chemistry, the half of the paper that makes the discovery land, can only be told. Its hands are kept: the drop under the finger is A's pinch preview, and its four drops are A's opening panel.

The hybrid, written as the skill asks: A's decision (where to divide, whether to divide) carried out by C's hands (the finger on the drop). That is the pick.

## 10. The pick, and why

A. The control is the paper's own variable, the ratio of the division, which the paper leaves to structure and chance and the game gives to the player's hand; the hold is the paper's own resonance capture; the blast is the paper's own formula (kinetic energy from radius and charge), calibrated to its 200 Mev; the climb is the paper's own beta chains drawn; the shelf is the paper's own borrowed record. The deliberation is real at the first action (where to land, knowing pieces climb), the middle one (how to reach a row the pinch cannot reach: by column) and the last (the one tube no pinch reaches: hold). The discovery, that the elements beyond uranium are middle-weight elements, is produced by the player's pinch and seen as tubes sliding down the table, not read. The author test: Meitner and Frisch know the answer, and still get to pinch the drop, watch Hahn's shelf come down the table, and find their own eka-rhenium quietly surviving in cell 93. Originality: no game in the table has had the player be the nucleus, pinch a drop, or move a record across a periodic table. Runner-up: B, and what would make me switch: a reader who finds the table unreadable at phone width, in which case the fix is to shrink the table to the three rows the pieces visit and keep the pinch. The reckless concept's loss: the wordless screen; its hands, the finger deforming the drop, are kept as the pinch preview, and its four drops open the game.

## 11. The shape

Three stages, one page each, then the verdict; about five minutes.

1. **Three drops** (the opener, free taps, under a minute): lead (82), uranium (92), an imagined drop of charge 100, labelled imagined. Each tap drops a neutron; the first rings and settles, the third falls apart unprovoked, the second wobbles, necks and holds. The paper's rule (charge lowers surface tension to zero near 100) is seen before it is used. Advance after uranium has been tapped.
2. **Uranium at the bench** (five neutrons; three suffice): the shelf of nine tubes from the paper's borrowed record. Each neutron is one decision: a pinch position, or hold. The three distinct things to find: the climb (first pinch), the column (the dashed row comes down), the hold (the 24-min body). Two spare neutrons are the practice allowance. The stage ends when the shelf is clear or the neutrons are spent.
3. **Thorium** (one neutron): the paper's last suggestion. Two tubes, filed as radium and actinium; the drop is 90, the partner arithmetic changes, the same products light them. One new thing: a different drop, the same fission.

Presets are the paper's regimes in the paper's order: uranium, then the capture resonance (inside stage 2, as the hold), then thorium. Score: the neutrons used, said once in the verdict beside the paper's three, and never on the play screen as a running total; the yardstick during play is the shelf itself, tubes lit of tubes standing.

## 12. Controls, mechanism, feedback, moment

**Controls.** The pinch line: dragged along the drop; the left piece's charge is Z₁ = round(Z · position), clamped so that no piece is below 28, and the right piece is Z − Z₁; both numbers show live. *Split here.* *Hold together.* Nothing else. The thorium stage has the same two.

**Mechanism** (the toy, detailed in section 16 after build.md). (i) Division: the drop of Z protons and N neutrons divides at the pinch into Z₁ and Z − Z₁, with neutrons shared in proportion, N₁ = round(N · Z₁/Z). (ii) The blast: the Coulomb energy of two touching spheres, E = e²Z₁Z₂/(r₀(A₁^⅓ + A₂^⅓)), with r₀ set so that the equal split of ²³⁹U gives the paper's 200 Mev; an uneven pinch gives less, and the screen says so. (iii) The climb: each piece climbs one cell per beta decay until its charge reaches the valley of stability, Z*(A) = A/(1.98 + 0.0155·A^⅔), rounded; from uranium's mix that is four or five steps for any piece the pinch allows, which is the rule stated on screen. (iv) The hold: the drop becomes ²³⁹U (or ²³³Th), sits at its own cell, then climbs one. Deterministic throughout; the paper's "partly on chance" is removed so the player can aim, and the screen says the game does this.

**Feedback.** The drop necking under the finger with the two numbers; the red landing outlines in the table; the snap, the speed lines, the lettered **FISSION!!**, the energy line; the pieces in vermilion while they climb, black when they stop; tubes sliding down their column and lighting vermilion; the result line in the game's voice; the shelf count.

**The moment.** The pinch at 43: five tubes leave the dashed row one after another as the piece climbs, and the row beyond uranium is suddenly empty.

Nuclear isomerism, which the paper dismisses and the verdict mentions in a sentence. Chain reactions and neutron emission, which the paper does not mention and the game does not either; About says that they are not in this paper.

## 15. Divergences

- **The ratio of division is the player's.** The paper leaves it to "finer structural features and perhaps partly on chance". The game hands it to the player so that there is something to decide, and says so on the play screen.
- **Neutrons are shared in proportion, deterministically.** The paper says "varying proportions of neutrons may be given to the two parts" and uses this to explain several periods per element. Removed so that the climb is a rule the player can state and aim with; declared in About.
- **The climb uses a valley-of-stability formula** that is ours, not the paper's; the paper names three chains (Ba–La–Ce; Kr–Rb–Sr–Y–Zr; masurium–Ru–Rh–Pd–Ag–Cd) and the formula reproduces their shape (four or five steps) but not every endpoint (barium climbs to about neodymium, the paper stops at cerium). The tubes the paper's chains light are lit either way.
- **The pinch is clamped to pieces of at least 28 protons.** The paper discusses only "roughly equal" division. The clamp is our reading of the drop picture (a sliver would be pulled back by the skin); it keeps the player off alpha emission, which the paper treats separately via Gamow.
- **The blast is the Coulomb energy of two touching spheres with one radius constant, fitted to 200 Mev.** The paper says "calculated from nuclear radius and charge" and gives the result; the formula and the fit are ours.
- **The 1939 table** places thorium, protactinium and uranium under hafnium, tantalum and tungsten, with the five transuranics in the next five columns, as the table was drawn then. That is the historical placement and is why the chemists' "eka-" names fit their chemistry; the modern table is not shown.
- **The chemists' five transuranic tubes** are named eka-rhenium to eka-gold. The paper names eka-rhenium and eka-osmium; iridium, platinum and gold follow from the same 1937 scheme (the paper's reference 2) and are marked as context in the config.
- **The clock** is compressed: a climb that takes seconds to days on the bench takes two seconds on screen; the 24 minutes on the tube is the paper's.
- **Thorium's shelf has two tubes**, radium-like and actinium-like, from the paper's one sentence; the paper's reference 8 lists more periods, which are cut.

## 16. Mechanism kind and claim boundaries

**Toy.** The paper is an argument with five numbers; the game runs a model built to illustrate it. Three rules: (i) division at the pinch, neutrons shared in proportion; (ii) the blast as the Coulomb energy of two touching spheres, r₀ fitted to the paper's 200 Mev; (iii) the climb to a standard valley-of-stability line, one cell per beta decay; plus the skin fraction for the opener, fitted to vanish at Z = 100, and the column rule for lighting tubes. Every one of these is ours and tagged in `CONFIG`.

What the game **demonstrates**: that under these rules barium's partner is krypton, a piece near masurium climbs through the five columns the chemists had filed beyond uranium, no pinch gives the 24-minute body and the hold does, and thorium's "radium" and "actinium" light from barium and lanthanum. What it **assumes**: the toy; determinism where the paper says chance; the 1939 table with Th, Pa, U under Hf, Ta, W; eka-iridium to eka-gold from the paper's reference 2; the rare earths in lanthanum's cell. What the paper **establishes**: the drop argument and its 200 Mev; krypton as barium's partner; the conjecture, hedged as "rather probable", that the transuranics are light elements; thorium's fission as a suggestion; the 24-min body as ²³⁹U. Nothing about neutron emission or chain reactions.

**Which discoveries follow from the paper and which from the rules.** Barium forces krypton: the paper (arithmetic). The ghosts come down into the middle rows: the paper's claim, produced in the game by the column rule, which is the chemists' own practice (a body is identified by the carrier it follows). That all five come down from one pinch: depends on the climb being four or five steps, which the paper's own chains give (Kr→Zr 4, Ma→Cd 5); under rules with shorter chains they come down in two pinches (checked in the calibration; the About screen says so). The hold being the only way to the 24-min tube: the paper's reading (a resonance process), encoded as the `byHold` rule. The three-neutron minimum: a consequence of the rules, said in the verdict as "where three would do", not as a finding. The equal pinch being poor: a consequence of the column rule and the table, not a claim about physics; the verdict does not call it wrong, only notes uneven pinches as "not the paper's picture".

## 17. Calibration plan and result

Checked in `calibration.js` against the paper's own numbers and statements, with tolerances: the equal split gives 200 Mev within 5 % (by the fit) and r₀ lies in 1.2–1.6 fm; the lost weight is about a fifth of a proton; the skin vanishes at Z = 100 and lead/uranium/imagined classify as holds/barely/gone; ²³⁹U climbs exactly one step to 93; the three chains the paper names are reproduced in shape (krypton reaches zirconium or a little beyond, barium reaches cerium or beyond, masurium reaches exactly cadmium); every allowed piece climbs 3–6 steps; every tube stands in the column of its 1939 cell; uranium is under tungsten and 93 under rhenium; strontium under barium and yttrium under lanthanum. Then the moves: 56 | 36 lights barium and lanthanum and passes strontium and yttrium; 43 | 49 lights all five ghosts; the hold lights the 24-min tube and nothing else; no pinch lights it; no piece ever enters the dashed row; no two neutrons clear the shelf and 90 pairs plus a hold do; the pinches that bring all five ghosts down are 42, 43, 49, 50; barium and lanthanum light from 16 pinches but not the exactly equal one; some pinches light one tube or none; an uneven pinch gives a smaller blast; thorium at 56 | 34 lights both its tubes and some thorium pinches light neither. Robustness: under five alternative valley rules that still reproduce the paper's chains, and with the smallest piece set anywhere from 24 to 32, the same facts hold; with the masurium piece given 2–3 neutrons more or fewer than its share, at least four ghosts still come down.
