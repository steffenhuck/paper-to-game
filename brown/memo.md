# Brown's Restless Dust — design memo

## 0. Given

Steffen's, in the great-paper series (Einstein, Watson, Cantor, Hubble, Benjamin): signature and counter. Ideas given, which bind the design: (1) the paper has been reinterpreted since (Einstein 1905, Perrin 1908, and a popular version of what Brown saw and thought); the game sticks to what Brown says, and the later interpretation is discussed in a coda on the final verdict screen; nothing in the game's play uses the later theory, and the coda is after the verdict and marked as later. (2) Make it fancy, with animated SVG capturing the motions; not the dark palette of Hubble and the Olive; the engine picks the look, and it should be astonishing to look at. (2) overrides the quiet museum-label chassis the engine first wrote (section 13 records the change): the register is now a hand-coloured natural-history plate, outsized and in motion.

## 1. Paper

Robert Brown, *A brief account of microscopical observations made in the months of June, July and August, 1827, on the particles contained in the pollen of plants; and on the general existence of active molecules in organic and inorganic bodies*. Privately printed pamphlet, London, dated July 30th, 1828 ("Not published"); reprinted in the *Edinburgh New Philosophical Journal* 5 (1828): 358–371 and in the *Philosophical Magazine* 4 (1828): 161–173. With the supplement *Additional remarks on active molecules*, dated July 28th, 1829 (*Edinburgh Journal of Science* 1 (1829): 314–319; *Philosophical Magazine* 6 (1829): 161–166). Both as reprinted in *The miscellaneous botanical works of Robert Brown*, vol. 1, ed. J. J. Bennett (London: R. Hardwicke, 1866), pp. 463–486. No DOI. Available: the 1866 reprint, scanned (Biodiversity Heritage Library; also the New York Botanical Garden scan the user sent, 24 pages). No data, no figures, no code: the paper is prose. Every number in it is a measurement stated in words (fractions of an inch, minutes, hours, years), read from a scan of typeset text and transcribed by hand; see the config block.

## 2. What the paper hands over

**Primary: an estimate**, of a descriptive kind: a catalogue of observations with the conditions under which each was made. Particles from the pollen of *Clarckia pulchella*, oblong, 1/4000 to 1/5000 of an inch long, in motion; smaller spherical "molecules" (his word for the smallest things he could see), 1/20,000 to 1/30,000 of an inch across, in rapid oscillatory motion; the same in every plant examined; the same in plants dried twenty years and a hundred; the same in every part of the plant when bruised; the same in animal tissue, gum, coal, London soot, fossil wood, window glass, granite, a fragment of the Sphinx, lava, obsidian, pumice, meteorites, manganese, nickel, bismuth, antimony, arsenic; the same in wood, linen, paper, cotton, wool, silk, hair and muscle after burning in a candle or blowpipe; short fibrils of two or three molecules in vivid motion, four or five in motion, longer ones at rest; nothing obtained from oil, resin, wax, sulphur, from metals he could not grind, or from bodies that dissolve in water (and, in 1829, sulphur, resin and wax yielding them after all, the earlier failure put down to "defective manipulation"). The 1829 supplement hands over a second set: an open drop lasts under a minute and develops currents from centre to rim, slow at first, then rapid, that "disturb or entirely overcome the proper motion"; drops of water from 1/50 to 1/2000 of an inch shaken into almond oil last more than an hour with the motion "undiminished"; some such drops hold a single particle, which moves; the experiment inverted, minute oil droplets on the surface of a water drop lie at rest while particles inside it move.

**Secondary: an argument**, by elimination. In 1827: the motion belongs to the particle, not to currents or evaporation. In 1829: it is not animation (he never said so and says so plainly); not currents, not evaporation, not attraction and repulsion between particles, not capillary or hygrometric action at the surface, not air bubbles; the oil-drop experiment answers the most important of these at once; and the cause is one "for which I am unable to account". The game's mechanism is a toy built from the catalogue, declared as such.

## 3. The discovery, and its kind

**A counterintuitive result, reached by identification.** The motion first looked like life: particles of pollen, the part of a plant that makes new plants, swimming. Brown's inquiry takes that belief and kills it in stages, until the restless particles have been found in a chip of the Sphinx and in burnt hair, and every physical cause anyone has proposed has been removed from the drop with the motion undiminished. What is left is a property of smallness itself: any matter, ground fine enough and suspended in water, dances, the finest most vividly; and no cause for it. The paper's discovery is not an explanation; it is the demolition of every explanation on offer, including the author's own first one. The game lets the player try to stop the dance, with Brown's own means, and fail as Brown failed.

## 4. The core narrative

Look through a microscope at a drop of water with a speck of pollen dust in it, and the specks are dancing: trembling, turning, never still. The obvious guess is that they are alive, because pollen is the living stuff of flowers. So you try to make them stop. You take dust from a plant dried a hundred years ago: it dances. You burn hair in a candle and grind the ash: it dances. You chip a piece off the Sphinx, four thousand years old and never alive, and grind that: it dances. Maybe it is the water drying up and stirring the drop: you seal the drop in oil so nothing can dry; it dances for an hour. Maybe the specks are pulling on each other: you make a drop so small it holds one speck alone; it dances alone. The only thing that ever changes the dance is how small the specks are: big lumps lie still, fine dust dances, the finest dust dances most. Robert Brown spent two summers at this and wrote down, honestly, that he could not say why. Nobody could, for seventy-seven years.

## 5. Who lives in the paper

- **Brown, the observer**, with a pocket microscope and one lens of 1/32 inch focus. He controls what goes on the slide (the specimen, its age, whether burnt, how fine), how the drop is prepared (open; sealed in oil; how small; which way round), and how long he watches. He can be asked to anticipate what each change will do to the dance.
- **The particles**, two kinds: the oblong pollen particles and the spherical molecules. They are the thing measured. Playing one is playing a mechanism Brown does not supply; ruled out by the brief.
- **The suspects**: life, currents, evaporation, mutual attraction and repulsion, the surface of the drop, heat (what fire leaves), air bubbles. The 1829 supplement names them; the game lets the player test them.
- **The drop** and its two worlds: open on the glass (a minute, then currents, then nothing) and sealed in almond oil (an hour, no currents).
- **The earlier observers** (Needham, Buffon, Gleichen, Drummond, Bywater), who saw motion and called it animal. They stay in About.

**What this game does differently.**

- **Position: controller.** The player sets the conditions of one drop (what is in it, how old, burnt or not, how fine, open or sealed, how small, which way round) and aims at a target: make the dust lie still. None of the three had a controller. The paper plainly also offers the experimenter's seat (Hubble's), and the game declines it: the player is not inferring a hidden quantity from noisy readings but intervening on a system to make something happen, and the thing cannot be made to happen. *I burnt hair, I ground the Sphinx, I sealed the drop in oil, and it danced through all of it.*
- **Format: the impossible challenge.** The skill allows it where the paper shows that every attempt must fail, and this paper does: every cause removed, motion undiminished. Benjamin's challenge had an optimum; Hubble's discovery had a law to find; Cantor's contest had a winning move. Here the target cannot be reached, the game says so the moment it is clear, and the discovery is the failing itself, plus the one lever that does change the dance (size), which is not a cause but a clue, and which Brown recorded without explaining.
- **Device: the live drop and the shelf (one of our own).** The drop of water is drawn live and is the only thing on the screen that moves; its dancing is the yardstick, read by eye. Beside it, a shelf of five suspect labels; a suspect the day's experiment has answered gets a paper slip laid over it, SET ASIDE. No halo, no plate, no strike.
- **Chassis: a hand-coloured natural-history plate, 1828, in motion (Austere family: the scientific plate).** Plate cream, ink, Prussian blue, almond gold, carmine, bottle green. IM Fell English. Labels with a double rule. After a poster (Worldly), a night sky (Instrument) and a fairground (Loud): a light, printed surface on which the only motion is the thing the paper is about, drawn live in SVG.

## 7. The aim and the verdict

**Aim**, on the start screen, at the top of the play screen, first line of How to play: *Dust is dancing in a drop of water. Make it lie still.*

**Verdict**, the end screen's first lines: *The dust is still dancing.* Then, in words: what you tried and what it danced through; which of the five suspects you set aside and which you left standing; whether you found the one thing that changes the dance (size) or never looked; the quiet drops you got, if any, were the ones with nothing in them (sulphur, sugar) or no water (the open drop watched to the end). Then what Brown says: that he could not make it stop either, that the smallest specks of any matter, living or never alive, burnt or not, dance in water, and that he could not account for it and did not claim the specks were alive. Then, marked as later and after a rule, the coda.

## 8. Three concepts

**A. The Sceptic's Bench (controller; impossible challenge).** One drop on the stage, a bench of Brown's own experiments, seven days. Controls: one tap to choose the day's experiment, one tap to look. Before pressing, the player is weighing which suspect they believe (life? drying? currents? pull between specks? the drop's skin?) and which experiment would remove it. After, they see the drop redrawn under the new conditions, dancing or not, and a slip laid over any suspect the experiment answered. Discovery moment: the chip of the Sphinx dances (life is gone), then the sealed drop dances for an hour (drying and currents are gone), then one speck alone dances (pull is gone), and then the coarse grind: lumps still, fine dust dancing, the first and only time the dance changed. Fun: anticipation (will the Sphinx dance?), discovery (it does; then the lumps do not), mastery (the second play goes straight to the oil drop, which answers three suspects in a day, and spends the days on size). Replayability: a second play tests a different belief, or hunts the lever instead of the causes; the verdict differs.

**B. Brown's Summer (experimenter; discovery under a budget).** June to August 1827, a cabinet of specimens, a budget of looks; the player chooses what to put under the lens to find out where the motion stops, and at the end states the law (the motion belongs to: pollen / living things / organic matter / everything). The discovery is the widening: plants, dead plants, animals, coal, glass, granite. Deliberation: which specimen is most informative. Fun: anticipation and discovery. Weak point: a sequence of yes, yes, yes; the deliberation is thin once the player sees the pattern, and it repeats Hubble's seat and shape.

**C. The Eye (subject; perception; real time; the reckless one).** A play screen with no words: the eyepiece field, a drop evaporating in real time, currents growing from the centre outward, and the player's one job is to tap the specks that move *of themselves* and not with the stream; then the same drop sealed in oil, where every speck is its own. Brown's 1829 distinction (proper motion against currents) under the thumb. Fun: perception, mastery, a clock. Discovery: in the open drop the task gets impossible as the currents grow; in the sealed drop it is easy, because there is nothing but proper motion. Walkthrough written in section 9 for the record. What it gives up: the causes, the Sphinx, the burnt hair, the whole elimination, which is the paper's spine.

## 10. The pick, and why

**A, The Sceptic's Bench.** The player operates Brown's own levers (specimen, age, fire, fineness, the open drop, the oil, the single speck, the inversion) at the one target the paper is about, and fails at it the way the paper fails, with the paper's own experiments and in roughly the paper's own order if they think as Brown thought. The deliberation is real: which suspect do I hold, and what removes it. The commitment is real: the bench says what you will do, not what you will see. The discovery lands inside the play three times (the Sphinx; the sealed drop; the lumps), not on the end screen. The sentence afterwards has no "guess": *I ground the Sphinx and burnt hair and sealed the drop in oil, and the dust danced through all of it; only the big lumps lay still.* Originality: no game in the table has an impossible target, and none has let the player work through an author's own list of rejected explanations.

**Author test.** Brown would recognise the Clarckia particles and the molecules, the herbarium specimens, the blowpipe, the Sphinx, the meteorites, the almond oil, the drop of 1/2000 of an inch with one particle in it, the inverted experiment, and the fibrils of asbestos at rest beyond four or five molecules. He would enjoy watching a player sure that the Sphinx will not dance; and he would insist, as he did in 1829, that the game never say the specks are alive, which it does not, and that it end where he ended: unable to account for it. The colleague who knows the answer still has to pick an order for seven days, and still cannot stop it.

**Runner-up: B.** Switch if the author wants the 1827 summer alone, the widening from pollen to the Sphinx, without the 1829 causes.

**What the reckless concept would have given.** A wordless real-time field in which the player feels Brown's distinction between proper motion and drift with their thumb. Kept from it: the open drop watched to the end, drawn live, with the drift growing from the centre and overwhelming the dance, so that the distinction is seen if not played. Given up: the clock, the tapping, the silence.

## 11. The shape

Seven days, one experiment a day, from a bench of fourteen; then the verdict; about four to five minutes. Screens: start; the stage (the drop, the shelf, the bench, the same screen redrawn each day); the end; How to play; About. No presets: the bench is the paper's own list of experiments, offered all at once, in no order, so that the order is the player's. Day 0 is the paper's opening observation (fresh Clarckia pollen) shown before the first choice. No score: the yardstick is the drop, read by eye, and the verdict is in words. Replay starts a fresh seven days. The budget of seven is ours (section 15): it makes the order a decision; it does not make the discovery, which holds for any order.

## 12. Controls, mechanism, feedback, moment

- **Controls:** one tap on a bench label (fourteen live options), one tap on LOOK. Nothing carries over between days: the bench clears.
- **Mechanism (toy, from the catalogue):** a drop has contents (material class, age, burnt, grind, specimen), a vessel (open; sealed in oil; sealed with one speck; inverted) and a clock. The contents yield specks or not (nothing from sulphur, resin, wax as in 1827; nothing from sugar, which dissolves). Each speck has a size; its dance has a vivacity that depends on size only, zero above a size limit (long fibrils; lumps), the same for every material, age and treatment. The open drop's currents grow with time from centre to rim and the drop is gone within a minute; the sealed drop has no currents and lasts hours; oil beads on the water's skin are at rest. Each experiment answers a subset of the five suspects, by the paper's own reasoning; the coarse grind and the asbestos reveal the size lever.
- **Feedback:** the drop redrawn live under the new conditions; one line under it, in the aim's units (dancing / lying still / nothing in the drop); slips on the shelf.
- **Moment:** the Sphinx dances; the sealed drop dances for an hour; the lumps lie still and the dust between them does not.

## 14. What is cut

The purpose Brown started with (tracing the pollen particles through the style to the ovulum, pp. 463–466, 475–477) and his disagreement with Brongniart (477–479): About only. The families (Onagrariae, Asclepiadeae, Periploceae, Orchideae, Gramineae, Mosses, Equisetum) beyond "moss" and "a leaf": cut. The change of form of the Clarckia particles (contraction, swelling, turning on the axis): shown as turning only. The "primary combinations" hypothesis Brown advanced in 1827 and withdrew in 1829: About. The uniformity of the molecule's size, which he was unsure of: the game draws them uniform and the About says he was unsure. The priority discussion (Leeuwenhoek, Gray, Needham, Buffon, Gleichen, Drummond, Bywater): About. The list of substances (travertine, stalactites, lava, obsidian, pumice, volcanic ash, manganese, nickel, plumbago, bismuth, antimony, arsenic, the haddock's mucus, the fern's powder): three on the bench (Sphinx, glass, meteorite), the rest named in About. Air bubbles and volatile matter as a suspect: folded into the sealed drop's result.

## 15. Divergences

- **Clock.** An open drop's minute is eight seconds on screen; the sealed drop's hour is eight seconds with a clock face running. Our choice: compression.
- **Days.** Seven, from a bench of fourteen. Ours: it makes the order a decision. The discovery (nothing but size changes the dance) holds for any choice and any order, by construction from the paper's catalogue; the check confirms it over random sequences.
- **Suspects.** Five, from Brown's 1829 list plus the 1827 belief in life: life; currents; drying (evaporation); pull (attraction and repulsion between particles); the skin of the drop (capillary and hygrometric action at the surface). Heat is folded into life ("what fire leaves"); air bubbles into the sealed drop. The mapping of experiments to suspects follows Brown's own sentences and is listed in the config block.
- **Vivacity by size.** Brown gives an ordering (molecules vivid; the larger pollen particles slower, with changes of form; fibrils of two or three molecules vivid, four or five moving, longer at rest; lumps that will not stay suspended, not moving). The game's vivacity curve is a smooth decreasing function through those points with a rest limit; the curve is ours; the ordering and the limit are his.
- **Sulphur and sugar.** In 1827 Brown could get no particles from sulphur, resin or wax, nor from anything soluble in water; in 1829 he reports getting them from sulphur, resin and wax after all. The bench uses the 1827 result (nothing comes off), and the result line and About say what 1829 added.
- **The Sphinx.** Brown ground "a fragment of the Sphinx" as one of the constituent minerals of granite. The game makes it one label; the About says it was a chip of its granite.
- **Nothing is random that matters.** Speck positions and the dance itself are drawn from a seeded generator inside the mechanism; outcomes do not depend on the seed.

## 16. Mechanism kind and claim boundaries

**Kind: toy**, built from the paper's catalogue of observations (section 2). The About screen says: *This game demonstrates* that, under its own assumptions, nothing done to the matter, the drop or the specks' company changes the dance, that the open drop's streams come after the dance and end with the water, and that the one thing that changes the dance is size. *It assumes* the toy: a vivacity that depends on size alone, with Brown's ordering and rest limit and our curve between; currents that grow over the open drop's minute, his words and our curve; a bench of fourteen looks mapped to five suspects by his own sentences; seven days, ours. *The paper establishes* the observations as reported and the argument by elimination; it establishes no cause and claims none.

**The discovery comes from the paper, not from the rules.** The central discovery (every attempt fails; size alone changes the dance) follows from the catalogue, not from the budget: the check B9 runs 300 random seven-day sequences over random seeds and finds the dust dancing in every look with water and specks small enough, and nothing at rest in water outside the two size looks. The budget of seven only decides how many suspects a player happens to set aside; three days suffice for all five (B9c). The heuristics table (section 18) is a property of the bench's mapping, which is Brown's; the mapping is listed in the config block with page references (B8).

**Robustness.** B9d doubles and halves the vivacity exponent and moves the rest limit by a factor of two each way: the ordering (molecule, then the larger pollen particle, then a lump at rest) and the sealed drop's undiminished dance survive all four.

**Later interpretation.** Nothing in the mechanism uses Einstein's or Perrin's theory; the size dependence is coded from Brown's ordering (pp. 466–468, 472), not from the later law. The coda on the verdict screen is the only place the later interpretation appears, after a rule, headed "What came later", and it says which two common claims about Brown are not in his pamphlet.
