What Brown wrote

He could not make it stop either. The smallest specks of any matter he could grind, living or never alive, dried a century or burnt in a flame, danced in water; sealed in oil with nothing to dry and no stream to carry them, and even one speck alone, they danced on. He set aside every cause anyone had offered, and his own first one, that it was the life of the pollen. He did not say the specks were alive, and in 1829 wrote plainly that he never had. He ended with motions, in his words, for which he was unable to account.

What came later

Brown's pamphlet ends without an answer, and this game ends where he did. Seventy-seven years on, in 1905, Albert Einstein worked out what a speck would do if the water around it were made of molecules far too small to see, in ceaseless heat-driven motion, striking it from all sides unevenly from instant to instant: it would wander just like this, and the smaller the speck, the farther it would wander. Jean Perrin measured the wandering in 1908 and found Einstein's numbers. “Brownian motion” became the first direct evidence that atoms and molecules are real.

Brown's “molecule” meant only the smallest speck he could see, roughly a thousand times wider than the water molecule that explains it. Two things often said of him are not in his pamphlet: that he watched pollen grains move (he watched the particles inside the grains, which are far too big to dance), and that he thought the specks alive (he denied it in print; “active” meant moving, nothing more). What is in it: that the smallest specks of anything dance in water, that no cause anyone proposed survived his drop of water in oil, and that the one thing that changed the dance was size, which he recorded and left unexplained.

How to play

Dust is dancing in a drop of water. Make it lie still.

You sit at Robert Brown's microscope in London in 1827. On the stage is a drop of water with dust in it, and the dust will not keep still: it trembles, turns and wanders. The drop is the blue circle; the dust is the specks in it. While there is water and the specks are small, they dance.

The shelf. Under the drop are five labels: Life, Currents, Drying, Pull, The skin. They are the five things people said made the dust move: that it is alive; that streams in the water carry it; that the drop drying up stirs it; that the specks pull and push each other; that something at the surface of the drop acts on it. When a look answers one of them, a red slip lands on its label: set aside.

The bench. Fourteen labels, each one thing Brown actually did: grind a chip of the Sphinx, burn hair in a candle, seal the drop in almond oil, make a drop with one speck in it, and so on. Tap one and a line says what you will do. Tap LOOK and the plate shows the act, then the drop as it is afterwards. Then a line says what you saw, and slips land if the look answered anything.

Seven days. One look a day. Think before you tap: which of the five do you believe, and which look would remove it? Some looks answer more than one; some answer none. After the seventh day comes the verdict: what you tried, what it danced through, what you set aside, and whether you found the one thing that changes the dance.

Play again to spend the seven days differently.
