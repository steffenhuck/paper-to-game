# Leibniz's Worlds — note for the author

1. Look first at `screenshot-a-4-window.png` and `screenshot-a-4-lifted-2.png` (the window and the second cover lifting), then `screenshot-a-2-coral.png` (world 2 with the coral star lit and four shut out), then `screenshot-a-2-best.png` (the best sky beside it, without the 9), `screenshot-a-3-sky.png` (world 3 as weight made it) and `screenshot-a-3-amber-out.png` (the amber star put out: three can be). Then play it on a phone.
2. Everything tunable is in `CONFIG` at the top of `mechanism.js`, the only copy of the mechanism: the three skies (stars, weights, positions, the pairs that cannot both be), the colours, the star radius, the words for how short a world is. Change a sky and run `node calibration.js`: it recomputes the best worlds and tells you whether the lessons still hold. Then `python3 build.py`, which runs the check again and refuses to build on a failure or a mechanism mismatch.
3. What we were least sure about: (a) numbers inside the stars: the deliberation is arithmetic, so they are there, against the skill's usual preference; (b) "what you put out stays out at Let it be" is a design rule, not Leibniz's, and is said in the cap under the button and on How to play; (c) the title is a dummy; (d) About says the translation is quoted "with his permission" on the strength of Strickland's nod — reword if that overstates it; (e) world 1 can be won by doing nothing (the rush-in builds the best world), which is true to the paper and may feel odd; (f) the window world is fixed in order (the full world first, then the short one); "can't tell" is never graded, only reported; (g) with lines and numbers found rather than given, a player who taps nothing in world 1 and lets it be still gets the best world, and learns the three pairs only from the reveal.

This game has had the builder's review only: screenshots and a scripted browser session at 380 px. It has not been played by real strangers, and its length has not been measured.
