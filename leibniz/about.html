Manuscript LH 4, 1, 10 Bl. 2–4, Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz Bibliothek, Hanover; first printed in Erdmann's Opera philosophica (1840), 147–150; Gerhardt, Die philosophischen Schriften VII, 302–308. Read in Lloyd Strickland's translation from the Latin (© 2014, complete, with the manuscript's deletions in the notes), published at leibniz-translations.com, and quoted here with his permission; the game's words for Leibniz's ideas lean on his. No DOI. Game built from this paper; the paper is the authority where the two disagree.

What the paper says

Why is there a world at all, and why this one? Not because of anything in the world: every state is copied from the one before, like a geometry textbook copied from an earlier copy, and however far back you go you never reach a reason why there are books at all. So the reason lies outside the series, in something that exists of necessity. Then the part this game plays: how that reason picks a world. Every possible thing has a claim to exist, a push, in proportion to how much reality there is in it. Not all possibles can exist together. What exists is the combination through which the greatest total of reality comes to be: "there is always in things a principle of determination which must be sought in maximum and minimum". Leibniz says this in the words of a game: "certain games where all the spaces on the board are to be filled according to certain rules, and where, unless you use some skill, you will in the end be excluded from certain spaces and forced to leave more spaces empty than you could have or wished to". He adds the picture of heavy bodies that settle into the arrangement of greatest total descent.

From the maximum follows his answer to the oldest objection, that the world is full of suffering and waste. The best whole is not the sum of the best parts. Cover all but a corner of a beautiful painting and you see "a confused chaos of colours"; dissonances make the music better; bitter things should be mixed in with the sweet. Afflictions are "stepping back in order to make a leap forward with greater force" (he writes the French reculer pour mieux sauter in the margin); when a part of the world is destroyed, "this very destruction and oppression is useful for achieving something greater, so that in a way we profit from our very loss". And since there is always more that is possible than has been made, progress never ends. He also tells the story of an ape that carried the infant King Christian of Denmark to the edge of a roof and, when everyone was frightened, put him back in his cradle laughing: we enjoy the danger for the restoring.

What the game does

It takes Leibniz's rule, not his board. Leibniz's possibles fail to exist together because of what they are, not because they overlap; so there is no board, and nothing is placed. A sky holds the things that could exist; each has a weight, how much there is of it; a line between two means they cannot both be. You decide what exists. On the first sky, more wants to be than can be together, so even the best world leaves something out. On the second, the grandest thing is the trap: it cannot be with four lesser things that together hold more than it does; whoever lets it be first ends with a sky a third short. On the third, the sky comes already filled by weight, heaviest first, and nothing left can be; the only way to a fuller world is to put something out, and when the right thing goes, three lesser things come in where it was, while putting the grandest thing out gains nothing. When you let a world be, whatever still can be comes in on its own, heaviest first: in the paper, "even if nothing further is determined, there exists as much as is possible". What you put out stays out. After each sky the best possible world is shown next to yours, found by an exact search over every set. The verdict is in words.

What kind of mechanism this is

Toy. The game runs a model built to illustrate the paper's argument, and where the argument had to be made exact, the toy chose: a thing's reality is a number (Leibniz: "perfection is nothing other than quantity of essence"); the only incompatibility is a listed pair that cannot both exist; the three skies, their weights and their pairs are ours, chosen so that each sky holds one of the paper's points, and the headless check proves that it does.

  • What the game demonstrates: that under a rule of this kind the fullest set must leave something out, can leave the grandest thing out, and can be reached from a decent set only by removing a thing that was fine. These follow from the three skies as built and from the paper's own criterion: the most reality together.
  • What the game assumes: everything in the paragraph above, and in particular that a thing's claim is proportional to its weight. The headless check weights more than proportionally (weight to a power) and finds that the grandest thing stays out of the best second world until the exponent is about one and three quarters, and that the third sky's step back still pays up to a little over two; so the lessons do not hang on the exact criterion, but they are lessons about these skies.
  • What the paper establishes: an argument. It does not prove that reality has a quantity, or that the actual world is the fullest; it argues that if possibles have claims in proportion to their reality and not all are compatible, then what exists is the maximum, and that a maximum of the whole need not look best in every part. The game shows the second half of that sentence happening in a sky. It is not evidence for the first.

What is cut, and where the game departs

The first argument (the copied books and the necessary being) is three lines on the start screen. The equilateral triangle, the shortest path, the sphere, the laws of motion, the commonwealth of minds and the law of justice are not played; the covered picture is read on the verdict screen. Leibniz's possibles do not get chosen by a hand; the game gives you the hand and then shows the maximum, and lets the striving act without a hand twice: when a world is let be, and in the third sky's filling by weight. The order in which waiting things come in (heaviest first) is ours, from his "in proportion to their reality". "Best possible world", "possible", "step back" are his words; "light", "lit", "dark", "waiting", "Let it be" are the game's.
