Leibniz, 23 November 1697

Leibniz’s Fullest World

Leibniz asked why there is a world at all, and why this one. Looking back in time never answers it: every state of the world is copied from the one before, for ever.

So he looked at what wants to exist. Every possible thing pushes to be, as hard as there is of it. Not all of them can be together.

The world that exists, he says, is the set of things that holds the most.

Tap a thing to let it be.
Tap it again to put it out.
Then take a torch into worlds already made.

Bring as much into existence as will fit.

Built on Lloyd Strickland’s translation of On the ultimate origination of things (2014), leibniz-translations.com. The game is dedicated to him.

How to play

Bring as much into existence as will fit.

Five worlds, drawn fresh every game, in two halves: the skies, where you make the world, and the salt mine, where you stand inside one already made. In the three skies Each ring is a thing that could exist; the bigger the ring, the more there is of it. Some pairs cannot both exist, and nothing tells you which in advance: you find out by letting things be.

Tap a ring to let that thing be. It lights up. Anything it cannot be with goes dark, with a red line to the thing that shut it out; the line stays, so you know that pair from then on. Tap a lit star to put it out again: a step back. Whatever it was holding down can be again.

The bar under the sky shows how much light your world holds. Let it be ends the world: it holds exactly what you lit, nothing more. Then the best possible world appears next to yours, the fullest set of things that can be together, with every line and every number shown.

The third sky arrives already filled, heaviest first, and nothing left can be. Making it fuller means putting something out, then letting in what that frees.

The fourth world is not yours to make. A world already made, in the dark: a small star lit, a big one dark, a line between them, and three more lines leading from the big one into the dark. Whatever is at their ends is what decides: a lit star there holds the big one down; a dark thing there holds nothing. One torch lies in the dark: drag it to where you want it struck, and for a few seconds the stars and lines around it show, faintly, before it dies. It cannot reach the ends from the big star; you have to pick a line. When you are done looking, choose: the other star (the small one goes out, and the big one comes in if nothing else holds it), or leave it. You see what that did, in the dark; then the cover lifts; then you see the twin, another world with exactly the same opening and the other truth. The salt mine has two rounds, each a fresh world with its own torch.

At the end, your score in Leibniz's own measure: the light your five worlds hold, against the light the five best possible worlds hold.
