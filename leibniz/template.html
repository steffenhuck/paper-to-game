Leibniz, 23 November 1697

Leibniz’s Worlds

Leibniz asked why there is a world at all, and why this one. Looking back in time never answers it: every state of the world is copied from the one before, for ever.

So he looked at what wants to exist. Every possible thing pushes to be, as hard as there is of it. Not all of them can be together.

The world that exists, he says, is the set of things that holds the most.

Tap a thing to let it be.
Tap it again to put it out.
Then judge a world through a window.

Bring as much into existence as will fit.

How to play

Bring as much into existence as will fit.

There are four worlds, one after another; in the first three you make the sky. Each ring in a sky is a thing that could exist. The bigger the ring, the more there is of it; the number inside says how much. A line between two rings means they cannot both exist.

Tap a ring to let that thing be. It lights up. Anything it cannot be with goes dark, with a red line to the thing that shut it out. Tap a lit star to put it out again: a step back. Whatever it was holding down can be again.

The bar under the sky shows how much light your world holds. More light is a fuller world.

Let it be ends the world. Whatever can still be comes in on its own, heaviest first; what you put out stays out. Then the best possible world appears next to yours: the fullest set of things that can be together.

The third sky arrives already filled, heaviest first, and nothing left can be. Making it fuller means putting something out.

The fourth world is not yours to make. You see a world already made, through a small window, and say whether it is as full as it can be. Then the cover lifts and you see the whole. Then a second world, through the same window.

At the end: what Leibniz says, what you found, and your three skies beside the best.
