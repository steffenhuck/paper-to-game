Leibniz, 23 November 1697

Leibniz's
Tiles

Leibniz asked why there is a world at all, and why this one. Looking back in time never answers it: every state of the world is copied from the one before, for ever.

So he looked at what wants to exist. Every possible thing pushes to be, as hard as there is of it. Not all of them fit together.

The world that exists, he says, is the arrangement that fits the most. He called it a game of tiles.

How to play

Bring as much into existence as will fit.

There are three boards, one after another. Each board is a piece of land: its dark cells are land with nothing in it. Under the board are shapes, drawn as thin outlines: things that could exist, each wanting to be on the board. The only rule of the world is that two things cannot be in the same place. A shape on the board is lit; a shape off the board is dim.

Your hands

  • Drag a shape from the row below onto the board. While you hold it, the board shows where it would land: its colour where it fits, a red outline where it does not. Let go to put it there.
  • Tap a shape in the row to turn it a quarter. Turn does the same for the shape you last touched.
  • Tap a shape on the board to pull it out. It goes back to the row, dim, and the land under it goes dark. Leibniz's phrase for this is stepping back for a better leap.
  • Let it be ends the board. Whatever still fits comes in on its own, heaviest first (nothing stops it, so it comes to be). Then the game says what your world holds, what was left out, and shows the best possible world: the fullest arrangement there could be.

What to expect

The shapes under each board add up to more than the land can hold, so something is always left out, even in the best possible world. Under the board, a line counts what is lit and what is dark; that is the whole score. The third board starts already filled by weight, with nothing left that fits; there the move is to pull something out.

At the end: what Leibniz says, what you found, where the two part, and his covered picture.

What the paper says

Why is there a world at all, and why this one? Not because of anything in the world: every state is copied from the one before, like a geometry textbook copied from an earlier copy, and however far back you go you never reach a reason why there are books at all. So the reason lies outside the series, in something that exists of necessity. Then the part this game plays: how that reason picks a world. Every possible thing has a claim to exist, a push, in proportion to how much reality there is in it. Not all possibles can exist together. What exists is the combination through which the greatest total of reality comes to be: "there is always in things a principle of determination which must be sought in maximum and minimum". Leibniz says this in the words of a game: "certain games where all the spaces on the board are to be filled according to certain rules, and where, unless you use some skill, you will in the end be excluded from certain spaces and forced to leave more spaces empty than you could have or wished to"; "tiles that are arranged so that as many as possible occupy a given area". He adds the picture of heavy bodies that settle into the arrangement of greatest total descent.

From the maximum follows his answer to the oldest objection, that the world is full of suffering and waste. The best whole is not the sum of the best parts. Cover all but a corner of a beautiful painting and you see "a confused chaos of colours"; dissonances make the music better; bitter things should be mixed in with the sweet. Afflictions are "stepping back in order to make a leap forward with greater force" (he writes the French reculer pour mieux sauter in the margin); when a part of the world is destroyed, "this very destruction and oppression is useful for achieving something greater, so that in a way we profit from our very loss". And since there is always more that is possible than has been made, progress never ends. He also tells the story of an ape that carried the infant King Christian of Denmark to the edge of a roof and, when everyone was frightened, put him back in his cradle laughing: we enjoy the danger for the restoring.

What the game does

It plays Leibniz's own analogy straight. A board is the capacity of the world; the shapes are possible things, each worth the cells it covers; the one rule is that two things cannot be in the same place. You fill three boards. On the first, more wants to exist than fits, so even the best world leaves something out. On the second, the biggest shape is the trap: with it on the board the most that can be lit is 29 of 36; without it the land is full; whoever takes the grandest thing first ends a fifth dark. On the third, the board comes already filled by weight, heaviest first, and nothing left fits; the only way to a fuller world is to pull a good tile out, and when the right one goes, three humbler shapes stand where it was. When you let a world be, whatever still fits comes in on its own, heaviest first: in the paper, "even if nothing further is determined, there exists as much as is possible". After each board the best possible world is shown next to yours, found by an exact search. The verdict is in words; then Leibniz's covered picture, on the best world of your third board.

What kind of mechanism this is

Toy. The game runs a model built to illustrate the paper's argument; the model is Leibniz's own analogy taken literally, and where it had to choose, it chose: a thing's reality is the number of cells it covers (Leibniz: "perfection is nothing other than quantity of essence", and the tiles that "occupy a given area"); the only incompatibility is overlap; the land is a square grid; a shape can be turned but not flipped; and the three boards and their shapes are ours, found by search so that each board holds one of the paper's points.

  • What the game demonstrates: that in a packing of this kind the fullest arrangement can leave the grandest piece out, must leave something out, needs the small awkward pieces, and can be reached from a decent arrangement only by removing a piece that was fine. These follow from the three boards as built, not from the scoring, which is the paper's own criterion: spaces filled.
  • What the game assumes: everything in the paragraph above, and in particular that reality is proportional to size. The headless check weights size more than proportionally (cells to a power) and finds that the big square stays out of the best second world until the exponent is about two and a half, and that the third world's step back still pays at any exponent tried; so the two lessons do not hang on the exact criterion, but they are lessons about these boards.
  • What the paper establishes: an argument. It does not prove that the world is a packing, that reality has a quantity, or that the actual world is the fullest; it argues that if possibles have claims in proportion to their reality and not all are compatible, then what exists is the maximum, and that a maximum of the whole need not look best in every part. The game shows the second half of that sentence happening on a board. It is not evidence for the first.

What is cut, and where the game departs

The first argument (the copied books and the necessary being) is three lines on the start screen. The equilateral triangle, the shortest path, the sphere, the laws of motion, the commonwealth of minds and the law of justice are not played. Leibniz's possibles do not get placed by a hand; the game gives you hands and then shows the maximum, and lets the striving act without a hand twice: when a world is let be, and in the third board's filling by weight. The order in which waiting shapes come in (heaviest first, first place that fits) is ours, from his "in proportion to their reality". "Best possible world", "possible", "step back" are his words; "the land", "lit", "dark", "waiting", "Let it be" are the game's.
