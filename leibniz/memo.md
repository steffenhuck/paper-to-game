# Leibniz's Tiles — design memo

## 0. Given

Mine: Steffen's signature and counter. Author title: *Leibniz's Tiles*. Three ideas given in the first reply: **no theology quiz**, **modern look**, **Strickland credit**. The first rules out the obvious wrong game (ask the player what Leibniz thought about evil) and pushes the design toward the one mechanism the paper hands over as a game. The second fixes the register family: the look is contemporary, not a 1697 manuscript, not a baroque emblem; where this met the precedent rule it did not conflict (none of the last three games is contemporary), so nothing was overridden. The third puts Lloyd Strickland, whose translation the game is built from, on the start screen's footer line and on the About screen, with his website.

## 1. Paper

G. W. Leibniz, *De rerum originatione radicali* (On the ultimate origination of things), dated 23 November 1697. Manuscript LH 4, 1, 10 Bl. 2–4 (Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz Bibliothek, Hanover); first printed in Erdmann's *Opera philosophica* (1840), 147–150; standard reference Gerhardt, *Die philosophischen Schriften* VII, 302–308. Read in Lloyd Strickland's translation from the Latin (© 2014, complete, with the manuscript's deletions in the notes), published on his site leibniz-translations.com. No DOI. Available: the translation as a PDF, six pages; no data, no code, no figures, no numbers. The game is built from the text alone.

## 2. What the paper hands over

An **argument** (primary), in two movements. First: no state of the world explains why there is a world at all, because every state is copied from the one before it, like a geometry textbook copied from an earlier copy for ever; so the reason for the world must lie outside the series, in something necessary. Second, and this is the part the game runs: *how* that reason picks a world. Every possible thing strives to exist, with a force in proportion to how much reality ("essence", "perfection") it contains; not all possibles can exist together; what exists is the combination through which the greatest total of essence comes into being. Leibniz says this in the words of a **game** (secondary, and the gift to this skill): "certain games where all the spaces on the board are to be filled according to certain rules, and where, unless you use some skill, you will in the end be excluded from certain spaces and forced to leave more spaces empty than you could have or wished to"; "tiles that are arranged so that as many as possible occupy a given area". Then the theodicy follows from the maximum: the best whole is not the sum of the best parts; a part looked at alone is "a confused chaos of colours"; dissonances make the music better; afflictions are "stepping back in order to make a leap forward" (he writes the French *reculer pour mieux sauter* in the margin); destruction "is useful for achieving something greater, so that in a way we profit from our very loss"; and progress never ends, because there is always more that is possible than has been made.

So the mechanism is a packing problem with an optional set: choose which possibles exist and where, under a compossibility constraint (they cannot occupy the same place), to maximise total essence. Leibniz supplied the board, the tiles, the rule, the skill, the failure mode (spaces left empty) and the moral. The formalisation is his own analogy taken literally; the toy's choices are listed in section 15.

## 3. The discovery, and its kind

**A mechanism made intelligible**, with a **counterintuitive corner** inside it. The mechanism: "best of all possible worlds" is not a mood, it is an *argmax*: the world is the fullest arrangement of things that can exist together. The counterintuitive corner, which is where Leibniz's theodicy lives and where a stranger will trip: the fullest world need not contain the grandest single thing (the most perfect possible can be exactly what leaves the most empty), it does contain small and awkward things (the "bitter" ones), and the way from a decent world to the best one can run through destroying a part that was fine. Three things to find out, one per world; the paper names all three.

## 4. The core narrative

There is a board, and there are shapes that want to be on it. Each shape pushes to get on with a force as big as itself. Not all of them fit together, so some have to stay off. The world is whatever arrangement gets the most onto the board. If you grab the biggest shape first, you end up with holes you can't fill and a world with less in it. If you look at one corner of the best board on its own, it looks like a mess; step back and it is the fullest board there could be. And sometimes the only way to get more on is to take something good off first.

## 5. Who lives in the paper

- **The possibles** (the shapes). Each "strives to exist in proportion to its quantity of essence". They choose nothing; they push. As a player: no decision. As the material: everything.
- **The dominant unity**, the extramundane reason, i.e. God, who "not only rules the world, but also constructs or makes it", and does so "according to the principle of wisdom or perfection"; "the wiser someone is, the more he is determined to what is most perfect". Controls which possibles exist and where. The natural player: the one whose skill decides how many spaces stay empty. The Given forbids a theology quiz, not this; the game never asks the player to be God in anything but the one respect the paper makes mechanical: packing. On screen the player is "you", at the board.
- **The capacity of the world**, time and place, "the land on which a building is to be constructed": the board. A candidate control (choose the land) rejected in section 8.
- **The heavy bodies**: Leibniz's physical analogy, weights that settle into the arrangement of greatest total descent. A candidate for a real-time concept (section 8).
- **The prisoner in the salt mines of Sarmatia**, who sees only his torchlight and judges the world by it; the viewer of the covered painting. The epistemic position: the one who sees a part. Not a player (any decision would be a guess about the paper's answer), but the verdict's last picture.
- **The afflicted good man**, whose loss is a step back for a leap. In the game: whoever pulls a tile off the board.

## 7. The aim and the verdict

**Aim** (start screen, top of every play screen, first line of How to play): *Bring as much into existence as will fit.*

The yardstick in the aim's units: the board itself, lit cells against dark, with one line under it: "Lit 29 of 36. Dark 7. Waiting 3." After "Let it be", the same line says what the world holds and what the best possible world holds.

**Verdict** (the end screen leads with these, in words):

- *What Leibniz says:* the world that exists is the arrangement of possible things that fits the most together, like tiles filling a board. Not everything possible can exist; the grandest thing need not be in the best world; the small, awkward things are; and a step back can be the way to a fuller world.
- *What you found:* one line per world, from what the player actually did. World 1: whether their board was full and what they left out. World 2: whether the big square is in their world and how much stayed dark because of it, or that they left it out and filled the land. World 3: whether they stepped back, what they pulled out, and whether the world got fuller for it.
- *Where you part:* the worlds where yours holds less than the best, and by about how much (a fifth, a few cells, nothing).
- *Had yours stood:* "a world with less in it than there could have been. Leibniz says that cannot be the one that exists: the possibles push, and the fullest arrangement is what comes through, the way weights settle." (Where the player matched the best on every world: "yours is the one Leibniz says exists.")

Then the covered picture, then the three boards small beside the best three.

## 8. Three concepts

**A. The Packer** (controller; challenge). The player is at the board with a tray of possibles, each a flat coloured shape whose size is its essence. Drag a shape onto the board; it lights up. Turn it. Pull it off again (Leibniz's step back). Not everything fits; "Let it be" ends the world, whatever still fits pushes in by itself, and the best possible world is shown next to yours. Three worlds, each with one thing to find out (section 11). Thinking before pressing: which shape, where, and what that leaves room for. Seeing after: the cell count and the dark cells; at the end, the best board beside yours. Discovery moment: the best board for world 2 appears without the big square in it. Fun: agency, mastery, discovery. Second playthrough: a different attack on world 2 (leave the square out from the start) and on world 3 (pull a different tile); the worlds are fixed, the best boards are fixed, and the player now knows the rule, which is the paper's point (a determined wisdom).

**B. The Weights** (agent; real time; the reckless one). The possibles are heavy bodies, as in Leibniz's own analogy: the player tilts the phone (or drags a vessel) and shapes with weight equal to their essence tumble and settle under gravity; whatever arrangement has the greatest total descent is what exists; the player's control is the order and direction of the pour. No words on the play screen. Thinking before: what order to pour. Seeing after: the pile, how high the surface is, what spilled over the rim (the excluded). Discovery moment: pouring the heavy one first leaves a worse pile than pouring the small ones round it. Fun: anticipation, discovery. Replay: other orders. Walkthrough (section 9) shows the weakness: after the first pour the player has nothing to think about except "try another order", and a physics pile does not make the paper's point about *compossibility*, which is a hard constraint, not a soft settling; the player would also learn about friction, which Leibniz did not write about.

**C. The Land** (controller, dual). Leibniz's "principle of determination in maximum and minimum: the greatest effect with the least expenditure", with the board as the expenditure. The possibles are given; the player chooses the smallest piece of land (a 5×5, a 4×7, a 6×5, a 6×6) that will hold them all, then packs it. Thinking before: how tight dare I go. Seeing after: whether it all fitted; land wasted or possibles left out. Discovery: the compact land holds the most, as liquids make a sphere. Fun: anticipation, mastery. Walkthrough shows the trouble: the choice of land is one guess before the real play, the score would need a price of land against a price of essence that the paper does not give, and the compact-land lesson is one sentence in the paper, not its spine.

Also written out and rejected: **the covered picture as a play loop** (experimenter with a torch over a hidden board, judge whether the world is well made): a quiz about the paper's conclusion, and the theology quiz the Given forbids; kept as the verdict's last picture, which is where Leibniz put it. **The copied books** (subject: tap back through the series of states looking for the reason): a demonstration with one tap; kept as three lines on the start screen.

## 9. The first five minutes

**A, The Packer.** 0:00 A dark screen, a big title, three lines: Leibniz asked why there is this world rather than none and rather than another; going back in time never answers it, every state is copied from the one before; so he looked at what wants to exist. Three acts: drag a shape onto the board, turn it, pull one out again. The aim. Begin. 0:20 "World 1 of 3. Fill the land." A 4×4 board, dark cells, a tray of six dim outlined shapes, a status line: Lit 0 of 16, Dark 16, Waiting 6. A line: "Every shape wants to be on the board. Not all of them will fit." They drag the big rectangle; it snaps to the grid, its footprint shows before they let go; it lights. They want the square next, then the L, which they turn with the Turn button (or by tapping it). They fill 15 and have a 1-wide gap and a 1×3 left; frown; they pull the square out (tap it; it dims back to the tray: "a step back") and try again. 1:10 Full, or one dark. "Let it be." The small single cell, still waiting, slides in on its own if it fits: "Nothing stopped the small one, so it came to be." The game's line: "Your world is full. Left out: the bar of three. The best possible world is also full, and also leaves one thing out: not everything that could exist can exist together." Grin: they beat it. 1:30 World 2, a 6×6, seven shapes: a big square, three long bars, three small squares. They take the big square first; of course they do; it is the most of anything. Then the bars; two fit, the third does not; the small squares go in; 29 lit, 7 dark, a bar waiting. They stare. Pull the square? That is the thing they are weighing; it is the most valuable piece. Some do, and find the three bars and three squares fill the land exactly. Some let it be. 3:00 "Let it be": "Your world holds 29 of 36; a fifth of it is dark. The best possible world is full, and the big square is not in it." Beside their board, the best one lights up, bar by bar. Frown, then the grin of seeing it. 3:20 World 3: a board already 31 lit, filled "by weight, heaviest first, wherever it fit", three shapes waiting, none fits anywhere. "Make this world fuller." Nothing to drag. They tap a lit tile: it comes out, "a step back"; the hole is bigger; now things fit, or do not. Which tile? The one next to the dark cells looks guilty; the big square looks guilty from last time. Pull the wrong one and the world gets to 32; put it back, pull another. 4:30 Pull the long bar in the middle and three humbler shapes take its place: 36. "Let it be." "Your world is full. You destroyed one thing and three came to be." 5:00 The verdict, the covered picture, the three pairs of boards.

**B, The Weights.** 0:00 A vessel, shapes above it, tilt to pour. 0:15 They pour; the shapes tumble and settle; a line at the rim shows the surface. 0:30 They want to pour again in another order; they do; the pile is different; higher or lower. 1:00 They wonder what they are supposed to be doing; the game has to say "the highest pile is the world", and the stranger learns that heavy things first leaves gaps, which is one lesson, in a loop of "try another order". There is no second thing to find out and no step back; the compossibility rule is soft. A demonstration with a tilt sensor.

**The three thoughts** for A: first action, "the big rectangle goes in the corner, obviously"; middle action (world 2), "do I keep the square, which is worth the most, or pull it and risk ending with less?"; last action (world 3), "which of these fine tiles do I destroy, and will three others really fit where it was?". Three different thoughts.

## 10. The pick, and why

**A, The Packer.** It is Leibniz's own analogy, played straight, and the three things he says about the maximum each become a thing a player does: leave something out, leave the grandest thing out, destroy a part. The author test: anyone who has taught "best of all possible worlds" has said the word *argmax* and never watched a stranger reach for the big square and lose a fifth of the world by it; and the step back (*reculer pour mieux sauter*, in the manuscript's own margin) as a game action is the paper's second half made into a decision rather than a consolation. The sentence afterwards: *I packed the board, and the fullest board left the biggest piece out and needed me to tear one up.* No "guess". Originality: no game in the table has had the player pack; the nearest, *Watson's Cardboard*, fits two pieces at a time by chemistry, not a board by area.

**Runner-up: C, The Land.** Switch if the author wants the minimum half of Leibniz's "maximum and minimum" played rather than read; it would then be a fourth world (choose the land, then pack it), and the About screen would own the price of land.

**The reckless concept, B,** would have given a play screen with no words on it and the heavy-bodies analogy as a feeling in the hand. Given up: the tilt, the tumble, the spill. Kept from it: the order in which leftover shapes push in at "Let it be" (heaviest first, "in proportion to their reality"), and the pre-filled world 3, which is what a world made by weight alone looks like.

## 11. The shape

One game, three worlds, then the verdict. Each world is one board, one tray, one "Let it be"; each has one thing to find out, and nothing is repeated:

1. **World 1, 4×4, six shapes (a 2×3, a 2×2, an L of three, a bar of three, a bar of two, a single), total 19 cells for 16.** Learns the hands: drag, turn, pull out, let it be. Finds out: not everything possible can exist together; even the best world leaves something out. The single that pushes in by itself at "Let it be" shows the striving.
2. **World 2, 6×6, seven shapes (a 3×3 square, three 2×4 bars, three 2×2 squares), total 45 cells for 36.** Finds out: the grandest possible is not in the best world. With the big square on the board the most that can be lit is 29; without it the three bars and three small squares fill the land exactly. The biggest-first player ends at 29, a fifth dark.
3. **World 3, 6×6, pre-filled by weight (a 2×5 bar, a 3×3 square, a 2×4 bar and a 1×4 bar placed heaviest first, first fit), 31 lit; waiting: a 2×3, a 1×4, an L of three, none of which fits anywhere.** Finds out: the way to a fuller world runs through destroying a part. Pull the 2×4 bar out and the three waiting shapes fill the 13 cells that opens: 36, with the bar left out. Pull the 3×3 (the lesson of world 2 misapplied) and the most is 32; pull the 2×5 and nothing improves.

No presets beyond the three worlds; they are fixed, not drawn, because the best possible world is fixed too, which is the paper's claim. Order: controls first, the trap second, the destruction third; the discovery arrives in world 2 within two minutes. Score: none during play but the lit count; per-world lines in words; the verdict in words with the three boards beside the best three. Length estimate: four to six minutes.

## 12. Controls, mechanism, feedback, moment

- **Controls.** Drag a shape from the tray onto the board (while dragging, its footprint shows on the grid in its own colour where it fits and as a red outline where it does not; letting go places it or returns it). Tap a shape in the tray to turn it a quarter; *Turn* does the same for the shape last touched. Tap a lit tile on the board to pull it out (it returns to the tray, dim). *Let it be* ends the world: whatever still fits pushes in, heaviest first, and the best possible world is shown.
- **Mechanism** (`mechanism.js`): `newGame` (the three worlds from CONFIG), `place`, `pull`, `turn`, `letItBe` (the rush-in, then the world's result), `solve` (exact search for the maximum total essence over subsets and placements, rotations only; the best boards are computed by it and stored in CONFIG after the calibration check confirms them), `verdict`. Essence of a possible is its cell count. Compossibility is non-overlap on the grid.
- **Feedback.** The fill: dark cells light as tiles land; the status line in the aim's units; after *Let it be*, the rush-in and then the best board beside yours.
- **Moment.** World 2's best board lighting up without the square; world 3's three small shapes filling the hole a good tile left.

## 13. The look

**Register: a 2020s puzzle app at night** (Contemporary, a family added to the list for this game; by the Given). From the paper's subject: Leibniz describes a tile game, and the tile game a stranger already has on their phone is the register. Dark mode, so the one visual rule can work.

- **Palette:** *night* `#0E1016` (the page), *slab* `#171A23` (the board's frame and panels), *void* `#07080C` (an empty cell: the darkest thing on screen), *chalk* `#F3F4F8` (words, the committing button), *mute* `#8F95A8` (small words); and five tile colours, one per possible, *coral* `#FF5C5C`, *amber* `#FFB830`, *mint* `#2FE3A3`, *azure* `#4F9DFF`, *lilac* `#B48CFF`. - **Type:** Space Grotesk (Florian Karsten; SIL OFL), one variable file: 700 for the title, the world number and the lit count; 500 for buttons; 400 for text. Embedded as data.

## 15. Divergences

- **Essence is area.** Leibniz: "perfection is nothing other than quantity of essence". The toy counts cells. A possible is worth the space it fills, no more; the About screen says this is the toy's one big assumption, and the calibration check shows the world-2 discovery survives weighting size more than proportionally (a square worth more than nine bars' cells) up to a point the memo records.
- **Compossibility is non-overlap.** Leibniz's rules of the board are "certain rules"; the toy's one rule is that two things cannot be in the same place. Nothing else excludes anything.
- **The board is finite and square.** "The capacity of the world" is a 4×4 and a 6×6. Time is not drawn.
- **Rotation, no reflection.** A shape can be turned, not flipped; the solver has the same freedom as the player.
- **The player places by hand.** In the paper nothing places; the maximum "arises". The game gives the stranger the hands and shows the maximum after them; the rush-in at *Let it be* and the pre-filled world 3 are where the striving acts without a hand.
- **The rush-in order** (heaviest first, first fit) is ours, from Leibniz's "in proportion to their reality".
- **Three fixed worlds** rather than the infinite combinations of possibles; the best boards are exact maxima of those three, computed by search.
- **Words.** "Possible", "exist", "world", "the best possible world", "step back" are Leibniz's; "the land", "lit", "dark", "waiting", "Let it be" are the game's, each defined where it first appears.

## 16. Mechanism kind and claim boundaries

**Kind: toy.** The game runs a model built to illustrate the paper's argument, and the model is the paper's own analogy (the game of filling a board, the tiles on a given area) taken literally. Where the analogy had to be made exact, the toy chose, and the About screen lists the choices: a possible's reality is the number of cells it covers; the only incompatibility is overlap; the land is a square grid; a shape turns but does not flip; the three worlds and their shapes are ours, found by search so that each holds one of the paper's points.

- **Demonstrates:** that in a packing of this kind the fullest arrangement can leave the grandest piece out, must leave something out, needs the small awkward pieces, and can be reached from a decent arrangement only by removing a piece that was fine. These follow from the three boards as built, under the paper's own criterion (spaces filled), not from any scoring of ours; the game has no score beyond cells lit.
- **Assumes:** the toy's choices above, chiefly that reality is proportional to size.
- **The paper establishes:** an argument, not a measurement. It argues that if possibles have claims in proportion to their reality and not all are compatible, then what exists is the maximum, and that a maximum of the whole need not look best in every part. The game shows the second half happening on a board and is no evidence for the first. On screen the writing says "Leibniz says" and "in this game"; never "if the argument is right".

**Where the discoveries come from** (build.md §1). All three are properties of the constructed boards, proved by the solver in the calibration check, and the memo says so: the paper's claim is that these things *can* happen under the maximum, and the boards are instances. **Robustness:** the toy's one big assumption is essence = cells. Weighting a shape's reality by cells to a power k, the big square stays out of the best second world until k reaches about 2.55 (so for any criterion up to "a thing is worth its size squared and a half", the lesson holds); the third world's step back (a fuller world than the one made by weight exists) survives every k tried up to 4. Neither lesson hangs on the exact criterion; both are about these boards, which the About screen says.

## 17. Calibration plan and result

There are no reported numbers; the plan was to prove the memo's claims about the boards with an exact solver, and to prove the solver. `calibration.js`, 46 checks, all passing (`calibration-result.txt`): the solver equals brute force on 60 random small instances; each shape has the right number of distinct quarter turns; each world's tray exceeds its board (19 for 16, 45 for 36, 44 for 36) and its best possible world lights 16, 36, 36, with a legal stored placement; world 1's best world is full and leaves two possibles out; world 2 with the big square on the board lights at most 29 over all sixteen positions, without it the land is full, heaviest-first lights 29, and 7 of 36 reads as "about a fifth dark"; world 3 is filled by weight to 31 with three shapes waiting, none of which fits anywhere, and pulling the long bar allows 36, the big square 32, the longest bar or the thin bar 31; the best third world leaves out exactly the long bar; the striving at *Let it be* brings in the single that still fits and names it, leaves the bar of three out, refuses moves after the end, rushes nothing in an untouched world 3, and comes heaviest first; the robustness sweeps of section 16; the heuristics table of section 18; three full games through the API (heaviest first everywhere, the best play everywhere, mixed) produce a verdict in words with no decimals, and the right lines for the player who matches every world and the player who keeps the square.

## 18. Playing it in numbers (build.md §3)

From the calibration run, cells lit:

| | heaviest first, first fit | lightest first, random spots (300 runs) | random order, random spots (300 runs) | best |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| World 1 (16) | 16 | mean 9.9 (9–13) | mean 12.7 (9–16; full in 35 of 300) | 16 |
| World 2 (36) | 29 | mean 22.7 (12–36) | mean 25.5 (12–36; full in 6 of 300) | 36 |

World 3 (best 36): never step back 31; pull the longest bar then play best 31; pull the big square then play best 32; pull the thin bar then play best 31; pull the long bar then play best 36; pull the long bar then random spots mean 30.9 (26–36); pull the long bar and let the rush alone do it 31.

Read as the stranger: in world 2 the obvious way of playing (the biggest first) goes wrong for the reason the paper names, and random play does not stumble into the full world by accident; in world 3 the obvious way (do nothing, or pull the piece the last world taught you to distrust) goes wrong, and one considered pull is enough. World 1 is practice: the heaviest-first player fills it, which is as it should be, and the thing to find out there is what is left out, not how much. No option is dead: every shape is in some best world except the ones the boards are built to exclude, and those are the lessons.
