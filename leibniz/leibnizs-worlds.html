Leibniz, 23 November 1697

Leibniz’s Worlds

Leibniz asked why there is a world at all, and why this one. Looking back in time never answers it: every state of the world is copied from the one before, for ever.

So he looked at what wants to exist. Every possible thing pushes to be, as hard as there is of it. Not all of them can be together.

The world that exists, he says, is the set of things that holds the most.

Tap a thing to let it be.
Tap it again to put it out.
Then judge a world through a window.

Bring as much into existence as will fit.

How to play

Bring as much into existence as will fit.

There are four worlds, one after another; in the first three you make the sky. Each ring in a sky is a thing that could exist. The bigger the ring, the more there is of it; its number shows once it has been lit. Some pairs cannot both exist, and nothing tells you which in advance: you find out by letting things be.

Tap a ring to let that thing be. It lights up. Anything it cannot be with goes dark, with a red line to the thing that shut it out; the line stays, so you know that pair from then on. Tap a lit star to put it out again: a step back. Whatever it was holding down can be again.

The bar under the sky shows how much light your world holds. More light is a fuller world.

Let it be ends the world. Whatever can still be comes in on its own, heaviest first; what you put out stays out. Then the best possible world appears next to yours: the fullest set of things that can be together.

The third sky arrives already filled, heaviest first, and nothing left can be. Making it fuller means putting something out.

The fourth world is not yours to make. You see a world already made, through a small window, and say whether it is as full as it can be, short, or whether you cannot tell. The window widens and you say again. Then the cover lifts and you see the whole. Then a second world, through the same two windows.

At the end: what Leibniz says, what you found, and your three skies beside the best.

What the paper says

Why is there a world at all, and why this one? Not because of anything in the world: every state is copied from the one before, like a geometry textbook copied from an earlier copy, and however far back you go you never reach a reason why there are books at all. So the reason lies outside the series, in something that exists of necessity. Then the part this game plays: how that reason picks a world. Every possible thing has a claim to exist, a push, in proportion to how much reality there is in it. Not all possibles can exist together. What exists is the combination through which the greatest total of reality comes to be: "there is always in things a principle of determination which must be sought in maximum and minimum". Leibniz says this in the words of a game: "certain games where all the spaces on the board are to be filled according to certain rules, and where, unless you use some skill, you will in the end be excluded from certain spaces and forced to leave more spaces empty than you could have or wished to". He adds the picture of heavy bodies that settle into the arrangement of greatest total descent.

From the maximum follows his answer to the oldest objection, that the world is full of suffering and waste. The best whole is not the sum of the best parts. Cover all but a corner of a beautiful painting and you see "a confused chaos of colours"; dissonances make the music better; bitter things should be mixed in with the sweet. Afflictions are "stepping back in order to make a leap forward with greater force" (he writes the French reculer pour mieux sauter in the margin); when a part of the world is destroyed, "this very destruction and oppression is useful for achieving something greater, so that in a way we profit from our very loss". And since there is always more that is possible than has been made, progress never ends. He also tells the story of an ape that carried the infant King Christian of Denmark to the edge of a roof and, when everyone was frightened, put him back in his cradle laughing: we enjoy the danger for the restoring.

What the game does

It takes Leibniz's rule, not his board. Leibniz's possibles fail to exist together because of what they are, not because they overlap; so there is no board, and nothing is placed. A sky holds the things that could exist; each has a weight, how much there is of it, shown by its size; some pairs cannot both be. Nothing tells you which pairs, or the exact weights, in advance: a line appears once one thing has shut another out, and a number once a thing has been lit. You decide what exists, and you find out what that costs by doing it. On the first sky, more wants to be than can be together, so even the best world leaves three things dark. On the second, the grandest thing is the trap: it cannot be with four lesser things that together hold more than it does; whoever lets it be first ends with a sky a third short. On the third, the sky comes already filled by weight, heaviest first, and nothing left can be; the only way to a fuller world is to put something out, and when the right thing goes, three lesser things come in where it was, while putting the grandest thing out gains nothing. When you let a world be, whatever still can be comes in on its own, heaviest first: in the paper, "even if nothing further is determined, there exists as much as is possible". What you put out stays out. After each sky the best possible world is shown next to yours, found by an exact search over every set. The fourth world is Leibniz's covered picture played: two worlds already made, seen through the same small window, in which the same lit 2 holds down the same dark 9. You judge each from the window (full, short, or cannot tell), the window widens and you judge again, then the cover lifts: one is as full as it can be, the other seven short, and the small window could not have told you which. The verdict is in words.

What kind of mechanism this is

Toy. The game runs a model built to illustrate the paper's argument, and where the argument had to be made exact, the toy chose: a thing's reality is a number (Leibniz: "perfection is nothing other than quantity of essence"); the only incompatibility is a listed pair that cannot both exist; the three skies, their weights and their pairs are ours, chosen so that each world holds one of the paper's points, and the headless check proves that it does.

  • What the game demonstrates: that under a rule of this kind the fullest set must leave something out, can leave the grandest thing out, and can be reached from a decent set only by removing a thing that was fine; and that a part of a world can look the same whether the whole is the fullest or not. These follow from the four worlds as built and from the paper's own criterion: the most reality together.
  • What the game assumes: everything in the paragraph above, and in particular that a thing's claim is proportional to its weight. The headless check weights more than proportionally (weight to a power) and finds that the grandest thing stays out of the best second world until the exponent is about one and three quarters, and that the third sky's step back still pays up to a little over two; so the lessons do not hang on the exact criterion, but they are lessons about these skies.
  • What the paper establishes: an argument. It does not prove that reality has a quantity, or that the actual world is the fullest; it argues that if possibles have claims in proportion to their reality and not all are compatible, then what exists is the maximum, and that a maximum of the whole need not look best in every part. The game shows the second half of that sentence happening in a sky. It is not evidence for the first.

What is cut, and where the game departs

The first argument (the copied books and the necessary being) is three lines on the start screen. The equilateral triangle, the shortest path, the sphere, the laws of motion, the commonwealth of minds and the law of justice are not played; the covered picture is the fourth world. Leibniz's possibles do not get chosen by a hand; the game gives you the hand and then shows the maximum, and lets the striving act without a hand twice: when a world is let be, and in the third sky's filling by weight. The order in which waiting things come in (heaviest first) is ours, from his "in proportion to their reality". "Best possible world", "possible", "step back" are his words; "light", "lit", "dark", "waiting", "Let it be" are the game's.
